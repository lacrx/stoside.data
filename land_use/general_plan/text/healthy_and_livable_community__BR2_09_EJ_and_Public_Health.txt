9.1	 Environmental 
Justice
ENVIRONMENTAL JUSTICE AND EQUITY
The City of Oceanside’s current General Plan 
describes the City as a place where everyone 
can live a long, healthy, and opportunity-filled 
life. However, not all people have the opportu­
nity to live their healthiest lives because of envi­
ronmental inequities, which means that certain 
geographies and groups of people unfairly and 
disproportionately experience higher exposure 
to pollution, greater negative health impacts, 
and less access to health-supporting features, 
such as green space and parks, safe and afford­
able places to live, quality jobs and schools, and 
stores that meet basic needs. 
Environmental justice is a movement to take 
concerted action to remedy environmental con­
ditions that result in economic and health dispar­
ities. Ultimately, environmental justice and social 
equity mean the fair treatment and meaningful 
involvement of all groups so that everyone has 
the chance to live in a healthy environment. 
DISADVANTAGED COMMUNITIES
Under California law (SB 1000), cities and other 
local jurisdictions must address environmental 
justice by developing policies for issues that 
affect disadvantaged communities, or commu­
nities that experience high levels of pollution, 
socioeconomic stress, and health impacts. SB 
1000 identifies the California Communities 
Environmental Health Screening Tool 3.0 
(CalEnviroScreen) as the primary method 
for identifying disadvantaged communities. 
Developed by the California Environmental 
Protection Agency (CalEPA), CalEnviroScreen 
uses environmental, health, and socioeconomic 
information to produce scores for every census 
tract in the state, thereby identifying commu­
nities that are most vulnerable to pollution’s 
effects. The score assigned to each census tract 
is a product of exposure to pollution, adverse 
environmental effects caused by pollution, pres­
ence of sensitive populations (i.e., those with 
asthma or cardiovascular disease), and socioeco­
nomic factors that increase vulnerability (i.e., low 
household income, low educational attainment, 
and unemployment). A disadvantaged commu­
nity is defined as one which scores in the top 
25th percentile of CalEnviroScreen’s cumulative 
score.

Overall CalEnviroScreen scores are calculated 
from scores of two groups of indicators, pol­
lution burden, and population characteristics 
scores. Pollution burden represents the poten­
tial exposures to pollutants, such as traffic, 
drinking water contaminants, toxic releases, and 
other sources, and the adverse environmental 
conditions caused by pollution, such as ground­
water threats, cleanup sites, solid waste sites, 
or impaired water bodies. Population character­
istics represent biological traits, health status, 
or community characteristics that can result 
in increased vulnerability to pollution, such as 
health conditions or socioeconomic factors like 
poverty or unemployment. 
Using these criteria, there are no census tracts 
within the Planning Area designated as disad­
vantaged communities. However, some tracts 
bear a disproportionate environmental burden 
compared to other census tracts in the Planning 
Area.  Some tracts in the Planning Area have 
individual pollution burden or population char­
acteristics scores above 70 . In general, tracts 
with higher burdens are located in the west of 
the city, due in part to higher relative pollution 
burdens along the I-5 corridor. Census tracts 
with cumulative CalEnviroScreen scores over 65 
percent, including Census Tracts 82 and 85.09, 
located in the south of Townsite and Loma Alta 
neighborhoods, are shown in Table 9-1 and in 
Figure 9-1.

9.2	Public Health   
One of the goals of the environmental justice 
movement is to improve conditions that affect 
people’s health. Determinants of health include 
social factors and physical conditions of the 
place in which people are born, live, learn, work, 
play, and age. Social determinants include pov­
erty, employment, educational attainment, and 
housing, while physical determinants include 
environmental quality, public transit accessibil­
ity, and walking distance to services. Both social 
and physical factors influence health.
HEALTH OUTCOMES
Evidence increasingly shows how interactions 
between the social and physical environments 
contribute significantly to an array of health 
risks and outcomes. While San Diego County has 
consistently ranked among the top 15 healthiest 
counties in California since 2016, according to 
California Health Interview Survey data, the City 
of Oceanside has higher rates than the county 
for adults ever diagnosed with diabetes (9.1 
percent versus 8.2 percent), low-income adults 
experiencing food insecurity (8.3 versus 6.1 per­
cent), adult obesity (26.1 versus 25 percent), and 
elevated weight and obesity for ages 12-17 (46.4 
versus 30.4 percent).1     
At the census tract level, health disparities 
become even more stark, with several census 
tracts in the Planning Area facing worse health 
outcomes than others. Figures 9-2 through 9-4 
show how census tracts within the Planning 
Area rank among all California counties for rates 
of asthma, high blood pressure, and low birth 
weight.

Figure 9-2 shows how rates of asthma within 
the Planning Area compare to the rest of tracts 
in California. Census tracts in and around 
downtown Oceanside--as well as tract 194.04, 
located along Oceanside Boulevard in southeast 
Oceanside--display elevated rates of asthma 
compared to the rest of the state; these neigh­
borhoods have asthma rates higher than 50 to 
73 percent of California census tracts. These 
census tracts also overlap with areas of the City 
that have higher rates of poverty and cardio­
vascular disease, as shown in Figures 9-3 and 
9-5. The causes of asthma are unknown, but 
both genetic and environmental factors can be 
involved, including poor air quality. Air quality in 
the downtown and Oceanside Boulevard areas 
may be affected by vehicular traffic along major 
corridors, which is likely to reduce over time 
as emissions standards become increasingly 
stringent. Pollution in the area has also likely 
decreased in recent years due to the decommis­
sioning and planned demolition of the nearby 
Encina Power Station, located just south of the 
area in Carlsbad.  Additionally, by facilitating 
alternative modes of transportation, the City can 
reduce vehicle miles traveled (VMT) and associ­
ated emissions. 
As seen In Figure 9-3, patterns of cardiovascular 
disease within Oceanside are similar to those 
for asthma, with ddowntown Oceanside expe­
riencing rates of cardiovascular disease that are 
higher than 50 to 74 percent of census tracts 
in California  . There are many risk factors for 
developing cardiovascular disease including age, 
diet, lack of exercise, smoking and exposure to 
air pollution. Exposure to outdoor air pollution 
following a heart attack has been shown to 
increase the risk of death. In addition to people 
with a past heart attack, the effects of air pol­
lution may also be greater in the elderly and 
people with other preexisting health conditions.
Figure 9-4 shows that two census tracts - Tract 
185.17, located along Vista Way in south central 
Oceanside, and Tract 186.11, corresponding 
closely to the boundaries of the South Morro 
Hills area - have incidences of low birth weight 
that place them in the top quartile for the state, 
meaning that low birth weight rates in these 
tracts rank higher than 75 percent of all census 
tracts in California. Poor nutrition, lack of prena­
tal care, stress, and smoking by the mother are 
known to increase the risk of having a low-birth-
weight baby. Studies suggest that pollution could 
also be a factor.2

SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF PUBLIC 
HEALTH
Poverty, Unemployment, and 
Educational Attainment
Social determinants can increase stress levels 
and/or make healthy living difficult, which can in 
turn cause populations to be more sensitive to 
the effects of pollution. For example, residents 
of high-poverty neighborhoods may be more 
likely to be exposed to toxic materials in homes, 
dangerous streets, and pervasive advertising 
promoting harmful substances than residents 
of higher-income neighborhoods.3 High-poverty 
neighborhoods also may have limited options 
for healthy food and safe outdoor physical activ­
ity, and fewer opportunities for education and 
high-quality employment than higher-income 
neighborhoods.4  These circumstances can 
result in a multi-generational cycle of poor nutri­
tion, poor health, and limited social, educational, 
and economic opportunities.
In 2018, approximately 29.4 percent of the City 
of Oceanside’s population was living 200 percent 
below the federal poverty level, on par with the 
county’s current rate of 29.3 percent. Though 
CalEnviroScreen uses now outdated ACS data 
from 2011-2015 for its 2018 assessments, two of 
Oceanside’s downtown census tracts, 182 and 
185.09, had poverty rates of 58 and 57 percent, 
respectively. These tracts, shown in Figure 9-5, 
ranked in the top quartile for poverty rates, or 
higher than 75 percent of the census tracts in 
California.

Stress from long-term unemployment can lead 
to chronic illnesses, such as heart disease, which 
can shorten a person’s life.5 In 2018, the unem­
ployment rate in the City of Oceanside was 6.2 
percent, approximately equivalent to the county 
average of 6.4 percent. As seen In Figure 9-6, 
Tracts 185.10 and 185.16, both located near cen­

top quartile of CalEnviroScreen-ranked tracts, 
meaning their unemployment rates were higher 
than 75 percent of the census tracts in California.
Low educational attainment also impacts 
health. Educational attainment correlates most 
strongly with life expectancy and most health 
status measures.6 Additionally, several studies 
have associated educational attainment with 
susceptibility to the health impacts of envi­
ronmental pollutants.7 According to 2018 ACS 
data, within the Planning Area, approximately 
13.3 percent of Oceanside residents have no 
high school diploma, higher than the county 
average of 12.9 percent. In Figure 9-7, Based on

had levels of educational attainment that are 
among the lowest 25 percent in California. These 
tracts are scattered across the City. While edu­
cational attainment is correlated with multiple 
factors, research indicates parental education 
and income is strongly associated with children’s 
educational achievement.8

Housing
Health and Safety
Access to safe and adequate affordable housing 
is a critical component of environmental jus­
tice. According to American Community Survey 
Data, 1% of homeowners and 6% of renters in 
Oceanside face two or more physical or finan­
cial issues. These issues can include a lack of 
complete plumbing facilities, a lack of complete 
kitchen facilities, more than one occupant per 
room, and housing costs as a percentage of 
household income greater than 30 percent. The 
data is used to identify those homes in which the 
quality of living and housing can be considered 
substandard.9  
Studies have indicated that renters have higher 
rates of material hardship, which might force 
acceptance of inadequate housing conditions.10,11 
Figure 9-8 shows the percentage of renters 
facing two or more issues by census tract across 
the City. In the northern portion of the Guajome 
neighborhood and the majority of the Lake 
neighborhood, 20-30 percent of renters face 
two or more issues, the highest percent in the 
City and much higher than the citywide average. 
Portions of East Side Capistrano, Airport, and 
east Guajome as well as Morro Hills and Loma 
Alta also face higher than average rates of sub­
standard housing.

Affordability
Generally, a household is considered “rent-bur­
dened” if 30 percent or more of the gross house­
hold income is spent on rent, which reduces 
the availability of funds for schooling, childcare, 
groceries, transportation, and other household 
costs.12 According to 2018 ACS data, approxi­
mately 57.3 percent of renting households in 
Oceanside pay more than 30 percent of their 
household income towards rent, as shown in 
Figure 9-9. 
While the majority of the Oceanside’s residents 
are homeowners (56 percent), many of these 
homeowners pay a significant portion of their 
income towards housing costs. Median home 
values have increased by about 7 percent over 
the past 17 years, from $429,700 in 2010 to 
$460,100 in 2018.13 Thirty four percent of house­
holds with a mortgage pay more than 30 percent 
of their household income towards housing 
costs, which includes all mortgage principal 
payments, interest payments, real estate taxes, 
property insurance, homeowner fees, condo or 
coop fees and utilities (not including telephone 
or cable television).14

Community Engagement 
Residents’ ability to engage in community life 
contributes to feelings of social belonging and 
good emotional and physical health. Community 
engagement programs and activities also pro­
motes environmental justice by providing resi­
dents with regular opportunities to share their 
environmental and health concerns and make 
their voice heard in the policymaking process. 
Two barriers that can inhibit sustained and 
meaningful community participation include 
linguistic isolation and lack of internet access.
Linguistic Isolation
Many households where little or no English is 
spoken experience linguistic isolation. Adults 
who are unable to communicate comfortably 
and effectively in English often have trouble 
talking to medical professionals and providers 
of social services, and consequently may not 
get the medical care or social support they and 
their children need. Additionally, members of lin­
guistically isolated households may not hear or 
understand important information when there is 
an emergency or an important community issue 
is under consideration.

Internet access facilitates access to online com­

ture, but most often are related to the financial 
burden of subscribing to an internet service and 
purchasing the necessary hardware.

Pollution Burdens in Oceanside
Environmental pollution can take a variety of 
forms, including pollution of the water, air, and 
soil. All forms can have detrimental impacts on 
human health. The City of Oceanside enjoys 
relatively good air quality, benefiting from an 
oceanfront setting and a relative lack of legacy 
heavy industrial, manufacturing, and refinery 
uses. However, there are a few environmental 
concerns within the City, including pesticides, 
automobile emissions, and water quality. 
Exhaust from cars and trucks is the major source 
of air pollution in much of California. Exhaust 
fumes contain toxic chemicals that can damage 
DNA, cause cancer, make breathing difficult, 
and cause low birth weight and premature 
births. Children who live or go to schools near 
busy roads have higher rates of asthma and 
other lung diseases than children in areas fur­
ther from roads. Major roads can also generate 
noise in nearby neighborhoods. According to 
CalEnviroScreen, tracts ranking in the highest 
quartile in the concentration of listed contam­
inants (?) are those nearest to Oceanside’s 
western border and the major transportation 
corridors of I-5 and Highway 78. These tracts are 
shown in Figure 9-11.
Most drinking water in California meets require­
ments for health and safety. However, water 
can become contaminated with chemicals or 
bacteria. Both natural sources and human activ­
ities can contaminate drinking water, and both 
sources can introduce bacteria, viruses and para­
sites. Natural sources can include rocks, soil, and 
fire.   Human sources include industrial opera­
tions, water conveyance systems, automobiles, 
sewage, and runoff from farms and urban areas 
. Tracts in the top quartile are shown in Figure 
9-12. Drinking water in these census tracts  have 
especially high levels of 1,2,3-TrichloroProprane 
(TCP), a solvent, cleaning and degreasing agent, 
and paint and varnish remover which can cause 
eye and throat Irritation, affect muscle coordina­
tion and concentration, harm kidney function, 
and cause cancer. 
Pesticides are chemicals used to control insects, 
weeds, and plant disease. Over 1,000 pesticides 
are registered for use in California. They can 
be applied to fields by air, farm machinery, or 
workers on the ground. Exposure to pesticides 
can cause short term health impacts such as 
eye and respiratory irritation, as well as long 
term impacts such as birth defects and cancer. 
Farmworker families and those who live near 
fields are the most likely to be exposed to high 
levels of pesticides. Based on CalEnviroScreen 
data, a number of census tracts in Oceanside are 
subject to some of the highest levels of pesticide 
exposure among CalEnviroScreen-ranked tracts 
in California, with some tracts in the top quartile 
(ranging from the 79th to 99th percentile). As 
shown in Figure 9-13, most of these tracts are 
within or in close proximity to South Morro Hills, 
Oceanside’s agricultural district.

Walkability and Access to Transit
The City of Oceanside has a Walk Score of 36 
(on a scale of 1 to 100), meaning that it is some­
what walkable but that most errands require a 
car.15 Amenities such as parks, restaurants, and 
grocery stores are within walking distance of 
many residents, but not in all areas of the City. 
As seen in the Figure 9-15, commercial ameni­
ties are grouped in downtown Oceanside, along 
segments of SR 76, and in the Tri-City area. 
While commercial amenities along segments of 
Oceanside Boulevard are in close proximity to 
residential areas, steep topography and other 
physical barriers make it challenging for resi­
dents to walk or bicycle to these venues.  
Convenient access to public transit also helps 
community members increase physical activity 
and decrease vehicle miles traveled. Almost one-
third of Americans who commute to work via 
public transit meet their daily requirements for 
physical activity (30 or more minutes a day) by 
walking as a part of their daily life, including to 
and from transit stops.16 While walkability and 
access to transit stations is one part of transit 
access, it is also important that people can access 
their destinations quickly and conveniently. 
15  Walk Score measures the walkability of a location by 
analyzing walking routes to nearby amenities. Points are 
awarded based on the distance to amenities in each category. 
Walk Score also measures pedestrian friendli-ness by analyzing 
population density and road metrics such as block length and 
intersection density.	
 
16  L. Besser and A. Dannenberg, “Walking to Public Transit: Steps 
to Help Meet Physical Activity Recommenda-tions”. Vol. 32, Issue 
4, American Journal of Preventive Medicine, at 273-280, 2005.
Transfers, extra stops, and distance from desti­
nation can all contribute to inconvenience along 
a transit route. The City should prioritize more 
direct routes between disadvantaged and tran­
sit-dependent communities and colleges, medi­
cal centers and employment centers. 
As seen in Table 9-2, very few residents within 
the Planning Area currently utilize public transit 
or engage in active transportation. Almost 88 
percent of residents in the Planning Area com­
mute to work via car, truck, or van. Of those 
who drive to work, 90 percent drive alone, while 
10 percent carpool. Less than two percent of 
residents use active transportation (walking or 
biking) to get to work, and only three percent use 
public transportation. At the county level, 3.6 
percent of the population uses active transporta­
tion and three percent use public transit to com­
mute to work. Compared to San Diego County, 
the City of Oceanside has slightly lower rates of 
walking for recreation and fitness (?) (39.5 versus 
41.2 percent).  
Dispersed land use patterns and the separation 
of residential and non-residential land uses 
contribute significantly to the lack of walkability 
in Oceanside, particularly outside of the City’s 
major commercial corridors.  Some of the City’s 
most isolated neighborhoods include South 
Morro Hills, Ocean Hills, Peacock, Guajome, and 
North Valley, all of which lie in eastern portions 
of the City and feature mostly single-family 
housing.

Access to Parks, Recreation, Healthy 
Foods, and Health Services
Safe and convenient access to parks and recre­
ation is an important community health indi­
cator. Regular physical activity has many phys­
ical health benefits, including a reduced risk of 
coronary heart disease, obesity, hypertension, 
stroke, some cancers, and other conditions that 
contribute to premature mortality.17 Regular 
physical activity also has many attendant emo­
tional health benefits, such as reduced depres­
sion, anxiety, stress, as well as improved mood 
and the ability to perform tasks and effectively 
navigate challenging life events.18 The National 
Parks Service and the Centers for Disease 
Control and Prevention use a half-mile radius 
to evaluate access to parks and other commu­
nity amenities, while the 2019 Oceanside Parks 
and Recreation Master Plan evaluates access 
at a one-quarter mile radius.19 According to 
the Parks and Recreation Master Plan, approx­
imately 56 percent of Oceanside residents live 
within one-quarter mile   of a park or community 
space, compared to 62 percent of residents of 
San Diego County. Moreover, as noted earlier, 
topography and other physical barriers (e.g., 
waterways, railroads) often complicate access to 
such amenities, even for those who live nearby. 
For more information on park access, please see 
Chapter 6: Parks and Open Space in this ECR.

public transit, a healthy community also has con­

foods. When health care facilities are accessible 
via public transit, medical care is more readily 
accessible to those who do not drive or own 
cars. There are multiple medical facilities located 
within the Planning Area, ranging from large hos­
pitals and medical complexes operated at the

Access to healthy foods is also important to a 
community’s health. The presence of a super­
market in a neighborhood is linked to higher 
fruit and vegetable consumption, as well as a 
reduced incidence of obesity.20 As shown in

Oceanside, the major commercial corridors of

9.3	Key Planning Issues 
and Objectives
•	
Creating environments that support 
better health outcomes.  While San Diego 
County is one of the healthiest counties in 
California, Oceanside residents experience 
higher rates of diabetes, food insecurity, 
and adult and child obesity. Within the 
City, populations that live in certain census 
tracts, especially those close to downtown 
Oceanside, have rates of asthma and high 
blood pressure that are higher than 60 per­
cent of census tracts in California. While 
health outcomes are the result of a number 
of factors, including genetics and behav­
ior, the General Plan Update will shape the 
environmental conditions that may impact 
health.
•	
Addressing communities with higher 
socioeconomic vulnerability. Some com­
munities in Oceanside, including tracts in 
central Oceanside, have poverty and unem­
ployment rates that rank in the highest quar­
tile in all of California. Others have some of 
the lowest rates of educational attainment 
in California. These factors make it difficult 
to live a healthy and opportunity-filled life. 
In wake of COVID-19, economic development 
programs, job training, and public services 
will be essential in supporting families and 
individuals with less resources, particularly 
residents in these geographic areas.
•	
Conducting targeted outreach and pro­
viding additional options for community 
engagement with linguistically, socially, 
or technologically isolated groups. Areas 
where people are not civically engaged and 
more linguistically isolated are also typically 
those areas where there is a lack of internet 
access   and worse health outcomes.  The 
City should explore other means of out­
reach to these communities in policymaking, 
information distribution, and engagement, 
including by mail, through existing com­
munity groups and events, and in multiple 
languages.  The City can also consider ways 
to reduce the “digital divide” faced by many 
lower-income and cost-burdened house­
holds such as implementing community 
broadband, providing financial assistance for 
data and hardware, and improving housing 
affordability. For example, a current goal of 
the Library department is to provide inno­
vative ways for customers to obtain library 
services and materials. This can Include a 
variety of technology solutions for informa­
tion delivery, and efforts to find out what 
tools community members have to take 
advantage of, and how to address the lack 
of such tools, as well as outreach to make 
the community aware of what the Library 
can provide. Outreach plans include devel­
opment of a translation team to assist with 
bridging linguistic gaps.
•	
Addressing Oceanside renters’ high 
housing cost burden. Nearly 60 percent of 
Oceanside renters are rent cost burdened. 
Median home prices have increased since 
2010, and nearly 40 percent of households 
with a mortgage pay more than 30 percent 
of their monthly income on housing costs. 
Affordable and quality housing are essen­
tial components of healthy neighborhoods. 
Beyond the social, economic, and physical 
impacts on families and individuals, the con­
sequences of the lack of affordable housing 
affect everyone. For example, if teachers, 
hotel and other service workers, agricultural 
workers, nurses, and other low- to middle-in­
come workers in Oceanside must live outside 
the Planning Area, this can negatively affect 
Oceanside’s educational system, the local 
economy, access to health services, and envi­
ronmental quality. Thus, affordable rental 
and owner-occupied housing for all types of 
lower-income families and individuals should 
be of utmost importance in the General Plan 
update.

Understanding pesticide use and mitigat­

Oceanside residents are subject to elevated

can cause short term health impacts such 
as eye and respiratory Irritation, as well as 
long term Impacts such as birth defects 
and cancer. Farmworker families and those 
who live near fields are the most likely to be 
exposed to high levels of pesticides.

Oceanside’s car dependence and asso­
ciated health issues. While downtown 
Oceanside is fairly walkable, the City as a 
whole is largely car-oriented has a low walk­
ability score.  Fewer Oceanside adults walk 
regularly than San Diego County residents 
as a whole. Relatively limited opportunities 
for walking may contribute to Oceanside’s 
elevated rates of childhood and adult obe­
sity. Currently, most of the Planning Area 
is not highly walkable in areas outside of 
downtown. As new, denser development 
continues to be built and the Planning Area’s 
population grows, improving walkability 
and pedestrian environments (including tree 
canopy and landscaping), as well as transit 
access and service levels is key, as is ensur­
ing that improvements are made across both 
new developments and existing communi­
ties with less access to resources.  
•	
Improving access to grocery stores. While 
Oceanside is well-served by several large 
chain supermarkets and smaller grocery 
stores that supply fresh fruits and vegeta­
bles, most of these are only accessible by car. 
While the economic feasibility of a greater 
density of grocery stores is subject to market 
realities, such as pressure from delivery ser­
vices and other trends, policies that support 
smaller grocery stores, neighborhood mar­
kets, local food stands, and other neighbor­
hood-serving options could increase walk­
ability and healthy food accessibility for an 
increased number of Oceanside residents. 
Increasing the number of households in an 
area, particularly within the corridors, could 
also help to provide a larger market needed 
for grocery store viability. The combination 
of improved food access and more walkable, 
complete neighborhoods can help to create 
conditions that support healthy living and 
reduced rates of obesity.  
•	
Improving access to educational and 
recreational facilities, public gathering 
spaces, and scenic resources. Some of 
Oceanside’s best resources for educational 
facilities and gathering spaces are the City’s 
libraries. One of the Library’s strategic goals 
is to connect customers to a wide variety 
of programs, services and activities spon­
sored by both the Library and by other 
community agencies and organizations. In 
addition to improving programmatic access 
to resources, the City can focus on improv­
ing connections to resources throughout 
the City. This can mean both ensuring that 
there are enough facilities to serve all areas 
of the City, and improving pedestrian, bicy­
cle and transit connections to the existing 
facilities across the City. As described in 
the Mobility Report, improving bicycle and 
pedestrian comfort and connections are key 
to improving access to the City’s facilities and 
resources.
