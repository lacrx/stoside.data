OCEANSIDE GENERAL PLAN
1-4
Situated at the northwest corner of San Diego 
County, along the Pacific Ocean, the City of 
Oceanside is a unique coastal community 
endowed with a rich history, a beautiful natural 
setting, a diverse population, a host of waterfront 
amenities, an array of distinct neighborhoods, and 
a growing and increasingly diverse local economy. 
Bounded by Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton, 
the cities of Vista and Carlsbad, and the unincor-
porated communities of Fallbrook and Bonsall, 
the City’s 42 square miles of land area include 
character-defining built environments, farmland, 
natural landforms, watercourses, riparian areas, 
and upland habitats. 
With a population of more than 175,000 residents, 
Oceanside is the third most populous city in the 
San Diego region. Housing growth has significant-
ly outpaced employment growth in Oceanside, 
resulting in a comparatively low jobs-to-housing 
ratio. However, over the past two decades, a 
large employment hub comprised of several light 
industrial parks has emerged in central portions 
of the City, providing high-quality jobs in a range 
of economic sectors. At the same time, the City 
has significantly expanded its hospitality sector, 
becoming a popular visitor destination.
After decades of suburban expansion into inland 
areas – i.e., the San Luis Rey Valley, the Oceanside 
Boulevard corridor, and the Highway 78 corridor – 
Oceanside must now provide for growth through 
more efficient use of land within the City’s already 
urbanized areas. In so doing, the City can revital-
ize its major commercial corridors, expand mobil-
ity options, and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) 
emissions while preserving natural resources, 
farmland, and the City’s exceptional vistas of sur-
rounding open spaces.
For the first time in its 135-year history, the City of 
Oceanside has prepared a comprehensive update 
of its General Plan, addressing all state-mandat-
ed topic areas as well as other matters of interest 
to community members. The General Plan (Plan) 
outlines the City’s vision for its future – one that 
reflects the values and priorities of community 
members and provides a blueprint for positive 
change. In keeping with this vision, the Plan pro-
motes responsible growth that supports quality of 
life, environmental quality, economic prosperity, 
and social equity. The Plan identifies what makes 
Oceanside a unique community – in terms of its 
assets, opportunities, and challenges – and out-
lines goals, policies, and actions that seek to pre-
serve and enhance the City’s positive attributes 
while attenuating conditions that work against 
fulfillment of the City’s vision. 
This introductory chapter outlines the Vision and 
Guiding Principles that have helped to shape the 
Plan. It also introduces the Plan’s purpose, use, 
and structure, and how it can be amended and 
updated to adapt to changing circumstances. The

1.1	 Oceanside Setting 
and Regional 
Context
An important gateway into the San Diego met-
ropolitan area, the City of Oceanside links the 
North County Coastal subregion with the inland 
cities of Vista, San Marcos, and Escondido, as well 
as portions of unincorporated San Diego County. 
While Oceanside’s physical characteristics bear 
many similarities to the neighboring coastal 
cities of Carlsbad, Encinitas, Solana Beach, and 
Del Mar, the City’s socioeconomic profile more 
closely resembles those of its inland neighbors. 
Consequently, Oceanside is viewed by many as 
the region’s most accessible, welcoming, and 
down-to-earth coastal community.
As shown in Figure 6-1, Interstate 5 connects 
Oceanside with other California coastal cities to 
the north and south.  Highways 76 and 78 tra-
verse the City in a generally east-west direction, 
linking the coast to the Interstate 15 corridor. The 
historic Coast Highway parallels Interstate 5 and 
links the City to the neighboring City of Carlsbad 
to the south. 
Oceanside offers more than three miles of beach-
es, the only pier and harbor in the North County 
coastal area, major recreational amenities such 
as Guajome Regional Park, Mission San Luis Rey 
(the largest mission in California), the California 
Surf Museum, the Oceanside Museum of Art, El 
Corazon Park, events such as surfing competitions 
and youth soccer tournaments, and markets and 
festivals. Oceanside is somewhat unique among 
southern California coastal cities in maintaining 
nearly 3,500 acres of farmland, located in the 
City’s northeast corner in the South Morro Hills 
neighborhood. 
Bounded by the Pacific Ocean to the west, Marine 
Corps Base Camp Pendleton to the north, and 
unincorporated rural areas to the east, Oceanside’s 
peripheral location within the San Diego region 
has historically presented challenges to economic 
development. However, in recent years the City 
has seen employment gains in hospitality, sports 
equipment and apparel, bio-technology, and other 
sectors. 
Oceanside is an important gateway to the San 
Diego region, as well as a major transit hub. The 
Oceanside Transit Center accommodates four rail 
services that connect the City to Orange County, 
Downtown San Diego, and other cities within the 
Highway 78 Corridor. Rail service is supplement-
ed by bus service operated by the North County 
Transit District (NCTD).
The City’s coastal assets, cultural venues, restau-
rant and micro-brewing scene, and linkages to 
other parts of southern California make it an 
increasingly popular visitor destination.

1.2	Planning Area
The General Plan Planning Area (Planning Area), 
as shown in Figure 1-2, encompasses the area 
addressed by the General Plan Update. The 
Planning Area includes Oceanside and its Sphere 
of Influence (SOI) – an area outside of the City 
limits designated by the San Diego Local Agency 
Formation Commission (LAFCO) as the City’s 
probable future boundary and service area. The 
Planning Area encompasses 27,012 acres (or 
approximately 42 square miles). The City’s SOI 
is relatively small—about 20 acres—and presents 
few opportunities for additional development. 
The City’s population as July 1, 2022 was estimat-
ed at 172,199 by the US Census, a slight decline 
from 174,068 in 2020, paralleling trends across 
the state. 
The Planning Area is bordered by the Marine 
Corps Base, Camp Pendleton to the north, the 
unincorporated rural communities of Fallbrook 
and Bonsall to the northeast, the City of Vista 
to the southeast, and the City of Carlsbad to the 
south. The Planning Area’s southwestern border 
includes three miles of Pacific Ocean coastline. 
The lower San Luis Rey River, Loma Alta Creek, 
and Buena Vista Creek flow in a westerly direc-
tion through the Planning Area and drain into the 
Pacific Ocean. These waterbodies flow through 
alluvial areas bounded by hillsides and mesas, 
essentially dividing Oceanside into three distinct 
east-west corridors.
A portion of the Planning Area is within the state’s 
Coastal Zone. Land uses in this area are governed 
by the Local Coastal Program (LCP) and Land Use 
Plan, which is certified by the California Coastal 
Commission. The LCP effectively supplants the 
General Plan as the governing land use policy 
document for the City’s Coastal Zone. As shown 
in Figure 1-2, the City’s coastal zone lies primar-
ily between the coastline and the inland side of 
Coast Highway, with inland extensions along the 
City’s east-west waterways.

1.3	Purpose and Scope 
of the General Plan
California Government Code Section 65300 
requires each city and county in California to 
adopt a General Plan “for the physical develop-
ment of the county or city, and any land outside its 
boundaries which…bears relation to its planning.” 
The Oceanside General Plan establishes the City’s 
overall approach to growth and change within its 
boundaries to the year 2050, containing both a 
statement of the community’s vision of its long-
term development as well as the goals, policies, 
and actions to support that vision and guide the 
physical growth of the City. The Plan contains pol-
icies to inform decision-making related to devel-

GENERAL PLAN REQUIREMENTS
California grants local authorities power over land 
use decisions. As a result, cities have considerable 
flexibility in preparing their general plans as long 
as state requirements are met. The California 
Government Code establishes both the content 
of general plans and rules for their adoption and 
subsequent amendment. Together, state law and 
judicial decisions establish three overall guide-
lines for general plans:
•	 Comprehensive. This requirement has two 
aspects. First, the general plan must be 
geographically comprehensive. That is, it must 
apply throughout the entire incorporated area 
as well as other areas the City deems relevant 
to its planning. Second, the general plan must 
address the full range of issues that affect the 
City’s physical development.
•	 Internally Consistent. This requirement states 
that the general plan must fully integrate its 
separate parts and relate them to each other 
without conflict. “Horizontal” consistency 
applies as much to figures and diagrams as to 
the general plan text. It also applies to data and 
analysis as well as policies. All adopted portions 
of the general plan, whether required by state 
law or not, have equal legal weight. None may 
supersede another, so the general plan must 
avoid conflicts among the provisions of each 
element.
•	 Long-Range. Because anticipated development 
will affect the City and the people who live 
or work there for years to come, State law 
requires every general plan to take a long-term 
perspective. This General Plan uses the year 
2050 as its planning horizon.
The state mandates that all general plans address 
at least seven topics: land use, circulation, hous-
ing, conservation, open space, noise, and safety. 
Recently, the state has established a requirement 
that, for cities and counties with state-defined 
“disadvantaged 
communities,” 
environmental 
justice should be addressed in the general plan, 
either in a standalone element or integrated with 
other elements. The Environmental Protection 
Agency (EPA) defines environmental justice as 
“the fair treatment and meaningful involvement 
of all people regardless of race, color, nation-
al origin, or income, with respect to the devel-
opment, implementation, and enforcement of 
environmental laws, regulations, and policies.” 
While there are no state-defined disadvantaged 
communities in Oceanside, the City recognizes 
environmental justice as an important concern 
and has thus chosen to address it throughout the 
General Plan. The City understands that even 
though there are no local communities that meet 
the state definition of “disadvantaged,” there are 
historically under-resourced areas in Oceanside 
where residents continue to face economic inse-
curity, linguistic isolation, exposure to pollution, 
and other challenges.
Beyond the state-mandated general plan topics, 
local governments may address optional topics 
in their general plans. Examples of topics local 
governments choose to address include air quality, 
public health, sustainability, community design, 
community facilities, economic development, 
energy, and agriculture, and recreational facilities. 
All general plan topics, regardless of whether they 
are mandatory or optional, must be consistent with 
one another. No element is legally subordinate to 
another.1

economy, foster safe and vibrant public spaces, pro-

and provide high-quality community facilities and 
services. The City will balance economic, environ-
mental, and social priorities in a manner that pro-

the City’s future will be shared by all members of the 
Oceanside community.
Housing and employment growth will be concen-

ticular focus on creating vibrant, walkable, visually 
appealing, and engaging mixed-use districts centered 
along Coast Highway, within the major east-west 
commercial corridors, around Sprinter stations, and 
in the Downtown District. This focus on infill and 
redevelopment will allow for the preservation and 
enhancement of recreational open space, sensitive

infrastructure

safe and comfortable spaces for residents to shop,

low-density residential areas to higher-intensity 
mixed-use corridors, improving multimodal con-
nectivity while preserving each neighborhood’s

Economic Development Element 
(EDE)
The EDE was adopted in 2019 and informs future 
actions affecting the City’s fiscal resources and the 
local economy. The overarching goal of the EDE is 
to grow and diversify the City’s economy in ways 
that lift the fortunes and enhance the wellbeing of 
all Oceanside residents. Goals and policies in the 
EDE will enhance quality of life; promote econom-
ic prosperity; make Oceanside more economically 
resilient; and strengthen collaborative partner-
ships between the City, educational institutions, 
business groups, and neighboring cities.
Energy and Climate Action Element 
(ECAE)
Prepared in conjunction with the EDE and also 
adopted in 2019, the ECAE focuses on the City’s 
responsibility to reduce its carbon footprint in a 
manner consistent with the state’s greenhouse 
gas (GHG) emissions reduction targets. The ECAE 
also addresses environmental sustainability more 
broadly, promulgating goals and policies that 
encourage cleaner, healthier, and less resource-in-
tensive consumer choices and business practices. 
The ECAE encourages smart growth, alternative 
transportation, solid waste diversion, water con-
servation and local water harvesting, urban for-
estry, and the preservation of local agriculture. 
The fundamental goal of the ECAE is to reduce 
reliance on fossil fuels and move the City toward 
a carbon-neutral future.
SMART AND SUSTAINABLE 
CORRIDORS SPECIFIC PLAN
Concurrent with the General Plan Update, 
the City prepared the Smart and Sustainable 
Corridors Specific Plan (SSCSP), a focused reg-
ulatory plan covering the three main east-west 
corridors—Mission Avenue, Oceanside Boulevard, 
and Vista Way. The SSCSP establishes the zoning 
for these corridors, with form-based design stan-
dards that promote high-quality development. 
The SSCSP envisions the three corridors as 
walkable, visually cohesive, tree-lined boulevards 
that foster a strong sense of place and community 
identity. Mixed-use development in these corri-
dors will accommodate much of the City’s housing 
and employment growth over the planning hori-
zon, while creating synergies between residential 
and non-residential uses and providing for more 
natural surveillance. The SSCSP is covered by the 
same EIR as the General Plan and was adopted 
concurrently.
CLIMATE ACTION PLAN (CAP)
The CAP has been updated concurrently with 
the General Plan to ensure consistency with the 
updated growth scenarios presented in the Plan. 
The CAP forecasts future GHG emissions from 
a range of sources, establishes emissions reduc-
tion targets consistent with California’s goal of 
achieving carbon neutrality by 2045, and outlines 
a broad range of strategies designed to reduce the 
City’s carbon footprint.
ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACT 
REPORT
The General Plan is accompanied by an EIR, 
prepared in accordance with the California 
Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). The EIR also 
serves as the EIR on the SSCSP and the CAP. The 
EIR is a detailed analysis of the potential environ-
mental effects of the General Plan/SSCSP/CAP, 
evaluating alternatives and presenting ways to 
reduce or avoid environmental damage. The EIR 
process has also informed policies that mitigate 
potentially adverse environmental effects of the 
Plan, thus resulting in a largely “self-mitigating” 
Plan.

Local Coastal Program
Oceanside’s Local Coastal Program (LCP) is a plan-
ning document that regulates development in the 
City’s Coastal Zone and establishes a long-range 
vision for the community. The California Coastal 
Act, passed in 1976, provides for coastal jurisdic-
tions to adopt a Local Coastal Program to ensure 
local implementation of Coastal Act priorities. 
The City is currently updating its Local Coastal 
Program, originally adopted in 1986, to address 
the changing issues that the City is facing in its 
Coastal Zone, including the threat of sea level rise, 
increasing visitor traffic, more stringent water 
quality and habitat management requirements, 
and aging infrastructure.
Coast Highway Vision and Strategic 
Plan
In 2009, the City of Oceanside adopted the 
Coast Highway Vision and Strategic Plan. This 
plan serves as a blueprint for the revitalization 
and enhancement of the Coast Highway corridor 
between Harbor Drive in the north and Buena 
Vista Lagoon in the south. The Vision and Strategic 
Plan includes several recommendations to trans-
form Coast Highway from an auto-oriented thor-
oughfare into a “complete street” that serves all 
modes of transportation from automobiles to 
pedestrians to bicyclists and transit vehicles.
Downtown – Article 12 Amendment
In 2020 the City Council adopted an amend-
ment to Article 12 in the City’s Zoning Ordinance 
that regulates development in the Downtown. 
The updated Downtown Zoning Ordinance not 
only allows for more commercial uses, which 
are currently allowed in other parts of the City, 
within the downtown area, but also implements 
a streamlined review of some commercial uses 
in the downtown area through an administrative 
conditional use permit (ACUP) process or by per-
mitting the use by right. This update to Article 12 
is the latest move in the City’s effort to revitalize 
the Downtown and encourage new development 
in the area.
Parks and Recreation Master Plan
In 2019, the City adopted a new Parks and 
Recreation Master Plan (Master Plan). The Master 
Plan provides a guide for the orderly development 
of future park, recreation, and open space facili-
ties and programs in order to meet the communi-
ty’s current and future needs. The Master Plan is 
intended to provide a realistic view of the City’s 
parks and recreation system and to develop a 
vision for the parks and recreation system through 
2030. The General Plan builds on the work done 
in the Parks Master Plan to present a comprehen-
sive view of City’s parks and recreation resources 
through the 2050 planning horizon. The General 
Plan growth projections and locations for new 
residential development would need to be reflect-
ed in the next update of the Master Plan.

AMENDMENTS TO THE GENERAL 
PLAN 
The General Plan is a living document. As such, 
it should be updated periodically as specific cir-
cumstances change from the time of writing, to 
respond to new state or federal laws, or to modify 
policies that may become obsolete or unrealistic 
over time. 
Changes in policy as well as the development of 
unforeseen opportunities or needs will require 
amendment of the General Plan. Per California 
Government Code Section 65358, no mandato-
ry element of this General Plan may be amended 
more frequently than four times during any cal-
endar year. Within this limitation, amendments 
may be made at any time as determined by the 
Oceanside City Council, and each amendment 
may include more than one change to the Plan.
