EXISTING CONDITIONS REPORT
2.1	Existing Land Use
Once confined largely to the coastal area between 
the San Luis Rey River and Buena Vista Lagoon, 
the City of Oceanside now extends as much as ten 
miles eastward along three key transportation 
corridors: Highway 76/Mission Avenue, Oceanside 
Boulevard, and Highway 78. Today, more than 75 
percent of the City’s developed land lies east of 
Interstate 5. The total acreage of the Planning 
Area is 27,012 acres. Twenty acres of land lie out-
side City limits but within the Sphere of Influence 
(SOI) to the southeast, south of Sunset Drive. 
Figure 2-1 provides a map of existing land use. 
To the west of Interstate 5, an approximately 
half-mile-wide and three-mile-long stretch of 
Oceanside paralleling the Pacific Ocean features 
a traditional development pattern, with short 
blocks, alleyways, and a diverse mix of uses, 
including a concentration of commercial uses 
along South Coast Highway and a wide range 
of coastal amenities and other public uses. 
Comprised of the Townsite and South Oceanside 
Neighborhood Planning Areas, the coastal area 
was the first part of Oceanside to be settled 
and developed. To the east of Interstate 5, the 
City exhibits a much more suburban character, 
featuring large single-family subdivisions, strip 
commercial development, and large industrial 
parks.  Watersheds, highways, and sloping ter-
rain contribute to a pronounced separation of 
land uses in the City’s inland areas. 
The vast majority of Oceanside’s 42 square miles 
lies east of Interstate 5.  These inland areas gen-
erally exhibit a dispersed land use pattern, dom-
inated by relatively low-density residential uses 
(see Table 2-1). Single-family residential is the 
most common type of residential development, 
comprising about 70 percent of residential uses, 
and single-family neighborhoods are dispersed 
throughout the City. Multi-family residential 
uses comprise 19 percent of residential uses, 
with multi-family developments bordering the 
east-west corridors of Highway 76, Highway 78, 
and Mesa Drive, as well as the north-south cor-
ridors of El Camino Real, Rancho del Oro Drive, 
and Old Grove Road. Multi-family housing is also 
prevalent in portions of the coastal area.  Rural 
residential uses make up eight percent of res-
idential uses and are primarily located in South 
Morro Hills. Mobile homes make up four percent 
of residential land uses and are concentrated 
near Douglas Drive, west of Interstate 5 around 
Loma Alta Creek, and just south of the SOI. Some 
mobile home parks in Oceanside are subject to 
local rent control.

Commercial uses occupy just three percent of the 
Planning Area. Most commercial uses—including 
retail, lodging, and offices—are located along 
major corridors, including the Coast Highway, 
Highways 76 and 78, and Oceanside Boulevard. 
Downtown Oceanside, which includes the 
blocks surrounding Mission Avenue and Coast 
Highway, contains a mixture of commercial uses, 
and include the City’s main clusters of hospital-
ity uses (five percent of commercial uses) and 
mixed uses (two percent of commercial uses). 
Major regional commercial retail centers include 
the Camino Town and Country Shopping Center 
and the El Camino North Shopping Center at 
the corner of El Camino Real and Highway 78; 
Oceanside Crossing at the corner of Interstate 
5 and Oceanside Boulevard; Mission Shopping 
Center near Mission Avenue and Interstate 5; 
Mission Marketplace near College Boulevard and 
Highway 76; Tri-City Crossroads; Pacific Coast 
Plaza; Town Center North; Plaza Rancho Del 
Oro; and other commercial centers. Office uses, 
which make up around six percent of commer-
cial uses, are primarily located along Highway 76, 
El Camino Real, Mission Avenue, and Downtown. 
Medical office space is concentrated in the Hwy 
78 corridor, with scattered medical office uses 
in Rancho del Oro and the Mission Avenue cor-
ridor.  Major employers in Oceanside include 
Genentech, Gilead Sciences, WalMart, Home 
Depot, and Hobie Cat. 
Industrial uses make up three percent of total 
land uses. Oceanside has 10 industrial business 
parks, including the Airport Industrial Area 
near Highway 76 and the Oceanside Municipal 
Airport; Industry Street, near Oceanside 
Boulevard and west of El Camino Real; the 
large clustered Ocean Ranch Corporate Centre; 
Pacifica Coast Business Park; Rancho Del Oro 
Technology Park; and Seagate Business Center; 
and the smaller clusters of Oceanside Business 
Gateway Park; Prescott Industrial Park; and 
Vista Pacifica/Temple Heights.  Warehousing 
and distribution, biotechnology, sports equip-
ment and apparel, and food processing are 
among the most common industrial uses in 
Oceanside.    
Public and institutional uses make up six per-
cent of the Planning Area’s existing land uses. 
Schools and educational facilities make up 
45 percent of this land use type.  Three public 
school districts operate in Oceanside: Oceanside 
Unified, Vista Unified, and Bonsall Elementary. 
Private schools in Oceanside include Coastal 
Academy, St. Mary Star of the Sea, and several 
Montessori schools.  Mira Costa Community 
College maintains two campuses in the City. 
Several vocational schools operate in Oceanside, 
including UEI College, California Career College, 
and Media Tech Institute.  Most vocational 
schools operate in commercial and industrial 
zoning districts.   Community facilities make up 
30 percent of all public and institutional uses 
and include the Junior Seau Beach Community 
Center, the Melba Bishop Community Center, the 
Chavez Community Center, the Country Club and 
El Corazon senior centers, and the SoCal Sport 
Fields. Other important public or institutional 
uses include the marina and the Oceanside 
Municipal Airport. While most City offices, includ-
ing City Hall, are located west of I-5, the City 
Operations Center is located at 4927 Oceanside 
Boulevard, seven to eight miles away from City 
Hall. Health care facilities, while primarily located 
in commercial and industrial districts, make up 
five percent of all public and institutional uses 
and include the Tri-City Regional Medical Center 
and other healthcare clusters near Highway 78 
the Oceanside Boulevard corridor.

Just over a fifth of the Planning Area (21 percent) 
accommodates recreational and natural open 
space. The largest open space areas are found 
near Oceanside’s northwestern border, along its 
principal watersheds, including the San Luis Rey 
River, Loma Alta Creek, and Buena Vista Lagoon, 
as well as within Guajome County Park south of 
the South Morro Hills area. 
In the City’s northeast corner, the South Morro 
Hills area contains most of the Planning Area’s 
agricultural land, which comprises approxi-
mately 11 percent of total land use. 
Streets and other rights-of-way make up 16 per-
cent of the Planning Area. 
Vacant parcels (less than four percent of the total 
land area) are spread throughout Oceanside. the 
largest parcels are located south of the Crouch 
Street Sprinter Station and Oceanside Boulevard, 
along the Coast Highway corridor, west of 
Thunder Drive and south of Highway 78, and at 
the southeastern corner of Oceanside Boulevard 
and Melrose Drive.
The 20 acres within Oceanside’s SOI are primar-
ily rural residential (14 acres), with four acres 
of public/community facility uses including the 
Sunset Drive Church of Christ and Chabad Jewish 
Center of Oceanside/Vista. Remaining land uses 
include roads/right of way (1 acre), single family 
homes (1 acre), and approximately two acres of 
vacant land.

2.2	Current Plans
CITYWIDE PLANS
Existing General Plan (2002)
The City of Oceanside General Plan and associ-
ated long-range planning documents provide 
goals, policies, and programs that guide land use, 
capital improvements, conservation efforts, and 
municipal operations. The General Plan includes 
all seven elements required by California State 
law, including Land Use, Circulation, Recreational 
Trails (as a sub-element of Circulation) 
Environmental Resources Management, Public 
Safety, Noise, and Housing. Several of these 
elements were prepared and approved at var-
ious times after 1974, with updates in the year 
1986. The General Plan text was reformatted 
without policy changes in 2002; figures were 
not reformatted. The Housing Element, which is 
required by State law to be updated on an eight-
year cycle, was last updated in 2013. The 2013 
Housing Element is in a standalone document 
separate from the other elements but will be 
updated as part of this phase of the General plan 
update. In addition to the required elements, the 
General Plan also includes several optional ele-
ments: Community Facilities, Hazardous Waste 
Management, and Military Reservation. Two 
other elements, the Economic Development and 
Energy Climate Action element, were adopted 
in 2019 as part of the first phase of the General 
Plan Update. 
The major goals of each of the City’s 10 General 
Plan elements are summarized below:
•	 Land Use Element (LUE): The LUE provides 
direction related to how future development 
will occur, including architectural quality, 
intensity/density, and site design, in addition 
to also addresses the relationship between 
development, community enhancement, and 
natural resource management.
•	 Housing Element (HE): The HE is a state 
mandated element of the General Plan 
that requires the City to plan to meet the 
existing and projected housing needs of all 
economic segments of the community. The 
law recognizes that, in order for the private 
market to adequately address housing needs, 
local governments must adopt land use 
plans and regulatory systems that provide 
opportunities for, and do not unduly constrain 
housing development. The primary goals 
of the Housing Element are to facilitate the 
development of a variety of housing types for 
all income levels and resident needs; address 
and remove constraints to the maintenance, 
improvement and development of housing; 
maintain and enhance the quality of existing 
residential neighborhoods; promote equal 
housing opportunities for all persons; and 
encourage new housing growth patterns that 
conform to local, regional and state policies 
for sustainable development and energy use. 
It should be noted that of all the elements, 
State laws pertaining to housing elements 
are some of the most changed since the last 
update, and the new Housing Element would 
also need to reflect the updated housing 
needs for Oceanside as allocated by San Diego 
Association of Governments (SANDAG).

•	 Community Facilities Element (CFE): The 
CFE provides direction for the provision of 
adequate public facilities necessary to serve 
existing and future residential, commercial, 
and industrial development throughout 
the City. The element addresses required 
community facilities needed to meet the 
current needs of the community as well 
as additional needs generated by future 
development. The element is intended 
as a comprehensive information source 
containing data regarding the status of the 
City’s major capital facilities. The element 
guides the phased distribution of funds for the 
construction and maintenance of community 
facilities 
•	 Circulation Element (CE): Last updated 
in 2012, the CE provides policy direction to 
maintain and improve the City of Oceanside’s 
transportation system and enhance travel 
choices for current and future residents, 
visitors, and workers. The CE calls for 
goals, policies, and objectives for long-term 
development and to provide direction for 
future decision making. Cities must have 
an efficient and integrated transportation 
system to essentially maintain quality of life 
and economic growth, and this can be seen 
in Oceanside’s direct impacts related to the 
Economic Development and the CE’s goals, 
polices, and objectives.
•	 Recreational Trails Element: A subsection of 
the Circulation element adopted in 1995, the 
primary goals are to encourage safe multiple 
use trails and a safe, interconnected network 
of bicycle facilities, including a transition 
from Oceanside to Camp Pendleton; develop 
a system of equestrian trails, including a trail 
along the San Luis Rey River corridor; create an 
interconnected network of pedestrian facilities 
linked to destinations; and encourage walking 
through organized programs. 
•	 Environmental Resource Management 
Element (ERME): The ERME ser ves as 
the City’s state-mandated conservation 
element, promoting responsible stewardship 
of local natural resources that include 
fresh water, wildlife habitat, the ocean and 
coastline, agricultural land, cultural sites, and 
recreational and scenic areas. The goals and 
policies of the ERME promote the long-term 
health, quality, and function of the City’s 
natural assets. The ERME calls for the thorough 
evaluation of the impacts of new development 
on local ecosystems and geological features 
and the management of growth “in an 
ecologically, aesthetically, and economically 
sane manner.”

•	 Safety Element (PSE): The PSE identifies risks 
associated with geologic hazards, flooding, 
and wildfire. Adopted in 1975, the PSE does 
not directly acknowledge climate change and 
its potential to increase the City’s exposure 
to both natural and human-induced hazards. 
However, the element does speak to the 
need for civil disaster preparedness through 
coordinated emergency operations, which are 
essential to mitigating the immediate impacts 
of climate-induced phenomena like extreme 
rainfall, coastal storm surge, and wildfire.
•	 Noise Element: Adopted in 1974, the primary 
goal of this element is to minimize the effects 
of excessive noise.
•	 Economic Development Element: The EDE 
was adopted in 2019 and informs future 
actions affecting the City’s fiscal resources and 
the local economy. The overarching goal of the 
EDE is to grow and diversify the City’s economy 
in ways that lift the fortunes and enhance the 
wellbeing of all Oceanside residents. Goals 
and policies in the EDE will enhance quality 
of life; promote economic prosperity; make 
Oceanside more economically resilient; 
and strengthen collaborative partnerships 
between the City, educational institutions, 
business groups, and neighboring cities. 
•	 Energy Climate Action Element: Prepared 
in conjunction with the EDE and also 
adopted in 2019, the ECAE focuses on the 
City’s responsibility to reduce its carbon 
footprint in a manner consistent with the 
state’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions 
reduction targets. The ECAE also addresses 
environmental sustainability more broadly, 
promulgating goals and policies that encourage 
cleaner, healthier, and less resource-intensive 
consumer choices and business practices. The 
ECAE encourages smart growth, alternative 
transportation, solid waste diversion, water 
conservation and local water harvesting, 
urban forestry, and the preservation of local 
agriculture. The fundamental goal of the ECAE 
is to reduce reliance on fossil fuels and move 
the City toward a carbon-neutral future.

General Plan Land Use
Chapter 1: Land Use contains goals and policies 
related to future development, and a diagram of 
the land use designations. The Land Use Element 
represents a strategy and intended uses for 
land use development through 2020 and does 
not necessarily reflect land uses that exist on 
the ground. General Plan land use designations 
are mapped in Figure 2-2 and described in Table 
2-2. In general, Residential land uses are located 
throughout the Planning Area outside of the cor-
ridors. Commercial uses are primarily located 
along the Coast Highway, on Highways 76 and 
78, and Vista Way. Industrial land uses are 
largely found along Oceanside Boulevard, and to 
a lesser extent, Highway 76, Mission Avenue, and 
south of North River Road. Downtown uses span 
the City’s northwestern border southwest of 
Interstate 5 to the coast, with Wisconsin Ave as 
its farthest southern border. Civic Institutional 
uses are distributed throughout the Planning 
Area, while Open Space areas are mostly found 
near the western boundary and around the 
Lower San Luis Rey River. Agricultural uses are 
mostly found in South Morro Hills, the northeast 
portion of the Planning Area. 
Table 2-2 shows the base and maximum res-
idential densities for various residential land 
use designations. The General Plan states that 
densities may not be able to be uniformly attain-
able on all portions of a site, and the difference 
between base and the maximum reflect density 
potential that could be obtained on developable 
portions of a site. However, grant of densities 
higher than the base is discretionary and may 
be given to projects that demonstrate design 
excellence; provide infrastructure, landscape, 
recreational facilities, or open spaces exceeding 
City standard; or contain other features listed 
in the General Plan. Commercial and industrial 
land use intensities are defined in the Zoning 
Ordinance. Commercial intensities range from 
a maximum floor area ratio (FAR) of 1, with an 
allowable bonus FAR of 0.2 to 0.5, with a max-
imum FAR of 4.0 for the entire downtown sub-
district 12. Industrial intensities range from 0.8 
to 1.0.

Zoning Ordinance
Oceanside’s Zoning Ordinance is an implemen-
tation tool for the General Plan and provides 
permitted land uses and development stan-
dards for each land use category. As with the 
General Plan land use designations, zoning 
designations reflect the desired future land use 
pattern, and do not always coincide with what 
is on the ground. The City of Oceanside’s Zoning 
Ordinance only applies within City limits, while 
the small portion of land in the SOI is under 
San Diego County zoning regulations. Figure 
2-3 maps the existing zoning districts for the 
Planning Area, including the unincorporated 
areas under the jurisdiction of San Diego County. 
Table 2-3 summarizes City of Oceanside and 
applicable San Diego County zoning regulations.
The most common zoning designation within 
the Planning Area is residential. There are nine 
different residential zoning districts: Residential 
Estate Districts A and B; Single-Family Residential 
District; Medium-Density Residential Districts A, 
B, and C; the High-Density Residential District; 
the Urban High-Density District; and the 
Residential Tourist District. Though most res-
idential zoning districts have the same height 
limit (36 feet), residential zoning districts differ 
on the basis of minimum lot area, ranging from 
5,000 square feet to 43,560 square feet (one 
acre), and maximum lot coverage ranging from 
30 percent to 60 percent.  There are three 
Residential Coastal Districts—R-I/CZ, R-3/CZ, and 
R-T/CZ—with a height limit of 35 feet or two sto-
ries (whichever is lower), a minimum lot size of 
6,000 square feet, and a maximum lot coverage 
between 40 and 60 percent. 
Most commercial designations are located along 
major corridors, including Mission Avenue/
Highway 76; Oceanside Boulevard; and Vista 
Way/ Highway 78. Heights in commercial districts 
are limited to 50 feet. Minimum lot sizes range 
from 1,000 to 10,000 square feet. Maximum lot 
coverage ranges from 50 to 75 percent. Heights 
within the Coastal Commercial Districts are gen-
erally limited to 45 feet or four stories. Within 
the Downtown Districts heights limits are set at 
35 or 45 feet, with minimum lot sizes of 5,000 
square feet and maximum lot coverage of 75 or 
85 percent.
Industrial areas are located generally east of 
Interstate 5, concentrated around Oceanside 
Boulevard. Industrial districts throughout the 
Planning Area allow building height maximums 
of 80 feet and require larger minimum lot areas 
than commercial districts, starting at a minimum 
of 20,000 square feet in the IL Limited Industrial 
District. 
Any mixed-use development with commer-
cial and residential land uses combined on 
one site requires the submission of a “Mixed-
Use Development Plan” and Conditional Use 
Permit. Base District Regulations and Property 
Development Regulations for Residential 
Districts and Commercial Districts shall serve 
as the guideline for a mixed-use development. 
Minimum Area. The minimum area of a Mixed-
Use Plan is 1-acre, though smaller sites may 
be approved if found to meet the intent and 
purposes established in the Mixed-Use Plan. 
The maximum density for residential units in a 
Mixed-Use Plan project is 29 du/ac for the total 
area of the parcels designated for mixed-use.
The Planning Area also contains several overlay 
districts that establish specific requirements for 
certain geographical areas within the Planning 
Area. These overlay districts include the 
Historic Overlay District (H), Scenic Park Overlay 
District (SP), Neighborhood Conservation 
Overlay District (NC), Interim Study Overlay 
District (IS), Master Plan Overlay District (MP), 
Nonconforming Use Amortization Overlay 
District (N), Equestrian Overlay District (EQ), and 
Senior Mobile Home Park Overlay District.

Parks and Recreation Master Plan 
(2019)
Updated in 2019, Oceanside’s Parks and 
Recreation Master Plan provides a guide for 
the orderly development of future park, recre-
ation, and open space facilities and programs 
in order to meet the community’s current and 
future needs through 2030. The Master Plan 
update includes a recreation needs assessment; 
program recommendations; park and facility 
improvement recommendations; a trail devel-
opment program(?); and a prioritized plan and 
schedule for implementation. 
The Master Plan identifies a few areas with 
accessibility gaps, as measured by the goal of 
a 15-minute walk for neighborhood parks or a 
5-minute drive for community parks and spe-
cial facilities. Additionally, several facilities are 
expected to have level of service deficiencies if 
no additional facilities are built, based on a 2035 
projected service population. Master Plan goals 
include improving connectivity to parks; reno-
vate existing parks and facilities; preserve open 
space; create additional trails through open 
space; augment programs and enhance division 
efficiencies; expand financial opportunities; and 
construct new park facilities. 
Master Plan for the Arts (2019)
In 2017, the Oceanside’s Arts Commission under-
took the process of creating Oceanside’s first 
Master Plan for the Arts (O’Arts), approved in 
2019. O’Arts considers the multiple roles the arts 
play in the local economy, for youth, and in the 
ongoing development taking place throughout 
the community. The Master Plan’s four goals 
include supporting Oceanside economic devel-
opment through arts and culture; enhancing 
the Cityscape through creative placemaking; 
improving Oceanside as a place for artists and 
creatives; and enhancing arts governance. In 
addition to these goals, O’Arts also includes an 
implementation plan.

Coast Highway Vision and Strategic 
Plan, Corridor Study, and Incentive 
District (2019)
In 2009, the City of Oceanside adopted the Coast 
Highway Vision and Strategic Plan (CHVSP) to 
transform the Coast Highway from an auto- 
oriented thoroughfare into a vibrant mixed-use 
pedestrian-oriented environment that accom-
modates new residential uses and expanded 
commercial amenities for residents and visitors. 
This plan serves as a blueprint for the revital-
ization and enhancement of the Coast Highway 
corridor between Harbor Drive in the north and 
Buena Vista Lagoon in the south. 
The CHVSP recommended alternating higher 
density development ‘nodes’ with less dense 
and more landscaped portions of the Coast 
Highway. The CHVSP identifies four nodes for 
concentrated development in the planning area, 
focusing on creating transit-, pedestrian-, and 
bicycle-oriented development and siting higher 
density development adjacent to the two transit 
stations in the planning area. Suggested devel-
opment densities range from 43+ du/acre in the 
nodes, where higher density residential devel-
opment is paired with employment hubs and 
commercial venues. Parking is reconfigured to 
be sited away from street frontage and required 
parking ratios are reduced near transit stations. 
See Figure 2-5 for land use recommendations 
from the Coast Highway Vision and Strategic 
Plan.
In 2014 the City initiated the Coast Highway 
Corridor Study, which assessed existing and 
future transportation conditions and explored 
“complete street” improvements such as reduc-
ing the number of traffic lanes, installing round-
abouts at key intersections, adding bicycle lanes, 
and improving pedestrian facilities. In 2019, City 
Council voted to reduce Coast Highway traffic 
lanes from four to two lanes between the Harbor 
and Morse Street. 
One of the Strategic Plan components includes 
creation of an Incentive District along the cor-
ridor, which provides an alternative to existing 
zoning standards that allows for additional 
building height and density in exchange for 
public benefits, standalone residential uses, and 
expedited permit processing supported by form-
based development standards. The Incentive 
District was adopted in August 2019.  The Smart 
and Sustainable Corridors Plan, a key compo-
nent of the second phase of the General Plan 
Update, will be informed by the Coast Highway 
Incentive District.

Local Coastal Program (includ-
ing Coastal Hazard Vulnerability 
Assessment and Coastal Hazards 
Adaptation Plan, 2018)
The California Coastal Act, passed in 1976, 
requires coastal jurisdictions to adopt a Local 
Coastal Program to ensure local implementation 
of Coastal Act priorities. The City of Oceanside 
is updating its Local Coastal Program, a plan-
ning document that regulates development and 
establishes a long-range vision for property in 
the City’s Coastal Zone, shown in Figure 2-3, an 
area defined under State law which includes all 
property from the inland side of Coast Highway 
to the Pacific Ocean, as well as property in prox-
imity to portions of the San Luis Rey River, Loma 
Alta Creek, and Buena Vista Lagoon.  This update 
of the City’s LCP, assessment of sea-level rise 
impacts and coastal resources vulnerability is 
being undertaken separately. The City adopted 
its current Local Coastal Program in 1986. 
In 2018, the City performed a Vulnerability 
Assessment to address existing conditions 
and future vulnerability of Oceanside’s social, 
economic, and physical coastal resources to 
projected sea-level rise, coastal flooding, and 
erosion. Using publicly available data and best 
available science, the Vulnerability Assessment 
identified several areas currently at-risk that 
may experience increased exposure to hazards, 
as well as those that may be subject to flooding 
under projected future conditions. 
Informed by this assessment, Oceanside pro-
duced an administrative draft of the Coastal 
Hazards Adaptation Plan (AP). Designed as a 
toolbox, the draft AP outlines a wide range of 
potential adaptation strategies the City can 
deploy as future conditions warrant. The AP eval-
uates the feasibility and efficacy of each of these 
strategies, highlighting associated tradeoffs. The 
draft AP also identifies potential triggers that 
would provoke the City to reevaluate and/or 
implement adaptation strategies, such as beach 
widths, extent of sea level rise, and others, in 
addition to establishment of benchmarks, moni-
toring, and documentation protocols. 
It is estimated that completion of the LCP and 
accompanying Local Coastal Land Use Plan, 
which will include land use, public access, rec-
reational and visitor-serving uses, water quality, 
biological resources, visual resources, and shore-
line protection policies, will be brought forward 
for adoption in 2021. 
Agritourism Strategic Plan (2016)
In an effort to identify the dynamics of the local 
agritourism industry and its potential impact on 
tourism, the local community and stakeholders, 
the City of Oceanside began development of an 
agritourism plan in 2015. The strategy outlines 
two tiers of new agritourism uses for South 
Morro Hills. Tier 1 is comprised of lower-in-
tensity and lower investment uses like U-Pick 
operations, petting zoos, farm stands, and small 
bed and breakfasts. Tier 2 is comprised of high-
er-intensity and higher-investment uses that 
may require additional public infrastructure, like 
hotels, festivals, and larger retail operations to 
sell farm products. The City of Oceanside Zoning 
Ordinance has been recently updated to allow 
Tier 1 uses. Tier 2 uses are contingent upon the 
South Morro Hills Community Plan, preparation 
of which is underway concurrently with the 
General Plan.

El Corazon Specific Plan (2009)
Once the site of the U.S. Silica Mining Company 
and donated to the City in 1994, the 465-acre El 
Corazon property is located between El Camino 
Real and Rancho del Oro Drive to the west and 
east, and Oceanside Boulevard and Mesa Drive 
to the south and north. In 2009, the El Corazon 
Specific Plan was adopted to guide and imple-
ment the vision for El Corazon Specific Plan 
Area as established by the El Corazon Master 
Planning process in 2003. The site is divided 
into six districts, each with a particular vision 
for future development with development stan-
dards, customized allowable uses, and design 
guidelines: parks and recreation (212 acres), 
habitat (164 acres), civic services (34 acres), 
Oceanside Boulevard commercial (25 acres), vil-
lage commercial (19 acres), and hotel (11 acres). 
Development of the El Corazon Specific Plan 
began in with groundbreaking of the El Corazon 
Aquatics Center on October 30, 2019.  Work is 
scheduled to be completed in the spring of 2021 
with a grand opening slated shortly afterward. 
The City recently approved Arena Califino, a mul-
tipurpose sports arena and event center with 
associated parking that will be sited In the north-
east quadrant of El Corazon. The City has also 
initiated public outreach on Park Site I, which 
will include a variety of recreational features and 
amenities.
Oceanside Boulevard Vision Statement 
(2007)
In 2007, City Council directed staff to initiate a 
visioning and master planning effort for the 
area of Oceanside Boulevard bound by I-5 and 
El Camino Real. The visioning process for the 
Oceanside Boulevard integrated input from staff, 
the Task Force, and the public, and resulted in a 
vision for a beautiful and sustainable creek along 
a vibrant and prosperous corridor. The Vision 
Statement outlined steps to achieve this vision, 
including development of a Master Plan; encour-
agement of economic development and redevel-
opment of areas to offer quality services and a 
mix of uses; creation of a continuous network of 
paths and open spaces to encourage bicycling 
and pedestrian activity; incorporation of natu-
ral landscaping, art, and placemaking compo-
nents; implementation of traffic management 
techniques;  and establishment of a revitalized 
Loma Alta creek as the “core” of the open space 
and trail system. The City sought grant funding 
in 2009 for the Master Planning project; how-
ever, this effort was not completed.  
Downtown Oceanside Master Plan 
(2000)
The Downtown Oceanside Master Plan was 
prepared to implement the land use policies 
approved by the Coastal Commission as part 
of LCP-1-91, which encourages the develop-
ment of visitor-serving commercial facilities and 
tourist and visitor-oriented accommodations 
with the Strand Study Area. The Land Use Plan 
further requires that a minimum amount of vis-
itor-serving commercial facilities and visitor-ori-
ented accommodations be constructed within 
the nine-block area bounded by The Strand, 
Cleveland Street, Seagaze Drive and Civic Center 
Drive (known in the Plan as the MPA).
The Master Plan applies to a nine-block area and 
is designed to assure a high quality of develop-
ment on the site, consistent with the objectives 
of the community, the Redevelopment Plan 
and the Local Coastal Program. In addition, 
the Master Plan establishes development stan-
dards and clear criteria for evaluating proposed 
developments within the MPA. The Master 
Plan consists of standards and guidelines and 
serves to implement existing City regulations 
(e.g. Zoning Ordinance, Local Coastal Program) 
and Community Development Commission 
land use regulations and development criteria. 
Development in the nine-block area has either 
been completed or is underway.

Ocean Ranch Master Development 
Plan (1999)
The Ocean Ranch Corporate Center was origi-
nally approved in 1999 to subdivide a 393-acre 
parcel into 21 industrial lots and establish the 
Ocean Ranch Master Development Plan. The 
site is comprised of four separate parcels total-
ing 4.22 acres located at the northwest corner 
of Ocean Ranch Boulevard and Maritime Way 
within the Ocean Ranch Corporate Center. 
Ocean Ranch now includes corporate offices of 
Coca-Cola, Hydranautics, Titleist, Genentech 
Pharmaceuticals, Gilead, Suja, Pirch, Magnaflow 
and Marriott. The Site also includes the nearly 
100,000 square foot San Diego Live Well Center, 
a county health hub offering a family resource 
center, public health services, military and vet-
eran services, aging and independent services, 
and child welfare services. 
Rancho Del Oro Specific Plan (1985)
The Rancho Del Oro Specific plan encompasses 
approximately 1,940 acres of land within the 
geographic center of the City. The plan was pre-
pared to provide for the development of a high 
quality, comprehensive planned community 
made up of several subdivisions or “villages,” 
each with its own master plan. Many of those 
villages are located near College Boulevard 
north of Oceanside Boulevard and others south 
of Mesa Drive between College and El Camino 
Real. The mixed land use plan provides for the 
phased development of the property with indus-
trial and commercial uses as well as a variety of 
residential housing opportunities and includes 
provisions for implementing circulation system 
and public utility improvements.

REGIONAL PLANS
San Diego Forward: The Regional Plan 
The 2021 San Diego Forward: The Regional Plan 
(“Regional Plan”), adopted by the San Diego 
Association of Governments Board of Directors 
on October 25, 2019, is a long-term blueprint 
and vision for how the San Diego region will 
grow and facilitate getting around through 2050. 
The Regional Plan combines three planning doc-
uments that SANDAG must complete in accor-
dance with state and federal laws: The Regional 
Transportation Plan, Sustainable Communities 
Strategy, and Regional Comprehensive Plan. The 
Regional Plan also supports other regional trans-
portation planning and programming efforts, 
including overseeing which projects are funded 
under the Regional Transportation Improvement 
Program and the TransNet program.
The Regional Plan is guided by five key strate-
gies, known as the 5 Big Moves, which include 
Complete Corridors, Transit Leap, Flexible Fleets, 
the Next OS, and Mobility Hubs. The Oceanside 
Transit Center is envisioned to become a Mobility 
Hub, a well-connected transportation center that 
provides an integrated suite of mobility services, 
amenities, and supporting technologies to facili-
tate connectivity between high-frequency transit 
and an individual’s origin or destination. 
Oceanside Municipal Airport Land Use 
Compatibility Plan (2010)
This Compatibility Plan, prepared for Oceanside 
Municipal Airport (the Airport), is the fundamen-
tal tool used by the San Diego County Regional 
Airport Authority (SDCRAA) in promoting airport 
land use compatibility. The Compatibility Plan 
establishes policies which the SDCRAA uses for 
orderly growth of the airport and surrounding 
area, and to protect human health, including pol-
icies to address potential compatibility impacts 
related to noise, safety, airspace protection, and 
flight activity zones.

Figure 2-6: Key Growth in Population (2010-2018)

Population growth has largely occurred along 
the I-5 corridor and north of SR 76 in the East

western edge of the city, adjacent to the neigh-

of Morro Hills has experienced significant pop-

area did not show as much growth as expected, 
downtown growth may have occurred after 2018 
and therefore may not be captured by the cur-
rently available data.
Areas with the highest population density are not 
necessarily places that experienced the greatest 
growth between 2010-2018, with the exception 
of the San Luis Rey and North Valley neighbor-
hoods. For example, downtown Oceanside and 
the Tri-City neighborhoods are areas with higher 
population densities but did not have much pop-
ulation growth. On the other hand, the Greens 
neighborhood adjacent to Arrowood Golf Course 
and the neighborhood by Hubbard Lake north 
of the municipal airport experienced a signifi-
cant amount of growth. This growth may also be 
associated with new residential developments in 
those areas within the past decade. The Pacific 
Ridge and St. Cloud housing developments 
brought population growth to the Ivey Ranch/
Rancho del Oro Neighborhood Planning Area, 
as well as in the eastern edge of the City near 
the intersection of Oceanside Boulevard and 
Melrose Drive.

MAJOR PROJECTS
El Corazon
The El Corazon project is currently planned to 
contain a mix of land uses including parks and 
recre11ation, retail, residential and a hotel. In 
2019, Oceanside broke ground in October for con-
struction of the City’s El Corazon Aquatics Center, 
which will have the City’s first new public swim-
ming pool in 30 years.  The Aquatics Center will 
be accessible to Oceanside residents, local school 
districts, and regional swim meets. The Aquatics 
Center is expected to open in summer 2021.
North County Transit Center
The Oceanside Transit Center is one of the 
most robust transit hubs in southern California, 
providing rail service to downtown San Diego, 
Escondido, Los Angeles, and numerous points 
in between. Located in the 800 block of Mission 
Avenue, the 10.2-acre site is part of a proposed 
mixed-use development that will include as 
many as 101 affordable housing units and 547 
market-rate apartments or condominiums, as 
well as a 40,000-square-foot office building. 
Construction of the transit-oriented develop-
ment is slated to begin in 2024.
Downtown Development
Over the past five years, Downtown Oceanside 
has seen a dramatic resurgence brought about 
by new hotels and other visitor-serving uses, 
mixed-use development, new public amenities 
on the beachfront, and popular uses like the 
Sunset Market. The following are some of the 
major developments either under construction 
or approved for development in Downtown 
Oceanside.
GF Properties Mixed Use Sites
The GF Properties Mixed Use project includes 
four lots in Downtown Oceanside, including Lot 
5, Lot 18, Lot 19, and Lot 20. Lots 18 and 19, or 
the Pierside South and Pierside North apart-
ments, have recently been completed. Lot and 
retail have just been completed. Lots 5 and 20 
are approved for 64 units and 17,550 square feet 
of retail space. 
The Joie de Vivre and Destination Hotels
Located between North Pacific and Myers 
streets and spanning two City blocks, the 
adjacent Joie de Vivre Hotels and Destination 
Hotels properties will be the largest beachfront 
development on the San Diego coastline in over 
twenty years. The Joie de Vivre property will fea-
ture 160 guest rooms and suites and over 3,000 
square feet of street-front retail. The Joie de 
Vivre hotel will feature a restored Graves House, 
a historically protected Queen Anne Victorian 
commonly known as the “Top Gun” house. The 
Destination Hotels property will feature 226 
guest rooms and suites. The hotel properties 
will be located adjacent to the Oceanside Amtrak 
Station, offering direct rail access to several 
Southern California hubs. 
Residential and Mixed Use 
Developments
Major residential projects proposed downtown 
include the Seagaze 16, which will include 6 
ground floor live/work units and 10 residen-
tial units at 716 Seagaze Drive.  The One 11 
Tremont project includes three four-story 
buildings with 15 luxury condominiums and 3 
for-sale commercial spaces at the ground floor. 
The proposed Belvedere Mixed Use project is 
a mid-rise, mixed-use development located 
on a 65,949-square-foot site that includes 90 
Live/Work Units, 124 hotel rooms, and 8357 sq. 
ft. commercial/retail space. The North Beach 
Promenade development, commonly known as 
Lot #23, will include 52 condominiums and 9825 
sq. ft. commercial/retail space.

Ocean KAMP
The mixed-use Ocean KAMP Project is pro-
posed at the former Oceanside swap meet site 
located at 3480 Mission Avenue, Oceanside, CA 
92054. Proposed commercial uses would be 
located within the central/southwestern portion 
of the site, and would include a 300-key resort 
hotel, hotel conference buildings, a surf lagoon 
and beach club, and 11 commercial buildings 
(office, retail, restaurants, fitness center). Multi-
family residential uses are proposed to include 
approximately 700 units located within the 
northern and eastern portions of the project 
site. Approximately 20 acres of the site would be 
preserved as open space.
North River Farms
Approved by the City Council in 2019, the 17.6-
acre North River Farms project site is located 
within the South Morro Hills community. The 
project includes a residential com¬munity of 
approximately 585 homes, a 68-acre working 
farm, and other agriculture-focused amenities. 
As part of the November 2020 election, two-
thirds of voters rejected Measure L, which would 
change the zoning to Planned Development to 
accommodate the North River Farms project. 
More information about South Morro Hills can 
be found in the South Morro Hills Map Atlas.

2.5	Key Planning Issues 
and Implications
•	 Diverse mix of land uses. For a coastal 
city, the Oceanside Planning Area is unique 
in its relatively large amount of contiguous 
agricultural land and light industrial land uses. 
A little over a fifth of Oceanside’s land is parks 
or open space. The majority of Oceanside’s 
existing land use and designated General Plan 
land use is single-family residential. 
•	 Balancing economic objectives through 
land use choices with an eye toward future 
market trends. With the exception of a few 
standalone shopping centers, most commercial 
land use occurs along the vehicle-oriented 
corridors of Mission Avenue/Highway 76; Vista 
Way/ Highway 78; and the South Coast Highway. 
Between these commercially oriented corridors 
are vast areas of single-family residential land 
uses. As Oceanside seeks to accommodate 
more housing and employment, the General 
Plan will need to consider how future trends 
might affect future land use designation 
decisions, such as shifts toward working 
from home after the COVID-19 pandemic, 
changes to commercial patterns including 
online shopping and the accompanying rise 
in fulfillment centers, and effects of climate 
change on development close to the coast. 
The update offers the city an ability to balance 
land use supply with its economic development 
objectives, while providing flexibility to respond 
to changing market conditions.
•	 Accommodating the expansion of the 
industrial sector. The City’s inventory of 
developable industrial land is now almost 
completely saturated.  The City will thus need 
to consider changes to land use standards to 
accommodate additional industrial uses.  This 
may include more flexible commercial land 
use standards that provide for low-intensity 
and low-impact industrial uses In commercial 
zoning districts, as well as more restrictions on 
commercial and institutional uses in industrial 
zoning districts.  The City can also consider 
ways to promote more efficient utilization 
of industrial land (e.g., high-employment 
uses, smaller parking footprints, multi-story 
buildings).
•	 Efficient utilization of limited land 
resources. After four decades of rapid and 
expansive growth, the City is now largely 
“built out” in the conventional sense, with 
little vacant land available for development of 
any kind. Of the existing vacant land, much is 
already slated for development. Many vacant 
properties still eligible for development face a 
variety of physical and regulatory constraints 
– e.g., steep topography, sensitive habitat, 
limited access, and utility easements. The 
remainder of the City’s undeveloped acreage 
is either preserved open space or agricultural 
land. Moreover, being bounded by the Pacific 
Ocean, Camp Pendleton, and the cities of 
Carlsbad and Vista, Oceanside has a limited 
sphere of influence beyond its incorporated 
area and thus little opportunity to annex 
additional land. As Oceanside looks ahead to 
an era of infill rather than outward growth, it 
will be critical that the General Plan update 
considers land use alternatives that balance 
several goals, including housing development, 
economic vitality, open space and agricultural 
preservation, and meeting other community 
needs.

•	 Aligning General Plan update with several 
new and existing plans. The community 
vision, priorities, and consequent policies of 
the South Morro Hills Community Plan and The 
Smart & Sustainable Corridors Specific Plan will 
shape land use alternative options for General 
Plan Update. Several existing area-specific 
planning efforts, such as the El Corazon Master 
Plan, Local Coastal Program, Coast Highway 
Master Plan, and the Oceanside Boulevard 
Vision Statement. If applicable, the General Plan 
should provide updated information and goals 
for these neighborhoods as well as other areas 
covered under more outdated documents.
•	 Accommodating pipeline development. 
A substantial amount of new development, 
including housing, hospital/medical, retail, 
and commercial uses is currently under 
construction, entitled, or in the entitlement 
review process. In the past few years, major 
office and institutional projects have completed 
construction along Oceanside Boulevard, 
particularly in the Ocean Ranch Corporate 
Center and the Pacific Coast Collection and a 
few locations along Vista Way. Several hotels 
and mixed use projects that are currently being 
constructed or have been recently completed 
in Downtown Oceanside.  East of I-5, several 
major entitled mixed use projects are include 
the OceanKAMP resort, the El Corazon site, 
and the North River Farms project. Oceanside 
also has several entitled residential projects 
and a few under construction near the Mission 
Avenue corridor. The General Plan must take 
into account new growth, including jobs and 
residents, that current development projects 
will bring to Oceanside. Oceanside’s future 
development pattern could be an opportunity 
to create a denser, pedestrian-oriented city, 
with more mixed-use development occurring in 
the corridors. 
•	 Facilitating mixed use development. 
The Zoning Code offers a high degree of 
flexibility in designing mixed use projects. 
While there are no mixed-use districts in 
the General Plan or the Zoning Ordinance, 
housing is allowed in nearly all of the City’s 
commercial districts through administrative 
conditional use permits or Mixed-Use Plans. 
In the interest of promoting activity nodes 
that can serve as focal points for surrounding 
neighborhoods, there is an opportunity to 
create designated mixed-use zones to provide 
more guidance to developers about mixed use 
along key corridors while retaining the existing 
flexibility. Mixed use does not necessarily 
mean a mix of uses on every site; rather, land 
use designations and policies can be crafted 
to promote a mix of uses within an area or 
along a corridor. In developing and vetting 
land use alternatives, options for mixed use 
development will be tested with property 
owners, community members, and other 
stakeholders and evaluated in view of current 
and projected market conditions.
