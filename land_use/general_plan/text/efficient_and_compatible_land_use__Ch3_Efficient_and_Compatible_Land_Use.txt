and regulated to encourage efficient, compatible, 
and synergetic land uses that provide for housing,

3.1	Background
With its capacity to accommodate human activity, 
supply essential resources, support natural pro-
cesses, and inspire the mind and the spirit, land 
is arguably a community’s most valuable physical 
asset. It is thus essential that Oceanside manage 
land resources efficiently, provide for a wide range 
of uses that meet the needs of the community, 
promote synergies between uses and activities, 
and maintain open space for ecological, cultural, 
recreational, and aesthetic purposes. The Efficient 
and Compatible Land Use Element (ECLU) seeks 
to facilitate balanced land use and organized, con-
text-sensitive development that together foster 
an attractive and functional built environment, a 
healthy and bountiful natural environment, and 
appropriate connections and buffers between 
these two realms. 
Comprised of over three miles of coastline, allu-
vial valleys extending from the Pacific Ocean to 
the City’s inland boundaries, and hills and mesas 
that form distinct neighborhoods and corridors, 
Oceanside is in many respects a microcosm of 
the San Diego region. Oceanside’s land resources 
accommodate over 65,000 housing units, nearly 
10,000 non-residential buildings, more than 
30 public parks, roughly 3,500 acres of farm-
land, approximately 2,600 acres of preserved 
natural habitat, and over 4,000 acres of public 
rights-of-way that accommodate streets, active 
transportation facilities, utilities, landscape, and 
other public amenities. These features of the 
City’s built and natural environments collectively 
lend Oceanside its distinct physical character, and, 
in turn, influence housing, transportation, com-
merce, physical activity, social interaction, energy 
use, ecological processes, etc. While we may not 
often be conscious of it, Oceanside’s land use 
and development patterns shape our day-to-day 
experience of the City, while largely determining 
how its natural resources and other assets are 
accessed, utilized, and managed. 
Extending from the shoreline of the Pacific Ocean 
to the farmland of South Morro Hills, Oceanside’s 
42 square miles encompass a diversity of built and 
natural environments. The City’s coastal area com-
prises highly developed residential neighborhoods 
and business districts, connected by a grided street 
network and several major arterial corridors. Inland 
areas accommodate single-family subdivisions, 
clusters of attached housing, mobile home parks, 
strip commercial corridors, and industrial parks. 
Interspersed throughout the built environment are 
public parks, schools, places of worship, private 
open space areas, and other uses that provide for 
respite, recreation, and social interaction. 
The natural environment within the City includes 
three miles of coastline, three major watercours-
es and associated riparian habitat, several small 
lakes, upland habitat areas, and landscaped slopes. 
Natural open space can also be found in many of

the City’s 33 public parks. A designated wildlife 
corridor extends through the City between the 
Calavera Preserve in Carlsbad and wildland on 
Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton. 
A unique resource for an incorporated city, 
Oceanside’s 3,500 acres of designated farmland 
support both commercial and avocational farming 
operations, limit the extension of infrastructure, 
provide a profound sense of openness, and serve 
as a buffer between Camp Pendleton and more 
developed areas. This farmland harkens back to a 
time when much of the City’s land resources were 
devoted to agricultural use. 
The City seeks to strike a balance between accom-
modating new development, which provides for 
housing, employment, and essential services, and 
the protection and enhancement of the natural 
environment, which provides for ecological bal-
ance, resilience from natural hazards, recreation-
al opportunities, and visual quality. The City also 
seeks to conserve farmland and promote the eco-
nomic viability of farming, which will likely neces-
sitate additional flexibility for housing and agri-
tourism development. In pursuit of this balance, 
the ECLU outlines strategies for accommodating 
future growth in an equitable and sustainable 
manner and promoting land use patterns that make 
efficient use of the City’s limited land resources, 
facilitate open space and natural resource protec-
tion, and create an organized, cohesive, attractive, 
and uplifting built environment. The ECLU further 
seeks to coordinate land use and mobility planning 
to promote walking, biking, and transit, improve 
access to the places people want and need to go, 
and minimize the impacts of motorized transpor-
tation (e.g., safety hazards, pollution, noise, glare). 
PLANNING CONTEXT
Over the past 50 years, Oceanside has grown out-
ward, with the City’s incorporated area extending 
inland to nearly 10 miles from the coastline. After 
decades of growth into the San Luis Rey Valley, the 
Oceanside Boulevard corridor, and the Highway 
78 corridor, the City has essentially reached the 
limits of outward expansion. Bounded by the 
Pacific Ocean to the west, Marine Corps Base 
Camp Pendleton and the unincorporated com-
munity of Fallbrook to the north, the City of Vista 
and the unincorporated community of Bonsall to 
the east, and the City of Carlsbad to the south, 
Oceanside must now accommodate future growth 
through infill, redevelopment, and adaptive reuse 
of existing buildings.
This 
reality 
presents 
both 
challenges 
and 
opportunities. 
The lack of vacant developable property increases 
the cost of land (and, in turn the cost of housing and 
business), intensifies development pressure on open 
space and farmland, and limits or precludes certain 
types of housing and other development (e.g., sin-
gle-family homes, intensive industrial operations 
that require land assembly). The lack of vacant 
developable land can also make it difficult for local 
governments to provide public facilities, including 
parks, trails, and other recreational amenities.

At the same time, when “greenfield” for new devel-
opment is in short supply, new possibilities often 
arise. Owners of developed property become 
more motivated to pursue higher and better use. 
Both public and private investment can be redi-
rected to urban areas that have lost their utility 
and vitality. There is less need to extend costly 
infrastructure and public services into peripheral 
areas, thereby freeing up fiscal resources for other 
purposes. Compatible and complementary uses 
– once separated under Euclidean zoning – can 
be better integrated, thereby enhancing access to 
commercial goods and services, public amenities, 
and jobs. In turn, as the built environment becomes 
finer-grained, more efficiently utilized, and more 
inhabited, the commercial sector becomes more 
stable and diversified, active transportation and 
transit use become more viable, and there are more 
“eyes on the street” to provide natural surveillance 
and encourage civil and responsible behavior.
Acknowledging the finite nature of the City’s land 
encourages efficient use of developable property 
and thoughtful stewardship of natural and work-
ing lands. As Oceanside approaches traditional 
“build-out” conditions – i.e., little to no remaining 
inventory of vacant land available for develop-
ment – it is arguably more important than ever 
that the City promote both efficient land use in 
the built environment and conscientious care of 
its open spaces, sensitive habitat, and farmland. 
Indeed, doing so will be key to affording future 
generations of Oceansiders with the resources 
and opportunities that past and current genera-
tions have been able to enjoy. While the Oceanside 
community has expressed an openness to growth 
and change, there is an expectation that this 
growth and change will occur sustainably – that is 
to say, in a manner that conserves and enhances 
the City’s most important assets to the benefit of 
all community members. 
The City has undertaken various initiatives to pro-
mote infill development and enhance its Downtown 
and coastal areas in recent years, attracting 
new businesses, restaurants, and entertainment 
venues. Moreover, Oceanside's discrete plan-
ning areas, such as the Coastal zone, Downtown 
District, Planned Developments (PDs), Specific 
Plans (SPs), Master Plans (MPs), and Planned 
Business Districts (PBDs), ensure a well-organized 
and cohesive development approach. These plan-
ning areas, along with overlays that address specif-
ic needs or requirements, allow for future growth 
in the City to be thoughtful, strategic, and aligned 
with the community’s priorities. The General Plan 
furthers these steps by carefully orchestrating the 
location and intensity of new development.
Community Priorities
Throughout the planning process, Oceansiders 
have voiced their priorities for the future develop-
ment of the City. Preserving and enhancing existing 
residential neighborhoods emerged as a key focus 
for Oceansiders, with most community members 
supporting the direction of concentrating future 
growth along the City’s three major corridors and 
maintaining the unique character and charm of 
existing communities. Another critical priority is 
providing a range of housing options for commu-
nity members in order to maintain Oceanside as a 
diverse and inclusive city for its growing population.
Community members also emphasized the impor-
tance of balancing new development with the pres-
ervation of natural habitat, open space, parks and 
recreation areas, and unique farmland. The vast 
majority of those who have participated in com-
munity engagement activities agree that in order 
to safeguard Oceanside’s natural resources and 
promote a sustainable future, new development 
should generally occur within already urbanized 
areas. Where new development is proposed in or 
near natural habitat, community members expect 
the City to require proper mitigation, buffers, and 
long-term management strategies that ensure the 
ongoing viability of sensitive species.

Finally, Oceansiders want the City to prioritize 
public safety and security within both the built and 
natural environments. From a land use standpoint, 
this means siting and designing new development 
to enhance City and neighborhood visual quality, 
activate surrounding areas, and place “eyes on the 
street” as well as phasing out or removing uses 
and activities that attract nuisance. Providing for 
emergency access and ensuring resiliency against 
natural hazards are topics that are addressed in 
the Safe and Resilient Environment (SRE) Element.
Relationship to Other Plans
There are a number of related plans and pro-
grams—both local and regional—that are consid-
ered in the formulation, adoption, and implemen-
tation of local land use policy. Regional planning 
agencies, such as the San Diego Association of 
Governments (SANDAG), recognize that planning 
issues extend beyond the boundaries of individual 
cities. Efforts to address regional planning issues 
such as air quality, transportation, and habitat con-
servation have resulted in the adoption of regional 
plans, some of which directly influence the form 
and distribution of development in Oceanside. 
Relevant local and regional plans related to the 
Land Use Element are discussed in Section 3.2 of 
the ECLU.
TOPICS ADDRESSED
State law (Government Code Section 65302(a)) 
requires general plans to include a Land Use 
Element. In accordance with State law, this chap-
ter designates the general distribution, location, 
and extent of land for housing, business, industry, 
open space, education, public facilities, and other 
categories of public and private uses of land. It also 
includes standards of residential and non-residen-
tial density for the various areas covered by the 
General Plan.
The ECLU guides the location, form, and character 
of new development, shaping where people live, 
work, play, and shop. It presents the desired pat-
tern for the ultimate development of the City for 
the General Plan horizon (year 2050) and seeks 
to ensure that the community’s evolution reflects 
the City’s careful planning, conserves the natural 
environment, and promotes synergies between 
different land uses that encourage walking, biking, 
and sustainable lifestyles. The ECLU addresses 
housing supply and density, smart growth strat-
egies, conservation of natural and agricultural 
lands, climate mitigation and adaptation, and envi-
ronmental justice and social equity. The Element 
is closely related to several others, including the 
Housing Element and the Remarkable Community 
(RC) Element.
Among the important implementation mech-
anisms for the Land Use Element are specific 
and area plans, and the Zoning Ordinance. The 
California Government Code requires that a city’s 
zoning ordinance be consistent with its gener-
al plan, and that all provisions of specific plans 
and site-specific projects undertaken by a city 
must be consistent with the general plan. While 
Oceanside’s Zoning Ordinance and General Plan 
regulate allowable development, the State has 
passed a variety of laws in recent years that allow 
developers to access increased residential densi-
ty or exemptions to local land use regulations if 
they meet certain conditions stipulated by State 
law, chiefly to further affordable housing devel-
opment. Additionally, signed into law in 2019, SB 
330 has curtailed the authority of cities to further 
limit residential capacity and to deny the approval 
of residential developments based on regulatory 
barriers. Going forward, the City aims to use the 
goals and policies in the General Plan to comple-
ment and support State legislation and incentivize 
affordable housing development, while ensuring 
that new housing is built in accordance with the 
City’s smart growth priorities and enhances the 
public realm.
The goals and policies of the ECLU are intend-
ed to further the Oceanside Community Vision, 
accepted by the City Council in November 2021. 
The Vision reflects the community’s aspirations 
for Oceanside’s future. The Vision summarizes the 
broad goals to be achieved through the updated 
General Plan and companion planning documents, 
including the Smart and Sustainable Corridors 
Specific Plan (SSCSP), the Active Transportation 
Plan, and the Trails Master Plan.

3.2	Related Plans and 
Programs
Crafting a comprehensive and forward-thinking 
Land Use Element first requires an examination of 
the existing plans and programs that have shaped 
the City’s development landscape. Oceanside, 
with its stunning coastal location and diverse 
communities, has been the subject of numerous 
initiatives and strategies aimed at sustainable 
growth, infrastructure enhancement, and commu-
nity well-being. This section delves into previous 
planning efforts, all of which play a pivotal role in 
shaping the future of Oceanside’s land use.
REGIONAL PLANS AND 
PROGRAMS
Coastal Zone (1976)
The Coastal Zone is a legislatively-defined geo-
graphic region that establishes State jurisdiction as 
outlined in the California Coastal Act. The Coastal 
Zone encompasses both land and water areas 
along the entire length of the California coastline 
from the Oregon border to the Mexico border. In 
partnership with local governments, the California 
Coastal Commission (CCC) has planning, regulatory, 
and permitting authority over much of the “devel-
opment” taking place within the Coastal Zone. 
North County Multiple Habitat 
Conservation Program (2003)
The North County Multiple Habitat Conservation 
Program (MHCP) was prepared by SANDAG and 
adopted in 2003. The MHCP seeks to conserve 
approximately 19,000 acres of sensitive habitat in 
the cities of Carlsbad, Escondido, Oceanside, San 
Marcos, and Vista, roughly 2,500 acres of which 
lies within Oceanside. When completed, the hab-
itat preservation areas will serve as a key compo-
nent of the region’s smart growth efforts by pre-
serving habitat and open space and by directing 
forecasted growth into appropriate areas.
Oceanside Municipal Airport Land Use 
Compatibility Plan (2010)
This Compatibility Plan, prepared for Oceanside 
Municipal Airport (the Airport), is the fundamen-
tal tool used by the San Diego County Regional 
Airport Authority (SDCRAA) in promoting airport 
land use compatibility. The Compatibility Plan 
establishes policies which the SDCRAA uses for 
orderly growth of the airport and surrounding 
area, and to protect human health, including pol-
icies to address potential compatibility impacts 
related to noise, safety, airspace protection, and 
flight activity zones. 
San Diego Forward: The Regional Plan 
(2019)
The 2021 San Diego Forward: The Regional Plan 
(“Regional Plan”), adopted by the SANDAG Board 
of Directors on October 25, 2019, is a long-term 
blueprint and vision for how the San Diego region 
will grow and facilitate getting around through 
2050.The Regional Plan combines three planning 
documents that SANDAG must complete in accor-
dance with state and federal laws: The Regional 
Transportation Plan, Sustainable Communities 
Strategy, and Regional Comprehensive Plan. The 
Regional Plan also supports other regional transpor-
tation planning and programming efforts, including 
overseeing which projects are funded under the 
Regional Transportation Improvement Program and 
the TransNet program.
The Regional Plan is guided by five key strategies, 
known as the 5 Big Moves, which include Complete 
Corridors, Transit Leap, Flexible Fleets, the Next 
OS, and Mobility Hubs. The Oceanside Transit 
Center is envisioned to become a Mobility Hub, a

CITYWIDE PLANS AND 
PROGRAMS
Zoning Ordinance (Ongoing)
Oceanside’s Zoning Ordinance is an implementa-
tion tool for the General Plan and provides permit-
ted land uses and development standards for each 
land use category. As with the General Plan land 
use designations, zoning designations reflect the 
desired future land use pattern, and do not always 
coincide with what is on the ground. The City of 
Oceanside’s Zoning Ordinance only applies within 
City limits. 
Parks and Recreation Master Plan 
(2019)
In 2019, the City adopted a new Parks and 
Recreation Master Plan (Parks Master Plan). The 
Parks Master Plan provides guidelines for the 
orderly development of future park, recreation, 
and open space facilities and programs to meet 
the community’s current and future needs. The 
Parks Master Plan is intended to provide a vision 
and path forward for the parks and recreation 
system through 2030. More information on the 
Parks Master Plan and the City’s parks and recre-
ation resources can be found in the Healthy and 
Livable Community (HLC) Element. 
LOCAL MANAGEMENT AREAS 
Coast Hwy Vision and Strategic Plan 
(2009) and Coast Highway Incentive 
District (2024)
The Coast Highway Vision and Strategic Plan serves 
as a blueprint for the revitalization and enhance-
ment of the Coast Highway corridor. Adopted in 
2024, the plan is an advisory document that is used 
to guide future development in the area (via urban 
and building design guidelines) and to inform future 
legislative planning efforts such as General Plan, 
Local Coastal Plan, and Zoning Ordinance changes 
pertinent to the Planning Area.
As part of the implementation of the Coast 
Highway Vision and Strategic Plan, the City 
established an Incentive District along the Coast 
Highway corridor from Seagaze Drive to the City’s 
southern boundary at Buena Vista Lagoon. The 
Incentive District provides an alternative to (not a 
replacement of) existing zoning standards, allowing 
additional building height and density, standalone 
residential use, and expedited permit processing 
in exchange for public benefits and adherence to 
form-based development standards. 
Guajome Scenic Overlay District 
Owned and operated by the County of San Diego, 
Guajome Regional Park is one of the City’s fore-
most visitor destinations, providing over 30 camp-
sites, 4.5 miles of walking trails, playgrounds, and 
other amenities. The park supports wetland, ripar-
ian, and upland habitats and contributes to the 
visual character of eastern portions of the City.
The Guajome Park Scenic Overlay District seeks 
to ensure that land use and development in the 
vicinity of Guajome Regional Park complements 
and preserves the integrity of this important eco-
logical, recreational, and scenic resource. The pur-
pose and intent of the Overlay District is outlined 
in the City’s Zoning Ordinance:
•	 Conserve and protect valuable natural resources 
of recreational and scenic areas in and adjacent 
to Guajome Regional Park.

Local Coastal Program (1986), Coastal 
Hazard Vulnerability Assessment, 
and Coastal Hazards Adaptation Plan 
(2018)
The California Coastal Act, passed in 1976, 
requires coastal jurisdictions to adopt a Local 
Coastal Program (LCP) to ensure local implemen-
tation of Coastal Act priorities. 
The City’s LCP, certified by the CCC in 1986, serves 
as the governing land use document for the City’s 
Coastal Zone. In addition to land use, Oceanside’s 
LCP also addresses coastal access, recreational 
opportunities, environmental resources, visual 
quality, infrastructure, and coastal hazards. The 
City is pursuing a comprehensive LCP update. The 
update will devote considerable attention to beach 
loss and the City’s vulnerability to coastal hazards, 
including sea level rise, extreme tidal events, and 
coastal storms.
Oceanside’s Coastal Zone extends to properties 
on the inland side of Coast Highway and farther 
inland along the San Luis Rey River, Loma Alta 
Creek, and Buena Vista Lagoon. Portions of the 
City’s Downtown District lie within the Coastal 
Zone. The Coastal Zone boundary is depicted in 
Figure 3-1. The Coastal Zone includes a wide range 
of uses, including a variety of housing types, neigh-
borhood and visitor-serving commercial uses, and 
a host of public amenities (e.g., Oceanside Small 
Craft Harbor, Oceanside Municipal Pier). The 
Healthy and Livable Community Element of the 
General Plan (HLC) includes goals and policies for 
the operation and maintenance of public ameni-
ties within the Coastal Zone. 
Where the LCP is silent, pertinent General Plan 
goals, policies, and actions apply within the 
Coastal Zone.
In 2018, the City performed a Vulnerability 
Assessment to address existing conditions and 
future vulnerability of Oceanside’s social, eco-
nomic, and physical coastal resources to project-
ed sea-level rise, coastal flooding, and erosion. 
Informed by this assessment, Oceanside produced 
an administrative draft of the Coastal Hazards 
Adaptation Plan (AP). The AP evaluates the feasi-
bility and efficacy of each of these strategies, high-
lighting associated tradeoffs. The draft AP also 
identifies potential triggers that would provoke 
the City to reevaluate and/or implement adapta-
tion strategies, such as beach widths, extent of sea 
level rise, and others, in addition to establishment 
of benchmarks, monitoring, and documentation 
protocols. Findings of the assessment are reflect-
ed in a variety of planning documents.

Loma Alta Creek Watershed

SPECIFIC PLANS
San Luis Rey River Specific Plan 
The Specific Plan addresses development for the 
San Luis Rey River and vicinity in the downtown, 
coastal area. The San Luis Rey River and the sur-
rounding vacant parcels are the last major portions 
of undeveloped land in Oceanside’s Coastal Zone. 
The plan is intended to provide a comprehensive 
land use plan for the area which will: a) become 
an adjunct and refinement to the City’s General 
Plan, intended to coordinate planning activities 
of the governmental agencies; b) serve as a cer-
tified component of Oceanside’s Local Coastal 
Program; and c) provide management policies and 
development criteria to guide future actions in the 
river area. Since the river is a significant coastal 
resource in terms of its recreational, scenic, and 
wildlife values, the San Luis Rey River Specific Plan 
includes measures to address the conservation 
and wise use of these resources.

Mission San Luis Rey Historic Area and

This report addresses long range goals and objec-

guidelines for detailed proposal review. The guide-

Sterling Property Specific Plan (1988)
The area subject to this specific plan contained 
substandard military housing. In exchange for 
constructing new military housing on MCB Camp 
Pendleton, a developer was chosen to receive the 
Sterling Homes property for redevelopment. The 
Sterling Property Specific Plan was prepared to 
define the basic development parameters for the 
Sterling Homes site including the land use mix and 
density; the primary circulation pattern; the overall 
open space concept and open space requirements; 
and the location and capacity of utilities to be pro-
vided by the developer.   
Specific Plan for the San Luis Rey River 
Flood Control Project (1999)
This Specific Plan is intended to serve as an over-
lay and refinement to the City’s General Plan. The 
plan contains policies that would prohibit develop-
ment in flood prone and environmentally sensitive 
areas. The plan is designed to provide protection 
to both life and property within the 7.2-mile reach 
targeted by the project. 
Downtown Oceanside Master Plan 
(2000)
The Downtown Oceanside Master Plan was pre-
pared to implement the land use policies approved 
by the Coastal Commission as part of LCP-1-91, 
which encourages the development of visitor-serv-
ing commercial facilities and tourist and visitor-ori-
ented accommodations with the Strand Study Area. 
The Land Use Plan further requires that a minimum 
amount of visitor-serving commercial facilities and 
visitor-oriented accommodations be constructed 
within the nine-block area bounded by The Strand, 
Cleveland Street, Seagaze Drive and Civic Center 
Drive (known in the Plan as the MPA).The Master 
Plan applies to a nine-block area and is designed 
to assure a high quality of development on the site, 
consistent with the objectives of the community, the 
Redevelopment Plan and the Local Coastal Program. 
In addition, the Master Plan establishes development 
standards and clear criteria for evaluating proposed 
developments within the MPA. The Masterplan 
consists of standards and guidelines and serves to

3.3	Existing Land Uses
As a city with a population of approximately 
180,000 and 42 square miles of area, Oceanside 
encompasses a diversity of land uses and settings. 
To the west of Interstate 5, an approximately 
half-mile-wide and three-mile-long stretch of 
Oceanside paralleling the Pacific Ocean features a 
traditional development pattern, with short blocks, 
alleyways, and a diverse mix of uses, including a 
concentration of commercial uses along South 
Coast Highway and a range of coastal amenities 
and other public uses. The vast majority of the 
City lies east of Interstate 5. These inland areas 
generally exhibit a dispersed land use pattern, 
dominated by relatively low-density residential 
uses. Single-family residential is the most common 
type of residential development, comprising about 
70 percent of residential uses, and single-family 
neighborhoods are dispersed throughout the City. 
Existing Land Use patterns are shown in Figures 
3-1, 3-2 and Table 3-1.
Multi-family residential uses comprise 19 percent 
of residential uses, with multi-family developments 
bordering the east-west corridors of Highway 76, 
Highway 78, and Mesa Drive, as well as the north-
south corridors of El Camino Real, Rancho del Oro 
Drive, and Old Grove Road. Multi-family housing is 
also prevalent in portions of the coastal area. Rural 
residential uses make up eight percent of residential 
uses and are primarily located in South Morro Hills. 
Mobile homes make up four percent of residential 
land uses and are concentrated near Douglas Drive, 
west of Interstate 5 around Loma Alta Creek, and 
just south of the SOI. Some mobile home parks in 
Oceanside are subject to local rent control.
Commercial uses occupy just three percent of the 
City. Most commercial uses—including retail, lodg-
ing, and offices—are located along major corridors, 
including Coast Highway, Highways 76 and 78, 
and Oceanside Boulevard. Downtown Oceanside, 
which includes the blocks surrounding Mission 
Avenue and Coast Highway, contains a mixture of 
commercial uses, and houses the City’s main clus-
ters of hospitality uses (five percent of commercial 
uses) and mixed uses (two percent of commercial 
uses). Major regional commercial retail centers 
include:
•	 The Camino Town and Country Shopping Center 
and the El Camino North Shopping Center at the 
corner of El Camino Real and Highway 78;
•	 Oceanside Crossing at the corner of Interstate 5 
and Oceanside Boulevard;
•	 Mission Shopping Center near Mission Avenue 
and Interstate 5;
•	 Mission Marketplace near College Boulevard 
and Highway 76;
•	 Tri-City Crossroads;
•	 Pacific Coast Plaza;
•	 Town Center North; and
•	 Plaza Rancho Del Oro.

Residential
Land designated for standalone residential use 
in Oceanside is largely built out – i.e., virtually all 
property designated exclusively for residential use 
in the City has already been developed.  
A large percentage of the City’s land area is ded-
icated to single-family housing – both production 
tract homes and custom homes. Production tract 
housing is predominant in the San Luis Rey Valley, 
the Hwy 78 corridor, and eastern portions of the 
City. Custom homes are predominant in the coast-
al area and the City’s older neighborhoods (e.g., 
Fire Mountain, Loma Alta, Eastside Capistrano). 
Multifamily housing is dispersed throughout the 
City, with concentrations in the coastal area, east-
ern portions of Fire Mountain, the Los Arbolitos 
area, and along stretches of Canyon Drive, 
Greenbrier Drive, Rancho del Oro Road, Old Grove 
Road, and North River Road.  
The City also includes several mobile home parks, 
with a significant concentration in the San Luis 
Rey neighborhood. Some of the City’s mobile 
home parks are resident-owned (e.g., San Luis Rey 
Mobile Estates), but most are under single owner-
ship. A number of the City’s mobile home parks are 
subject to affordability covenants, providing hous-
ing opportunities for lower-income households 
and people on fixed incomes State law establishes 
procedures for mobile home park closures, including 
a required tenant impact report that assesses the 
consequences of closure. Mobile home park resi-
dents must be provided adequate notice of impend-
ing park closure, fair compensation for mobile homes 
that cannot be relocated, and safe and habitable 
living conditions until the park is legally closed. 
These established residential areas are not expect-
ed to experience significant change over the ECLU 
planning horizon, though it is likely that single-family 
areas will see new accessory dwelling units (ADUs), 
as facilitated by state law. Large-lot single-family 
areas may see new units facilitated by Senate Bill 
(SB) 9, which allows two single-family homes on a 
single-family lot and the subdivision of single-family 
lots to accommodate as many as four single-family 
homes. Land use designations for the City’s residen-
tial areas – most of which were assigned decades ago 
– largely remain the same under this iteration of the 
ECLU.  
The ECLU establishes seven residential land use des-
ignations, most of which carry over from the 1986 
Land Use Element. These designations provide for 
a range of housing types, including single-family 
homes with different minimum lot size requirements, 
medium-density detached and attached housing 
(e.g., twin homes and duplexes), higher-density 
apartments and condominiums, and mobile home

Residential Estate (RE)
The Residential Estate designation is intended to 
provide for very low density single-family housing, 
compatible with the topography and public service 
capacities. Two types of Residential Estate districts 
are established: the Estate A (RE-A) District where 
the maximum potential density is 0.9 dwelling units 
per acre; and the Estate B District (RE-B) where the 
maximum potential density is 3.5 dwelling units per 
acre.   
Single Family Detached Residential  
(SFD-R)
As there are few opportunities for new low-density 
single-family development in Oceanside, the Single 
Family Detached Residential designation primarily 
serves to protect Oceanside’s existing low density 
single-family neighborhoods. This designation allows 
for single family detached homes on properties with 
a minimum lot size of 6,000 square feet. Accessory 
dwelling units and SB 9 units are permitted per state 
regulations. Existing duplex, triplex, and fourplex 
development may remain as a conforming residen-
tial use, though multi-unit development cannot be 
expanded beyond its existing footprint, total square 
footage, or maximum height. SFD-R applies to large 
portions of the City’s existing residential neigh-
borhoods. The Single Family Detached Residential 
designation has a minimum density of 3.5 dwelling 
units per acre and a maximum potential density of 
5.9 dwelling units per acre.
Low-Medium Density Residential     
(LMD-R)
The Low-Medium Density Residential designa-
tion provides for small-lot detached and attached 
single-family dwellings such as twin homes and 
duplexes, as well as clustered multiplex housing. 
Many of the City’s LMD-R districts accommodate 
senior housing. LMD-R neighborhoods often feature 
common landscape areas and recreational facilities. 
Mobile homes and compatible uses such as residen-
tial-care facilities are permitted. The LMD-R desig-
nation has a minimum density of 6.0 dwelling units 
per acre and a maximum potential density of 15.0 
dwelling units per acre.

Medium Density Residential (MD-R)
The Medium Density Residential designation 
is intended to foster a range of housing types 
and supportive residential facilities, including 
single-family detached and attached, low-rise 
multi-family, live-work housing, and group quar-

MD-R designation has a maximum potential densi-

High Density Residential (HD-R)
The High Density Residential designation allows 
for a full range of high-density housing types. Most 
new development under the HD-R designation is 
expected to be low- to mid-rise (generally four 
stories or less) multifamily housing. Single room 
occupancy (SRO) facilities, live-work housing, 
and group quarters (e.g., residential care facilities 
and nursing homes) are also permitted under this 
designation, subject to development standards in 
the Zoning Ordinance. Efficiency units (sharing 
kitchen and common areas with other units) and 
SRO facilities, with building area per housing unit 
of less than 550 square feet, will be counted at 
half the residential density. The HD-R designation 
has a maximum potential density of 40.0 dwelling 
units per acre. 
Urban High Density Residential          
(UHD-R)
The UHD-R designation is applied along major 
streets and in transitional areas between commer-
cial districts and single-family residential neigh-
borhoods. Most UHD-R properties are located 
in and around the Downtown District, transit-ori-
ented sites, and other locations along corridors 
where high density housing can leverage existing 
infrastructure and transit service. This designation 
would permit a range of housing types and occu-
pancies, including multi-family, SRO facilities, live-
work housing, and group quarters (e.g., residential 
facilities and nursing homes), subject to specific 
standards in the Zoning Ordinance. Efficiency 
units (sharing kitchen and common areas with 
other units) and SRO facilities, with building area 
per housing unit less than 550 square feet would 
be counted at half the residential density. The 
UHD-R designation has a minimum density of 30 
dwelling units per acre and a maximum potential 
density of 50.0 dwelling units per acre.

JOBS/HOUSING RATIO
Jobs/housing ratio—or more precisely, jobs/
employed residents’ ratio—is the quantitative rela-
tionship between jobs located within a city and 
residents of the city who are employed, whether 
within the city limits or elsewhere. A ratio of 1.0 
indicates a balance (that is, the number of in- and 
out-commuters is equal); a ratio of less than 1.0 
indicates lower jobs availability relative to the 
city’s available work¬force, while a ratio greater 
than 1.0 indicates a jobs surplus. 
Given employers’ needs for workers with specific 
skills, large numbers of two-income households 
with jobs located in different cities, and potential 
mismatches between housing affordability and 
types of available nearby jobs, even complete 
parity between local jobs and local workers would 
not obviate the need for commuting. Nevertheless, 
a balanced jobs/employed residents’ ratio is gen-
erally associated with benefits such as reduced 
driving and congestion, lowered air pollution emis-
sions, lower costs to businesses and commuters,

lower public expenditures on facilities and ser-
vices, greater family stability, and a higher quality

3.6	Compatible Land 
Use
Like in most cities, development in Oceanside is 
regulated by a Zoning Ordinance that delineates 
allowable land uses. For example, residential dis-
tricts are largely restricted to certain types of 
housing, with regulations controlling lot sizes, set-
backs, and other dimensional standards. While this 
approach to zoning has served to minimize incom-
patibilities between different land uses, it has also 
resulted in isolated residential neighborhoods, a 
surplus of commercial zoning in certain areas, a 
lack of land resources for low-impact industrial 
uses, and the fragmentation of open space and 
sensitive habitat.
By separating residential uses from commercial 
districts, public facilities, transit service, and other 
essential services, traditional zoning has contrib-
uted to Oceanside’s dependence on the private 
automobile and given rise to a roadway network 
that privileges automobiles over pedestrians and 
bicyclists. Furthermore, by restricting or alto-
gether prohibiting small-scale assembly and man-
ufacturing in commercial zones, existing zoning 
has reduced opportunities for such uses and for 
synergies between these uses and retail activity 
(i.e., where small-scale processing and manufac-
turing—such as brewing—can be combined with a 
storefront on the same site).
In an effort to contain further urban sprawl, pro-
mote active transportation, and stabilize the City’s 
commercial sector, the ECLU seeks to facilitate the 
integration of compatible land uses through mixed-

mixed-use developments. The ECLU also seeks to 
expand opportunities for small-scale manufactur-

subject to development standards that render such 
uses compatible with customer-serving environ-
ments. In addition, the City recently liberalized its 
home occupation standards to allow residents to 
conduct a wider range of business activities within 
their homes. In so doing, the City has encouraged 
home businesses to seek business licenses, and, 
in turn, conform to the City’s home-occupation 
standards. 
Of course, integration of land uses that have tra-
ditionally been separated raises questions about 
compatibility and balance. This section of the ECLU 
addresses these questions with goals and policies 
that promote the integration of compatible and 
synergistic uses while requiring adequate buffers 
and other attenuating features between incompat-
ible uses.
As Oceanside continues to grow and develop and 
the land uses in the City evolve in response to 
market conditions and other factors, the ECLU aims 
to ensure continued compatibility between land

MILITARY READINESS
Due to Oceanside's adjacency to Marine Corps 
Base Camp Pendleton, the City is required to 
address the potential impact of new growth on 
military readiness activities. Such activities include 
training, support and operations that prepare men 
and women for combat; operation, maintenance 
and security of the military facility; and testing of 
military equipment, vehicles, weapons, and sen-
sors for combat use. 
While the ECLU focuses on the potential effects of 
land use changes on military activities, other ele-
ments of the General Plan—including the IM, SRE, 
HLC, and VSR—address additional aspects of com-
patibility and adjacency with Camp Pendleton.
Established in 1942, Camp Pendleton is the major 
West Coast base of the United States Marine 
Corps and one of the largest Marine Corps bases 
in the United States. Camp Pendleton extends 
along the entirety of Oceanside’s northern bound-
ary. While much of this boundary is comprised 
of open space, there are housing tracts, public 
facilities, institutional uses, and farmland that abut 
or lie in close proximity to the base. Additionally, 
there are two points of entry into the base located 
in Oceanside: the “main gate” on Harbor Drive and 
the “back gate” on Vandergrift Road.
Camp Pendleton has evolved with Oceanside, pro-
viding on-base housing, commercial venues, med-
ical facilities, recreational facilities, social services, 
and other elements that render the base a largely 
self-sufficient community. Consequently, the base 
has become less dependent on Oceanside and 
other nearby communities.
The proximity of Camp Pendleton benefits 
Oceanside in a number of ways. Camp Pendleton 
supplies many jobs for Oceansiders. Many base 
personnel make their homes in Oceanside, send-
ing their children to local schools, participating 
in community activities, and purchasing local 
goods and services. While Oceanside’s economy 
no longer relies as heavily on consumer demand 
from Camp Pendleton, active-duty military per-
sonnel, military contractors, and military families 
who stay in local hotels, dine in local restaurants, 
and patronize other visitor-serving uses in the 
community support the City’s hospitality sector. 
Additionally, extensive open spaces along Camp

3.7	Mixed-Use Districts
The City’s proposed mixed-use districts are con-
centrated along the three major east-west corri-
dors, with the majority occurring along Mission 
Avenue east of I-5 and between El Camino Real and 
Rancho del Oro, along Oceanside Boulevard east of 
I-5 and surrounding the five Sprinter stations, and 
along Vista Way near the Tri-City medical center. 
Allowing for higher land use intensities than permit-
ted elsewhere in the City, and establishing a mini-
mum required commercial FAR of 0.15 or 0.20, the 
City’s mixed-use districts are envisioned as dense, 
walkable, transit-oriented villages. 
Additionally, the Coast Highway Incentive District, 
adopted by the City Council in 2019 but yet to 
be officially certified in the City’s LCP, promotes 
midrise mixed-use development in portions of the 
Coast Highway corridor extending from Seagaze 
Drive to Morse Street.  The CHID provides for 
additional residential density and building height 
in exchange for specific public benefits (i.e., public 
parking, publicly-accessible open space, commercial 
floor area above minimum standards).  The CHID 
also provides for standalone residential uses in 
certain stretches of the corridor, subject to more 
restrictive development standards.

3.8	Residential Uses 
As of 2023, Oceanside's housing stock is comprised 
of 64 percent single-family housing (detached and 
attached), 31 percent multi-family housing, and five 
percent mobile homes. Over the past five years, 
nearly 70 percent of housing added to the City 
has been multi-family. Despite the overwhelm-
ing demand for housing in the San Diego region 
and rising housing prices throughout the County, 
housing prices in Oceanside have remained rela-
tively affordable compared to County averages. In 
order to maintain Oceanside’s accessible housing 
market and keep up with projected growth in the 
County, Oceanside must add a significant number 
of housing units over the Planning horizon. The 
City must also ensure that new housing develop-
ments include a diversity of housing options and 
levels of affordability, including income-restricted 
affordable housing.
With very little undeveloped vacant land remaining 
in the City, much of the residential growth project-
ed for the City is envisioned as infill development 
along the City’s major corridors and Downtown, 
preserving the City’s existing single-family neigh-
borhoods to the extent possible within the frame-
work of the State’s evolving housing laws. 
Within both single-family and multi-family resi-
dential districts, residents have expressed desires 
for greater connectivity and easier access to 
amenities and essential services. In response, this 
General Plan provides greater flexibility for allow-
ing small scale residentially compatible commercial 
uses at major intersections and other focal points 
in residential neighborhoods. Improvements to 
pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure within res-
idential districts will also help reduce the City’s 
reliance on single occupancy vehicles, reducing 
traffic and harmful emissions. Bicycle and pedes-
trian improvements are discussed in further detail 
in the Integrated Mobility (IM) element.

3.9	Commercial Uses 
Oceanside’s commercial areas are largely concen-
trated in Downtown, the Coast Highway corridor, 
and along the City’s three main east-west corri-
dors. Vista Way houses the largest concentration 
of regional commercial uses, while in recent years 
Downtown has seen an increasing number of 
hotels. While these areas will continue to see new 
commercial uses and replacement and refurbish-
ment of existing ones, new commercial develop-
ment is also anticipated as part of the El Corazon 
Specific Plan, which includes an arena, hotels, and 
commercial uses. 
Many existing commercial developments have low 
FARs and large parking lots, catering to patrons 
who arrive by car. While commercial develop-
ments will continue to accommodate cars for the 
foreseeable future, improvements in parking effi-
ciency and a shift away from reliance on single-oc-
cupancy vehicles may reduce the need for surface 
parking lots. A reduction in the need for surface 
parking will open up land area for intensification 
of existing commercial uses or infill mixed-use 
developments. More patrons accessing commer-
cial areas without cars will necessitate safe and 
attractive pedestrian connections, connections to 
transit hubs, and active transportation amenities. 
The emphasis on pedestrian and bicycle connec-
tivity also underscores a commitment to sustain-
able transportation and community interaction.
The City's strategy for commercial uses emphasiz-
es innovation, inclusivity, and integration within 
the community. By prioritizing high employment 
density uses and streamlining business entry 
processes, the City will encourage dynamic eco-
nomic centers that cater to diverse needs and 
foster job growth. This includes accommodating 
emerging trends like e-commerce and promoting 
spaces that foster a culture of innovation and 
entrepreneurship.
Ensuring balanced and synergistic coexistence 
between commercial and residential areas is 
another core focus of the Plan. It will be import-
ant to implement thoughtful controls on elements 
like lighting and signage and to mandate landscape 
buffer requirements that promote land use transi-
tions and compatibility.

3.10	 Industrial Uses 
Industrial uses provide high quality jobs and eco-
nomic benefits for the City. While the amount of 
industrial land is modest compared to some sur-
rounding communities, the City has seen an influx 
of high-technology and bio-technology industrial 
and research and development uses in the past 
two decades. The City also has clusters of older 
industrial uses, particularly south of Oceanside 
Boulevard. 
The City's approach to industrial development is 
defined by a commitment to balancing the asso-
ciated economic benefits with the well-being 
of the community. Encouraging lower impact, 
higher employment density industrial uses such 
as research and development maximizes the job 
and economic benefits while minimizing impacts 
to surrounding communities. Implementing noise 
abatement measures, limiting outdoor storage vis-
ibility, and promoting enclosed industrial activities 
will improve compatibility with surrounding use 
for all industrial uses. Flexible parking standards, 
vertical space utilization, and enhanced design in 
new industrial developments will accommodate 
higher density activities without increasing the 
negative impacts of those uses. Active transpor-
tation amenities, supporting services, and food 
options to cater to the needs of industrial work-
ers will improve the quality of life within industrial 
districts. Through these strategies, the City seeks 
to cultivate a robust industrial landscape that not 
only drives economic prosperity but also contrib-
utes to a visually appealing, harmonious, and inclu-
sive urban environment.

3.11	 Development 
Review Process
The development review process is a means of 
implementing the land use and development pri-
orities outlined in this General Plan. The devel-
opment review process should be transparent, 
consistent, efficient, and manageable. This process 
should encourage civic engagement and provide 
an opportunity for stakeholders and decision-mak-
ers to reflect upon the City’s vision for its future. 
Robust public notification, access to information, 
and multiple means of providing input are all fun-
damental to the decision-making process. 
At the same time, for projects that accord with and 
advance the City’s vision, comply with applicable 
standards, and avoid and/or mitigate potentially 
significant impacts, the development review pro-
cess should generally be as predictable and expe-
ditious as possible. Streamlined development is 
also required by the State for certain housing proj-
ects. A streamlined development review process 
will help to free up the City’s staffing resources 
for other purposes, bring much needed housing 
and jobs-producing uses into operation in a more 
timely manner, reduce costs that are inevitably 
passed on to end users (i.e., homebuyers and

tenants), and allow the City to more effectively 
plan for and implement capital improvements and

Objective development and design standards, 
supplemented by graphics that clearly illustrate 
the type, character, and quality of development 
required and desired by the City, will streamline 
the development review process without compro-
mising its integrity. 
For projects subject to environmental review 
under the California Environmental Quality Act 
(CEQA), the environmental review process can 
be streamlined through programmatic environ-
mental impact reports (PEIRs) that proactively 
address the potential cumulative impacts of future 
development within specified areas, and, in turn, 
outline specific conditions of approval and/or mit-
igation measures that future projects must imple-
ment to avoid and/or minimize such impacts. This 
General Plan is supported by a PEIR that is meant 
to streamline environmental review for projects 
that demonstrate consistency with the General 
Plan and accompanying Smart and Sustainable 
Corridors Plan, abide by all applicable conditions of 
approval, and implement all applicable mitigation 
measures. Certain types of development within 
other specific plan areas are also exempt from 
CEQA review. Additionally, CEQA statutes and 
guidelines promote "smart growth” within already 
urbanized areas by exempting most infill and rede-
velopment projects from environmental review. In 
this way, CEQA serves to direct new development 
away from open space, farmland, and sensitive 
habitat and toward areas where new development 
can, for the most part, be served by existing infra-
structure and public services. Thus, the City can 
leverage CEQA to facilitate development that pre-
serves open space, biological resources, and farm-
land while reducing vehicle miles traveled. 
Through efficient entitlement and environmental 
review processes focused on the guiding princi-
ples of the General Plan, the City seeks to enable 
growth that contributes to community identity, 
civic engagement, environmental quality, and 
effective governance.
