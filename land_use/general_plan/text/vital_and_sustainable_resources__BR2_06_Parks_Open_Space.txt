EXISTING CONDITIONS REPORT
The Parks and Recreational Open Space Chapter 
of the Existing Conditions Report gives an over­
view of existing and planned parks and recre­
ation facilities, as well as relevant parks and 
recreational standards.
6.1	Existing Parks 
and Recreational 
Amenities
EXISTING PARKS
The City of Oceanside Parks and Recreation 
Division provides park and recreation services 
throughout the City.  The City currently has 
approximately 642 acres of park land.  As of 
2020, the City’s parks and recreation facilities 
consist of 15 community and 17 neighborhood 
parks, one regional park, three recreation 
centers (Junior Seau Community Center, Joe 
Balderamma Recreation Center, and Melba 
Bishop Recreation Center), a YMCA and Boys 
and Girls Club, two senior centers, five skate 
parks, and two pools. Residents can also enjoy 
more than 115 acres of school play areas as pro­
vided through Memorandums of Understanding 
(MOUs) with the Oceanside Unified School 
District. Other facilities, including Oceanside’s 
3.5 miles of beach, harbor and  pier, and addi­
tional community amenities are described in 
more detail in Chapter 7: Public Services. 
In November 2019, the City of Oceanside 
adopted the Parks and Recreation Master Plan 
(Master Plan). The Master Plan provides a snap­
shot of park and recreation conditions as of 2019 
and, coupled with an assessment of community 
needs and priorities and a discussion of trends 
in park and recreation now and in the future, is 
intended to develop a vision for the parks and 
recreation system through 2030. 
The Master Plan defines five major categories 
of park types: Neighborhood Parks, Community 
Parks, Community Centers, Regional Parks, and 
Special Use Parks.
•	 Neighborhood Parks are generally smaller 
parks that provide both passive and limited 
active recreation but tend to focus on passive 
recreation. They are typically less than five 
acres in size and serve nearby residents within 
a 15-minute walkshed. They generally do not 
include Citywide facilities, such as gyms, pools, 
or sports fields.
•	 Community Parks serve daily recreational 
needs of the community as well as the local 
broader neighborhood. They are generally 
larger than five acres in size and service an 
area within a 5-minute driveshed. Citywide 
sports fields, pools, and court sports are 
concentrated in these locations.

RECREATIONAL AMENITIES
In addition to parks, the City of Oceanside con­
tains a wide variety of recreational facilities and 
opportunities, including beaches, parks, and 
community centers. 
Oceanside’s trail system and trail planning is dis­
cussed in Background Report #4, Mobility. 
Beaches
Oceanside contains three miles of beaches, 
including Oceanside City Beach, Harbor Beach, 
Breakwater Way, Oceanside Boulevard Beach, 
Wisconsin Street Beach, Tyson Street Beach and 
Park, and Buccaneer Beach.
Collectively, these beaches provide access to 
a wide variety of recreational opportunities 
including surfing, picnicking, and volleyball, and 
include amenities such as tables, barbeques, 
restrooms, parks, and playgrounds.
Related attractions include the Oceanside Pier 
and Small Craft Harbor. Constructed in 1888 and 
located at Oceanside City Beach, the Oceanside 
Pier plays host to a variety of beachside activities 
including concerts, performances, and surf com­
petitions. The Harbor, located at Harbor Beach, 
provides access to water activities including 
deep-sea fishing, whale watching cruises, and 
boat rentals. 
Guajome Regional Park
Guajome Regional Park is owned and operated 
by the County of San Diego. Among the ameni­
ties provided are campgrounds, children’s play 
areas, day use picnic areas, trails, natural areas, 
and tent and RV campsites.
Buena Vista Audubon Society Nature 
Center
Established by the Buena Vista Audubon Society 
in 1987 and renovated in 2014, the Nature Center 
is a 3,500 square foot facility that houses a series 
of interpretative displays featuring local wildlife, 
as well as a natural history library, gift shop, and 
meeting rooms. The Nature Center hosts nature-
themed events for all ages.
Golf Courses
Oceanside is home to six golf courses: Goat 
Hill Park, Emerald Isle Golf Course, Arrowood 
Golf Course, Oceanside Municipal Golf Course, 
El Camino Country Club, and Camp Pendleton 
Memorial Golf Course.

Camp Pendleton
Located to the north of Oceanside, Camp 
Pendleton provides a very wide variety of rec­
reation facilities which are available to military 
personnel and staff only. These facilities within 
Camp Pendleton are neither controlled, influ­
enced, nor programmed by the City. There are 
members of the Oceanside community that are 
attached to Camp Pendleton as military person­
nel or staff, and while it is true that the facilities 
at Camp Pendleton are available to them, it is 
most likely that their recreation needs are best 
met by local facilities near their residences, 
schools, and friends. Recreation facilities at 
Camp Pendleton are not credited toward facility 
and acreage goals for Oceanside residents.
Ivey Ranch Equestrian Park
The Ivey Ranch is located within the City of 
Oceanside’s Historic Mission San Luis Rey. The 
Ranch, managed by nonprofit Oceanside Ivey 
Ranch Park Association, offers a variety of 
equestrian programs including riding programs, 
group events, school programs, summer camp, 
and childcare. The City retains ownership of the 
land and the Association is responsible for devel­
oping, administering, and maintaining the parks. 
The Association is also responsible for the finan­
cial elements of the park’s development and 
day-to-day operations. Originally constructed in 
1889, the house and surrounding grounds now 
serves as an equestrian center and garden that 
specializes in making equestrian activities acces­
sible to people of all walks of life.
Ivey Ranch underwent an upgrade and expan­
sion of some facilities in 2003. Upgrades to the 
equestrian center included enhanced accessibil­
ity features, a new Tack and Feed Room, and a 
new Hay Barn. One of Oceanside’s community 
gardens is located on the Ranch. Forty spaces 
are available to rent for families and senior cit­
izens who would otherwise not have the oppor­
tunity to cultivate a garden. Plot rental includes 
the use of the parcel, water, and access to the 
community storage shed. 
Public School Recreation Facilities
Within City boundaries, Oceanside children are 
served by five schools in the Vista Unified School 
District and 22 schools in the Oceanside Unified 
School District. Joint use agreements with each 
of the two unified school districts currently 
allows the City to program, use, and/or make 
available school facilities to a certain degree 
during non-school hours. Most of the Oceanside 
sports organizations utilize school fields for 
practice and games often scheduled by the City.
Recreation Centers
The City of Oceanside has three recreation 
centers: Junior Seau Beach Community Center, 
Jose Balderrama Recreation Center, and Melba 
Bishop Recreation Center. These recreation cen­
ters are designed to meet Oceanside’s diverse 
recreational needs and include features such as 
gymnasiums, meeting rooms, kitchens and host 
activities such as fitness classes, teen programs, 
childcare, sports activities, and special events.

6.2	Planned Parks
EL CORAZON PARK
El Corazon is a City-owned parcel of 465 contig­
uous acres located in the center of Oceanside, 
bounded by Rancho Del Oro Drive on the east, 
Oceanside Boulevard on the south, El Camino 
Real on the west, and Mesa Drive on the north. 
An ongoing planning process began in 2003, and 
in 2009, the El Corazon Specific Plan was adopted 
to guide and implement the vision for the area. 
The City is slated to eventually develop the site 
to contain 212 acres of parks and recreation, 164 
acres of habitat, 34 acres of civic services, 25 
acres of commercial, 19 acres of village commer­
cial, and 11 acres of hotel.
6.3	Park Standards and 
Provision 
PARKLAND STANDARDS
The City of Oceanside’s Parks Master Plan defines 
the level of service in terms of both the parkland 
acreage available by population and the park 
amenities available by population. The acreage 
standards reference the existing General Plan 
acreage standards of 5 acres per 1,000 people. 
The recreation facility metrics were developed 
based on national and regional standards but 
were tailored to the City of Oceanside based on 
input and local demographics.1  Both the acreage

parks - including Regional, Community, Special

PARKLAND ACCESSIBILITY
The Master Plan endeavors to ensure that parks 
are evenly distributed throughout the City by 
identifying locations that are currently park-defi­
cient, as measured by a 15-minute walkshed for 
Neighborhood Parks and a 5-minute driveshed 
for Community Parks and special facilities. 
Given current population growth trends and the 
location of existing facilities, about 24 percent 
of residents are served by the 15-minute walk­
shed for Neighborhood Parks and 87 percent 
of the community are served by the 5-minute 
driveshed for Community Parks, community 
centers, Special Use Parks, and Regional Parks. 
Many of the single family residential areas lack 
walkshed access to neighborhood parks due 
to the centralized location of parks and the 
non-pedestrian oriented streetscape design. 
Access to parks by driving is higher but does not 
account for Oceanside residents who are unable 
or unwilling to drive to reach their Community 
Parks.
The Master Plan offers detailed recommenda­
tions for reducing parkland access disparities. In 
order to reduce the shortage of parks and rec­
reation facilities in some of Oceanside’s neigh­
borhoods, and in order to close some of the geo­
graphic gaps, parks with new facilities should be 
added in these areas when possible. The Master 
Plan identifies three general locations for new 
parks: Area A, B, and C, as shown in Figure 6-1. 
Area A is located north of Route 78, east of El 
Camino Real, and south of Oceanside Boulevard. 
Area B is located south of Mesa Drive, North of 
Oceanside Boulevard, east of College Drive, and 
west of Melrose Drive. Area C is located south of 
Route 76 and east of Melrose Drive. The Master 
Plan recommends that new community parks 
and recreation facilities be centered in areas 
that are currently underserved and/or that are 
expected to see higher density development, 
especially near developments that meet the cri­
teria for density incentives for low- and moder­
ate-income housing.
6.4	Recent Trends in 
Park Usage
The Master Plan identifies several demographic 
and behavioral and cultural trends that have 
influenced how the Oceanside community 
makes use of its park and recreational facilities 
over the past several years. These broad trends 
include a focus on walking, hiking, and camping; 
shifting participation in team sports activities; 
and increased interest in swimming, visiting the 
beach, and other aquatic activities. According to 
the 2018 Sports, Fitness, and Leisure Activities 
Topline Participation Report, visiting the beach 
was the most popular outdoor recreation activ­
ity in Oceanside.
Demographic shifts are one driver of changing 
rates of participation in various recreational 
activities. Millennials (approximately 25 percent 
of the Oceanside population) tend to be the 
most physically active, followed by Generation 
X (20 percent) and Baby Boomers (22 percent). 
Baby Boomers prefer low impact fitness activi­
ties such as swimming, cycling, aquatic exercise, 
and walking. The spatial distribution of different 
age cohorts should be taken into account when 
determining the location of new and enhanced 
park amenities. The City of Oceanside’s elderly 
(age 65 years or older) population is gener­
ally concentrated in the Ocean Hills, Lake, and 
Oceana neighborhoods. The City’s youth pop­
ulation (under 18 years of age) is more widely 
distributed, with most neighborhoods including 
about 15 to 25 percent youth. However, there 
are particularly high youth densities in the East 
Capistrano, Airport, and Tri-City neighborhoods.

interest in more community events, fitness and 
wellness programs, and outdoor recreation

Another factor that may influence park priori­
ties in the future are the effects of the COVID 19 
pandemic on how people recreate and use park 
facilities. During the pandemic, indoor activities 
that present high risk of infection have served 
to emphasize the importance of outdoor public 
space for recreation, mental health, and safe 
social connection as critical City infrastructure. 
According to a December 2020 Citywide survey, 
respondents indicated improved trails and 
open space, as well as more outdoor recreation 
programs as a high priority, perhaps in light of 
desire to recreate more actively during COVID. 
While parks and open space usage will continue 
to evolve in the short term, the considerations of 
use flexibility, adequate outdoor space, and park 
access will continue to remain critical issues.

6.5	Key Planning Issues 
and Implications
•	 Unequal distribution and poor connections 
to some existing parks and recreation 
facilities. The City of Oceanside currently 
defines park-deficient neighborhoods as 
those  outside a 15-minute walkshed for 
Neighborhood Parks and/or a 5-minute 
driveshed for Community Parks. While most 
Oceanside neighborhoods are accessible by 
these standards, areas remain where access 
is an issue. These areas should be prioritized 
for new park development and improving park 
connectivity. 
Additionally, as the City looks ahead to the 
future, it will seek to accommodate much of its 
new housing development along the corridors. 
There may be a need to provide additional 
parkland in closer walking distance to the new 
residents, as well as improved linkages to com­
munity parks, open space, and trail systems. 
•	 Need to maintain, support and park 
infrastructure and amenities. Perception 
of public park facilities as being “unsafe” 
remains a key issue for residents. Given the 
fact that the City of Oceanside contains little 
undeveloped space in which to construct a 
new park, improved park programming, park 
access, and maintenance can help people feel 
safer using parks and recreation facilities and 
increase utilization rates. New buildings near 
parks could also include design guidelines 
or techniques that increase natural public 
surveillance and support increased feelings of 
safety.
•	 Future park amenity planning. As the City 
makes decisions about which amenities to add 
to parks, it is imperative to keep in mind that 
the City’s demographics, as well as relative 
interest in different recreational activities, will 
continue to shift over time. Park facilities must 
therefore be flexible, adaptable, and able to 
serve the needs of people of a variety of ages 
and walks of life. In light of COVID-19, future 
parks and trails should offer flexibility and 
ample space for people to recreate actively 
and safely. Prioritizing a complete trail system 
and improving connections between parks can 
also help to create active infrastructure as well 
as improve park accessibility. 
•	 Park gaps and funding. Areas lacking 
parks in close walking or driving proximity 
should be a priority. One approach to new 
park development is for the City, following 
the Quimby Act, to continue to condition 
development projects to build parks (rather 
than accept in-lieu fees) if the development is 
located in an area that is underserved by parks 
geographically and/or if it is underserved in 
terms of facilities.
