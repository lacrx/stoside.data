7.1	 Public Facilities and 
Services
SCHOOL DISTRICTS
The City of Oceanside does not provide or 
manage schools, or enrollment, or plan for 
future school facilities. The purpose of address-
ing school facilities as part of the General Plan 
Update process is to ensure that need for future 
facilities is considered as part of evaluating 
population projections. Changes in demand 
for school facilities would result not only from 
new population in new housing, but also from 
changes in population demographics and result-
ing change in school-age population cohorts.  
Oceanside students in kindergarten through 
12th grade are served by the Oceanside Unified 
School District (OUSD), the Vista Unified School 
District (VUSD), the Bonsall Unified School 
District (BUSD), the Carlsbad Unified School 
District (CUSD), and a variety of parochial and 
secular private schools. 
OUSD operates 23 schools: 16 elementary 
schools (three of which are located on Camp 
Pendleton), four middle schools, two compre-
hensive high schools, and one alternative high 
school. These facilities are shown in Figure 7-1. 
Oceanside students may also attend BUSD, 
CUSD, or VUSD just outside the City. The VUSD 
serves Oceanside students at four elementary 
schools, two middle schools, and one high 
school. The BUSD operates one elementary 
school in Oceanside. The CUSD does not have 
facilities in Oceanside but does serve Oceanside 
residents. Both the OUSD and VUSD operate 
adult education programs.  
Located in the City’s Tri-City Neighborhood 
Planning Area, MiraCosta Community College 
has an enrollment of approximately 10,000 
students and offers a range of degree and cer-
tificate programs. The community college also 
offers a wide range of job training programs for 
veterans, active duty military, military spouses, 
and the unemployed. For local employers, Mira 
Costa offers professional development and tech-
nical training courses through its Technology 
Career Institute. Vocational schools in Oceanside 
include California Career School and the 
Healthcare Academy of California.

DISTRICT CAPACITY
As of the 2019-2020 school year, OUSD served 
a student body of 19,371 students. The stu-
dent-teacher ratio for students in kindergarten 
through third grade was 24:1; the ratio was 34:1 
for students in kindergarten through eighth 
grade, 32:1 for middle school students, and 38:1 
for high school students.
According to the State’s eligibility standards, 
OUSD has 747 permanent and 242 portable 
classrooms, with a total capacity of 23,852 stu-
dents. According to the standards defined by the 
District, the District has 619 permanent and 187 
portable classrooms.
As shown by Figure 7-1, VUSD, BUSD, and CUSD 
all serve smaller areas of Oceanside than OUSD. 
VUSD is experiencing a decline in enrollment 
with an annual loss of over 429 students a 
year projected through 2026. BUSD enroll-
ment for the 2019-20 school year is also down 
slightly from the 2017-18 enrollment, but by an 
amount that may be within normal fluctuations. 
Contrastingly, CUSD enrollment has increased 
slightly in recent years, expanding from 11,077 in 
the 2015-16 school year to 11,481 in the 2019-20 
school year.

This decline in school-age population is reflected 
in demographic shifts happening within the 
City of Oceanside as a whole. Between 2010 
and 2015, the average age of the Oceanside 
population has increased slightly. Within the 
OUSD boundary, those 45 years of age or older 
increased from 30.9 to 32.0 percent of the popu-

younger decreased from 56.7 to 54.9 percent. 
Within the District’s sphere of influence, the

has responded to declines in school enrollment 
by consolidating some school facilities, such as 
alternative education programs.
TRENDS IN SCHOOL DESIGN
Several trends in education may affect the dis-
trict’s priorities and preference with regard to 
the design of future schools and classrooms, 
as well as the delivery of educational services. 
Desires to increase the personalization of 
education, promote collaboration, emphasize 
project-based learning, and allow distance 
learning will require that school designers think 
carefully about how technology is integrated 
into the classroom and whether schools and 
classrooms are designed to promote flexibility 
and engagement. Specifically, shifting priorities 
in the structure and delivery of education may 
favor an increase in individual and small group 
collaborative work (as opposed to direct, whole-
class instruction); flexible and non-traditional 
schedules; mobile walls and furniture; demands 
for wireless and bandwidth capacity, device 
charging and storage areas; and a variety of 
spaces that support both traditional education 
and project- or lab-based learning.
Sustainable approaches to school design con-
sider factors such as building durability, waste 
reduction, and energy efficiency. The OUSD has 
implemented explicit daylight harvesting strat-
egies in many of its buildings, installing its first 
solar tubes in 2004. On-site energy generation 
is expected to play an increasingly pivotal role 
in allowing the State to meet Zero Net Energy 
facility requirements by 2030. Currently, solar 
is the only technology capable of providing suf-
ficient energy to power a school campus. Most 
school solar consists of freestanding panels, 
often mounted over parking lots. Such systems 
placed in playgrounds or near classroom build-
ings could double as shade covers.

7.2	Police Service
The Oceanside Police Department is authorized 
226 sworn officers and 88 non-sworn employ-
ees, and a volunteer program consisting of youth 
police explorers and senior police patrol mem-
bers. The City currently possesses six resource 
centers, shown on Figure 7-2. Five of the six 
resource centers are staffed by volunteers 
while the Downtown Resource Center is utilized 
by police officers.  The volunteers stationed at 
the resource centers assist community mem-
bers with preparing crime reports and other 
police-related functions.  The Police Department 
operates the Downtown Resource Center and 
the Beach Center (seasonally) while the Housing 
and Neighborhood Services Department oper-
ates the other four centers. 
The Police Department responds to over 
75,000 calls each year. In Oceanside, rates of 
violent crime have generally been declining in 
Oceanside since 1999. Between 2014 and 2018, 
crime rates declined by approximately 7 percent 
per year. In 2018, the crime rate was 376.98 per 
100,000 people, a 3.95 decline from 2017 .
The Oceanside Police Department was estab-
lished in 1906 with the implementation of 
Ordinance 171. The location of the police sta-
tion has changed several times, from the City’s 
Marshal’s Office on Coast Highway to its current 
location at 3855 Mission Avenue. 
The Police Department conducted a needs 
assessment in 2007 which identified needs for 
an additional 100,000 square feet of police facil-
ities. Since the publication of the 2007 needs 
assessment, the Police Department has acquired 
more equipment and personnel. As of 2020, an 
RFP had been issued for a new facility needs 
assessment. The Police Department has a goal 
to maintains a response time standard of five 
minutes for all Priority I and II emergency ser-
vice calls.  In 2020, the average response time for 
Priority 1 calls was 6.5 minutes.
7.3	 Fire Service
The Oceanside Fire Department (OFD) provides 
service to the community with eight fire stations, 
staffed with personnel on eight front line fire 
engines, one ladder truck, five ambulances, a 
reserve fleet, as well as other specialty apparatus 
and support equipment.. Fire stations are shown 
on Figure 7-2. Fire Department staff consist of 
259 total personnel, 144 full-time fire personnel 
and 115 part-time fire personnel.  Fire Operations 
consists of daily staffing of 34 firefighting per-
sonnel and nine EMT ambulance personnel on 
24 hour shifts.  Lifeguards operate with seven 
full-time and 87 part-time lifeguard personnel. 
Fire Executive Management, Administration, 
Community Risk Reduction, Training and sup-
port services makes up the remainder of the fire 
department workforce with 32 full-time, part-
time and contract personnel.  
The 1990 General Plan recommends that the Fire 
Department  achieve National Fire Protection 
Association standard staffing levels of four people 
per fire suppression company.  While staffing 
levels are adequate to achieve a minimally locally 
desirable Insurance Service Office (ISO) rating, 
the Fire Department’s goal is to achieve the 
highest class to ensure community safety and 
lower insurance costs. An ISO rating calculates 
how well-equipped fire departments are to put 
out fires in that community. The ISO fire score is 
provided to homeowners’ insurance companies 
to help set homeowners insurance rates. An ISO 
fire insurance rating is a score from 1 to 10 that 
indicates how well-protected the community is 
by the fire department. In the ISO rating scale, 
a lower number is better: 1 is the best possible 
rating, while a 10 means the fire department did 
not meet the ISO’s minimum requirements. OFD’s 
2020 ISO rating is class 2/2x (depending on dis-
tance from facilities).  Achieving a Class 1 Rating is 
the Fire Department’s goal.

The majority of calls handled by the OFD are 
medical in nature. In 2020, the OFD responded 
at 20,754 incidents, including 443 fires, 13,824 
emergency medical situations, and 145 haz-
ardous conditions investigations. The neigh-
borhoods of Morro Hills, Capistrano, Crown 
Heights, and the Airport have typically reported 
the lowest number of incidents. North Valley and 
San Luis Rey have typically had higher demand 
for emergency services. An analysis of fire inci-
dent density has also revealed that the vast 
majority of fire demand is located in the west-
ern portion of Oceanside near the waterfront. 
The OFD has reported that it receives the higher 
concentrations of medical calls related to an 
aging populace in areas such as the Ocean Hills 
neighborhoods, mobile home parks, and senior 
communities. 
The City of Oceanside participates in Automatic 
Aid Agreements within San Diego County. 
and is a participant in the statewide Mutual 
Aid Agreement. This allows the OFD to pro-
vide and receive all-risk assistance across the 
State of California. With Automatic Aid, the Fire 
Department and its neighbors operate using the 
principle of closest available resource regard-
less of jurisdictional boundary. This improves 
response times and service to the community. 
Furthermore, participation in this program helps 
provide adequate fire station service area cov-
erage and response times to all portions of the 
City of Oceanside.
The Oceanside Lifeguard Division is an opera-
tional component under Fire Department man-
agement. In 2020, even with the reduced beach 
attendance due to the COVID19 pandemic, the 
Lifeguard Division responded to more than 1,600 
Oceans Rescues, over 3,400 medical aids, 16 
boat rescues, 18 sea life stranding, and numer-
ous other public contacts.
In 2012 the City commissioned a Standard of 
Cover study by Tri-Data. A wide range of defi-
ciencies to include response times, operations 
and administration staffing, the number of fire 
stations and apparatus in service were identi-
fied. Some, but not all of the recommendations 
found in the 2012 report have been addressed.
NFPA 1710, the Standard for the Organization 
and Deployment of Fire Suppression Operations, 
Emergency Medical Operations, and Special 
Operations to the Public by Career Fire 
Departments recommends that the first due 
response for all emergencies be six minutes or 
less 90% of the time with apparatus situated to 
facilitate a drive time of four minutes or less. 
In 2005 the City Council adopted a Resolution 
establishing a five-minute response time, 90% of 
the time for “Priority 1” level Fire and EMS calls 
in the City.
The City has found that it is becoming increas-
ingly difficult to meet this response time stan-
dard within the downtown core and select 
portions of the City. As development occurs 
in previously undeveloped or rural areas, 
new fire and/or medical facilities will need to 
be addressed. As redevelopment occurs that 
increases density (as with infill or increasing 
occupant loads), enhanced resources will need to 
be addressed. Examples of enhanced resources 
include additional vehicles and personnel at a 
fire station, and/or additional facilities.
Modern best practices require a  standard of 
cover survey that utilizes a community-based 
risk assessment.  A community-based risk 
assessment Is an analytical process of iden-
tifying and quantifying key factors within the 
community, that when combined, define risk in 
a way that can be compared to OFD’s response 
capability.  It Is this comparison that provides 
a valuable strategic planning and resources 
deployment tool for the OFD.  An updated study 
should be conducted to account for City growth 
in population and housing density.

7.4	 Libraries
The City’s library system, the Oceanside Public 
Library, consists of two established libraries: 
Civic Center Library (at 330 N Coast Highway, 
measuring 36, 315 square feet) and Mission 
Branch Library (at 3861 Mission Avenue, measur-
ing 12,260 square feet);  and Oceanside READS 
Learning Center (at 804 Pier View Way, in leased 
space measuring 2, 295 square feet), which pro-
vides literacy tutoring and related services for 
adults. These facilities are shown on Figure 7-3. 
The Library also provides two Mobile Libraries, 
vehicles that deliver library services and mate-
rials to Oceanside residents in underserved 
neighborhoods.
The Oceanside Public Library offers a variety 
of materials and services, including DVDs; CDs; 
audio books; eBooks; public computers with 
internet access; wi-fi; printing, faxing, scanning, 
and copying services; private study rooms; and 
special collections.  In 2019, the Library offered 
over 1,700 programs for all ages including author 
talks, concerts, and cultural events, for a com-
bined attendance of over 45,000. That year over 
510,000 library visits were made; over 10,000 
library cards were issued, maintaining a total 
of over 60,000 active users, and over, and over 
450,000 items were circulated, including e-books 
and other e-content.
Since fall 2019, the Oceanside Public Library 
system has become more tightly integrated with 
local schools, allowing Oceanside Unified School 
District students to use their student IDs to 
access Library materials.
The current General Plan, approved in 1990, 
stated a goal, based on standards or studies 
from prior decades, of providing 0.55 square 
feet of library facilities per resident and three 
staff- one librarian and two clerical staff- per 
6,000 residents. Currently, the Library’s square 
footage is about half of what the plan recom-
mends, and staff levels are about two-thirds of 
the previously recommended level. While these 
numbers do not reflect current library planning 
practices, they do indicate a deficiency in library 
services. The City has tentatively identified El 
Corazon as a potential location for a new library 
or joint library-arts venue, to partly address such 
concerns, and serve an area of critical demand. 
As checkout of Library materials in 2019 totaled 
only 730 fewer items at the Mission Branch 
Library than the Civic Center Library, in a space 
one-third the size, the usage and placement of 
current Library facilities is clearly vital in the 
Mission Branch area, and a facility capable of 
at least that much service must remain in that 
specific neighborhood, if the community’s needs 
are to be met.

OCEANSIDE SMALL CRAFT HARBOR
Oceanside Small Craft Harbor (Harbor) is oper-
ated as a public marina, run by the Oceanside 
Small Craft Harbor District (SCHD). Formed in 
1960, the SCHD is responsible for public marine 
facilities and leasehold improvements asso-
ciated with the north and south basins of the 
Harbor and the adjacent shoreline within the 
City of Oceanside.  SCHD operations are con-
fined to approximately 100 acres associated with 
the Harbor and leased from the City.
The SCHD maintains the Harbor as a high-qual-
ity recreational boating center; a transient boat 
stopover point; and a major patrol, search, and 
rescue base. The Harbor is managed by the 
Harbor and Beaches Division of the Public Works 
Department.  There are also eight police officers 
trained as boat operators and divers stationed 
in the Harbor.
According to a history of the Harbor provided 
by the Oceanside Chamber of Commerce, the 
Harbor was dedicated and formally opened in 
June 1963. in January 1964, ground was broken 
for a fishing village-style shopping center with a 
six-story lighthouse. Richard Helgren founded 
Helgren’s Sportfishing in 1979, and it continues 
to be one of the area’s key attraction, taking 
fishermen and tourists out to sea and on whale 
watching adventures. 
The U.S. Coast Guard opened a new shore facil-
ity in April 1975. The Harbor was the home port 
of the USCG Point Hobart from 1969 to 1999. Its 
purpose was to provide additional security for 
President Nixon when he visited the “Western 
White House” near San Mateo Point. The Point 
Hobart also provided search and rescue ser-
vices. In 1993, the Oceanside Harbor District 
was annexed by the City of Oceanside and all 
District employees became City employees, 
including the Oceanside Harbor Police. in 1999, 
Point Hobart was decommissioned, and the 
Oceanside Harbor Police became the only rescue 
vessels between Dana Point Harbor and Mission 
Bay. Currently, the Oceanside Police Harbor Unit 
remains the primary response vessel for over 37 
miles of coastline, assisting in mutual aid mutual 
aid calls for service from San Mateo to Del Mar.
Today, the Harbor is home to a hotel, several 
restaurants, gift shops, a deli, and an ice cream 
and sweet shop. SCHD facilities include per-
manent and transient moorings, fueling dock, 
fishing pier, launching ramp, and yacht club,. 
The SCHD is also responsible for administering 
leases for the tourist and restaurant facilities. 
The SCHD is dependent to the City of Oceanside; 
the Oceanside City Council acts as the District 
Board of Directors. A permanent Harbor and 
Beaches Advisory Committee advises the Board 
of Directors. District operations and slip rental 
services are overseen by City personnel.. 
The Harbor is one of Oceanside’s many coastal 
features that is vulnerable to coastal hazards, 
projected to become more severe under con-
tinued climate change and sea level rise. In the 
short term, 79 of the 100 acres of the Harbor 
fall within the potential future hazard zones, 
increasing to 86 acres in the mid-term and 93 
acres in the long term (2100 to 2140). Potential 
impacts of the expansion of coastal hazard 
zones include increased frequency of flooding 
and erosion, with associated water and flood 
damage; disrupted access to and from buildings 
and associated recreational and commercial ser-
vices; and disrupted access and damage to boats 
and docks.

OCEANSIDE BEACHES
Oceanside has approximately 35 acres of 
usable beaches under the control of the City. 
These beaches are beloved by Oceanside res-
idents and visitors alike; according to the 2018 
Sports, Fitness, and Leisure Activities Topline 
Participation Report, visiting the beach is 
the number one outdoor recreation activity 
in Oceanside. Approximately 54 percent of 
Oceanside residents visit the beach at least once 
a week. 
However, these treasured community features 
have long been threatened by erosion, storm 
damage, and sea level rise. The City of Oceanside 
has a history of beach erosion resulting from the 
construction of Camp Pendleton Harbor in 1942. 
in 1967, Congress authorized a review study of 
beach erosion at Oceanside which confirmed 
that the federal government was responsible 
for the erosion of Oceanside’s beaches. Despite 
numerous and significant efforts to replenish 
beach sand, a permanent solution to the mas-
sive erosion problem has yet to be achieved. 
The Water Resources Development Act of 2000 
authorized the City’s requested Shoreline Special 
Study at federal expense. However, the study is 
currently stalled due to a lack of federal funding.
Much of the Oceanside shoreline relies on shore-
line protection via seawalls or other armoring. 
Seawalls are the primary shoreline protective 
device from the northern City limits south to 
Tyson Street Park, and rip rap is the primary 
shoreline protective device from Tyson Street 
Park to the southern City limits.
Harbor Beach
Oceanside’s largest beach, Harbor Beach offers 
a variety of amenities including picnic areas, bar-
becue grills, gazebos, and restrooms with show-
ers, as well as three lifeguard towers. Harbor 
Beach also provides easy access to the Marina 
Inn Hotel and a variety of options for shopping 
and dining.
Breakwater Way
This beach is located just south of Oceanside 
Harbor Beach. A river jetty occurs where the 
San Luis Rey River meets the ocean. A sand-
bar is often formed here and provides a good 
surf break. Breakwater Way has showers and 
restrooms, picnic tables, barbecue grills, and a 
volleyball court. A pay parking lot is located each 
of Pacific Street.

Tyson Street Beach and Park
Tyson Street Beach and Park is located along the 
Strand within walking distance of the Oceanside

During the summer months, Oceanside 
Boulevard Beach hosts an area that is desig-

ers only. For safety, this area is closed to surfing

Buccaneer Beach

Southernmost Oceanside Beaches
South Oceanside beaches are accessible mainly 
at low tide, with the exception of the beachfront 
at St. Malo, accessible from the south side of the

OCEANSIDE MUNICIPAL PIER
The 1,564-foot Oceanside Municipal Pier serves 
as the heart for Oceanside’s beach activities. The 
pier extends about a quarter or a mile into the 
water.   The pier consists of a concrete structure 
that spans from the Pier View Way/Pacific Street 
intersection on the bluff to the wooden pier on 
the beach and wooden pier from the beach that 
extends into the ocean. The concrete structure, 
built in 1926 and refurbished in 1985, consists of 
twin conventionally reinforced bridges, a ramp 
down to the beach, and public restrooms. The 
restrooms in the concrete structure at the bluff 
were converted to storage decades ago and in 
1985 portions of the substructure closest to 
the surf were converted into a lifeguard head-
quarters. Today, the bridge provides pedestrian 
access to businesses on the wooden pier and 
beach.. The Pier is used for fishing, strolling, and 
sightseeing.
THE STRAND
The Strand is the name of the main street along 
the shore. The Strand serves both pedestrians 
and motor vehicles. The Strand is frequently inun-
dated and subjected to wave runup and damage 
during coastal storms or high-tide events due to 
its proximity to the coast and low elevation.

7.7	 Cultural Resources
In 2018, Oceanside was recognized as one of 
14 inaugural Cultural Districts by the California 
Arts Council. This designation highlights the sig-
nificance of Oceanside’s rich cultural diversity 
and unique artistic identity, embodied by such 
institutions as the Sunshine Brooks Theater and 
Oceanside Museum of Art.
JUNIOR SEAU PIER AMPHITHEATER
The Junior Seau Pier Amphitheater (Oceanside 
Bandshell) is a public entertainment venue 
located at the foot of the Oceanside Pier. The 
theater has a total capacity of approximately 
2,500 people (1,500 seated) and has been used 
to host large concert events, movies, awards 
ceremonies, high school graduations, and cul-
tural events. The theater was constructed in 
1937 as a project of the federal Works Progress 
Administration and significantly renovated in 
1950. The City is currently conducting a feasi-
bility study for the Oceanside Bandshell.  The 
study will identify and analyze the existing site 
conditions and evaluate site opportunities and 
constraints for the area.
OCEANSIDE MUSEUM OF ART
The Oceanside Museum of Art (704 Pier View 
Way) is a fine arts museum that hosts art 
focused on works by contemporary artists of 
southern California. Works exhibited include 
paintings, sculpture, furniture, textile art, and 
glass. The museum typically features over a 
dozen modern and contemporary art exhibitions 
each year. in addition to its exhibit offerings, the 
museum hosts a number of community pro-
grams including school visits, youth and family 
programs, adult classes and lectures, and a book 
club.
In 2019, the museum launched a partnership 
with SD Malkin Properties, Inc. (SDMP) to curate 
art for the developer’s two beachfront resorts, 
the Joie de Vivre Hotel and the Destination 
Resort. The museum has also hosted offsite 
exhibitions at such locations as the New Village 
Arts Theater, L Street Gallery, and North Coast 
Repertory Theater. in 2020, the museum began 
offering virtual museum experiences including 
virtual studio visits, artist talks, and workshops. 
The museum opened its dedicated facility, two 
buildings designed by Irving Gill and Frederick 
Fisher, in 1997. 
The museum has received funding and sup-
port from a variety of sources, including 
the Oceanside Arts Commission, Oceanside 
Charitable Foundation, and James Irvine 
Foundation.
SUNSHINE BROOKS THEATER
The Sunshine Brooks Theater (217 N Coast 
Highway) was founded in 1936 (under the name 
the Margo) and acquired by the City of Oceanside 
in 1993. The theater hosts a youth theater arts 
academy, concerts, art exhibits, and a number 
of theatrical productions. The Oceanside Theatre 
Company is the current professional production 
company in residence at the theater.
The Sunshine Brooks Theater receives financial 
support from the public in the form of tax-de-
ductible donations.

The California Surf Museum was founded in 1986

community, surf film festival, and an annual gala.

the county. in 2011, the California Surf Museum

DEVELOPER FEES
The City of Oceanside institutes developer or 
impact fees in order to provide funding for 
public facilities, schools, and parks. Residential 
developments pay $4.08 per square foot for 
OUSD and VUSD, $3.36 per square foot for BUSD 
and $3.48 per square foot for CUSD. Commercial 
Industrial developments pay $0.66 per square 
foot for OUSD and VUSD,  $0.54 per square foot 
for BUSD, and $0.56 per square foot for CUSD. 
Residential developments pay $2,621 per unit 
towards public facilities and approximately 
$4,431 per unit towards park funding as part 
of the Quimby Act. Commercial and industrial 
development pays $0.902 per square foot 
toward public facility development. 
Developer fees are also used to fund wastewa-
ter and water system capacity improvements. 
Developments are charged according to meter 
size, with multi-family and non-residential devel-
opments paying between $7,794 (5/8-inch meter) 
and $194,856 (4-inch meter) in wastewater 
capacity buy-in fees and between $5,680 (5/8-
inch meter) and $45,440 (2-inch meter) in water 
system capacity buy-in fees.

neighboring cities Carlsbad and San Diego

Oceanside’s coastal amenities are threatened 
by a combination of declining beach sand 
and sea level rise due to climate change. 
Future sea-level rise is expected to create

Between 19 and 28 recreational buildings
