3.1	City Structure and 
Character
Oceanside is a distinctive coastal community 
endowed with multiple scenic beaches, and 
blessed with great weather, three coastal water­
sheds, and a variety of unique coastal amenities. 
These amenities include the Oceanside Small 
Craft Harbor, the Oceanside Municipal Pier, the 
Junior Seau Amphitheater, and The Strand - the 
only public roadway on the beachfront in San 
Diego County. Featuring three distinct east-west 
corridors bounded by hills, mesas, and can­
yons, Oceanside contains an extensive network 
of public parks, trails, and natural open space. 
Incorporated in 1888, Oceanside has extended 
as much as 12 miles inland with a diverse array 
of industrial/business parks, commercial cen­
ters, and residential neighborhoods.  The City 
also includes roughly 3,500 acres of agricultural 
land in the South Morro Hills Neighborhood 
Planning Area.
Oceanside was first settled by Yuman-speaking 
Native Americans more than 3,000 years ago. 
The area’s original inhabitants lived in semi-per­
manent villages, foraging for food that included 
acorns, small terrestrial animals, and fish. 
Today, the San Luis Rey Band of Mission Indians 
represents the Native American community and 
ensures proper treatment of cultural resources. 
The City is home to Mission San Luis Rey de 
Francia, the King of the California Missions, 
founded in 1789. Other historic resources 
include San Luis Rey Pioneer Cemetery, Coast 
Highway (Historic Route 101), and the original 
Oceanside City Hall (designed by Irving Gill). 
The Oceanside Historical Society curates a rich 
archive of publications, photographs, oral histo­
ries, videos, and other materials that chronicle 
the City’s history.

Oceanside is a burgeoning tourist destination, 
drawing an increasing number of visitors to its 
harbor, beaches, restaurants, micro-breweries, 
museums, parks, natural habitat, and com­
munity events (e.g., Farmers Market, Sunset 
Market, Harbor Days, Ironman 70.3, Race Across 
America, surf competitions). The City is currently 
promoting agritourism in the South Morro Hills, 
modifying zoning standards to allow for a wide 
range of visitor-serving uses. 
While Oceanside is bounded and delineated by 
natural elements (e.g., the Pacific Ocean, Camp 
Pendleton wildland, watersheds), the City’s 
urban structure, shown in Figure 3-1, is largely 
defined by its network of highways and major 
arterials. The three major east-west commercial 
corridors include Mission Avenue/Highway 76, 
Oceanside Boulevard, and Vista Way/Highway 
78. Situated between these corridors are res­
idential neighborhoods set amongst rolling 
hills, as well as active-use open space and nat­
ural habitat. These corridors are intersected by 
three major north-south streets, El Camino Real, 
Rancho Del Oro Drive, and College Boulevard, 
with commercial nodes at the junctions. These 
nodal areas are activity centers that offer goods 
and services, entertainment venues, and transit 
connections.
Interstate 5 (I-5) also represents a major defin­
ing feature: west of I-5 is largely characterized 
by Oceanside’s coastal amenities and increas­
ingly vibrant Downtown, with several larger 
hotel and mixed-use developments coming to 
fruition. While to an outsider the coastal areas 
define Oceanside’s identity, the vast majority of 
the City’s 175,000 residents, as well as the City’s 
largest private employers such as Genentech, TE 
Connectivity, and Gilead Sciences—are located 
east of I-5.

DENSITIES AND INTENSITIES
While much of Oceanside is suburban and lower 
density—characterized by single-family neigh­
borhoods and commercial strip centers—the 
City also includes pockets of higher-density 
attached housing, several industrial areas, a 
Downtown District with a major regional tran­
sit hub, and coastal zone, roughly 3,500 acres 
of farmland, and extensive open space within 
its principal watersheds (the San Luis Rey River, 
Loma Alta Creek, and Buena Vista Lagoon). With 
some exceptions, the intensity of land use cor­
relates with proximity to major transportation 
corridors; the most intense land use patterns 
are generally concentrated along Coast Highway, 
Mission Avenue, and Highway 78. Most residen­
tial neighborhoods have a single-family charac­
ter, with variable lot sizes, though some neigh­
borhoods include a mix of housing types (e.g., 
Eastside Capistrano and other portions of the 
coastal zone).  Attached multifamily housing is 
distributed throughout the City, with concentra­
tions in the following areas:
•	 Downtown District
•	 Adjacent to the LOSSAN railroad corridor
•	 Hillsides southerly of Highway 76
•	 Portions of the North Valley Neighborhood 
Planning Area
•	 Along Greenbrier Drive and Canyon Drive
•	 Eastern portions of the Fire Mountain Neigh- 
borhood Planning Area
•	 Near the northwest corner of Vista Way and 
College Boulevard
•	 West of El Camino Real between Mesa Drive 
and Oceanside Boulevard
Mobile home parks are also prevalent in the 
City.  There are three mobile home parks in the 
coastal zone, with some coaches serving as per­
manent housing and others as vacation homes. 
Two mobile home parks are situated within the 
Oceanside Boulevard corridor.  There are eight 
mobile home parks in the San Luis Rey Valley, 
most of which are clustered near Mission San 
Luis Rey.

Residential Density
For residential uses, density is expressed as the 
number of housing units per acre (units/acre, or 
dwelling units per acre, du/ac). “Net” residential 
density excludes public rights-of-way, as distin­
guished from “gross” residential density, which 
includes land for streets and other rights-of-way. 
Figure 3-3 shows the distribution of existing 
residential density within Oceanside. More than 
half (61 percent) of the existing residential area 
has densities less than six units per acre. There 
are 17,467 units at a density above 18 units per 
acre, and only six percent of residential devel­
opment has densities above 20 units per acre. 
The average (net) existing residential density 
in the Planning Area is approximately 8.7 units 
per acre, demonstrating the relatively dispersed 
suburban nature of Oceanside and the predomi­
nance of detached single-family homes.

Non-Residential Development Intensity
Non-residential development intensity is ex- 
pressed as Floor Area Ratio (FAR), which refers 
to the ratio between a building’s total floor area 
(excluding any area devoted to structured park­
ing) and the total area of the site. While FAR does 
not directly correspond to the number of stories, 
building height, or building configuration, this 
metric generally accounts for both the horizontal 
and vertical components of development.
The intensity of existing non-residential devel­
opment in the Planning Area, including retail, 
office, industrial, and institutional use, is shown 
in Figure 3-4. Overall, non-residential buildings 
in Oceanside have an average FAR of 0.26, with 
93 percent of the existing sites accommodating 
an FAR below 0.50. Many of the non-residential 
buildings include commercial strip centers with 
extensive areas dedicated to surface parking.

CORRIDOR CHARACTER
East-West Corridors
The three major corridors connecting eastern 
and western areas of Oceanside are Mission 
Avenue/Hwy76, Oceanside Boulevard, and Vista 
Way/Hwy78. All three corridors extend through 
the entire stretch of the City east of I-5 and con­
nect the eastern neighborhoods to Downtown 
and the coast. Mission Avenue parallels Mission 
Expressway (SR 76) while Vista Way is adjacent to 
Ronald Packard Parkway (SR 78); both of which 
connect I-5 to I-15.  Thus, these corridors provide 
for local traffic movement and access to com­
mercial uses along the corridors, whereas the 
California State Route 76 and 78 freeways carry 
higher-volume, higher-speed vehicle traffic. 
Mission Avenue/Hwy 76 is primarily a commer­
cial corridor, with smaller percentages of indus­
trial, residential, and institutional land uses. 
Several residential neighborhoods immediately 
abut eastern stretches of Mission Avenue. This 
corridor features the Oceanside Municipal 
Airport, Mission San Luis Rey, Heritage Park, 
Guajome Regional Park, Oceanside Municipal 
Golf Course, and the South Morro Hills agri­
cultural area.  The San Luis Rey River extends 
through this corridor, providing contiguous open 
space and natural habitat.  The SLR River is a 
recreational amenity, a scenic resource, and an 
important source of potable water for the City. 
The river also serves to delineate certain neigh­
borhoods within this corridor.
Oceanside Boulevard includes a mix of land 
uses, including commercial, industrial, office, 
residential, and recreational facilities. The City’s 
largest employment hub, a collection of business 
and technology parks, extends along the north­
ern side of this corridor between Rancho del Oro 
and College Boulevard.  Major employers in this 
area include Genentech, Gilead Sciences, Coca-
Cola, MagnaFlow, and FedEx.  El Corazon Park, 
and Goat Hill Golf Course are major open space 
amenities within this corridor.  Loma Alta Creek 
parallels Oceanside Boulevard, running along the 
base of the hillsides that delineate the southern 
boundary of this corridor.  North County Transit 
District’s Sprinter rail line extends through this 
corridor.  The half-mile radii around each of the 
six Sprinter stations in this corridor have been 
designated as Smart Growth Opportunity Areas 
on SANDAG’s Smart Growth Concept Map.
The Vista Way corridor includes commercial strip 
centers, professional and medical office, multi­
family residential, and recreational facilities. Mira 
Costa Community College, El Camino County 
Club, several large shopping centers, and Tri-City 
Hospital are major features located within this 
corridor. Buena Vista Creek flows through this 
corridor, opening into an expansive lagoon at 
its western boundary.  Due in large part to the 
presence of, the coastal and Inland segments of 
Vista Way are bifurcated by sloping terrain and 
the Hwy 78/Interstate 5 interchange.
More detailed information on the character of 
these corridors can be found in the SSCSP Existing 
Conditions Report.

NORTH-SOUTH CORRIDORS
El Camino Real
El Camino Real is a north-south corridor starting 
at Douglas Drive and continuing south of Vista 
Way into the City of Carlsbad. It is a four-lane 
road with two lanes in each direction with a 
median and intermittent turn lane in the center. 
Bike lanes run on both sides of El Camino Real 
south of Mesa Drive.  North of Mesa Drive, only 
the northbound side of El Camino Real includes 
a bike lane The northbound and southbound 
lanes have a five-foot level difference between 
Mission Ave and Vista Rey with a slanted median. 
The medians are primarily paved with land­
scaped sections every 250 feet containing a mix 
of plantings, including deciduous trees, small 
bushes and shrubs. Sidewalk width is generally 
five feet wide and exists on both sides of the 
corridor with a large gap in the sidewalk net­
work adjacent the southbound lanes between 
Mission Ave and Mesa Drive. Street tree species 
vary along the corridor and existing trees are 
primarily found adjacent to the northbound 
lanes between Mission Avenue and Vista Rey. El 
Camino extends along three large tracts of pro­
tected open space between Mission Avenue and 
Fire Mountain Drive. 
Rancho Del Oro
Rancho Del Oro Drive is located in the center 
of the Planning Area, on average about one 
mile east of El Camino Real, and runs north to 
south from Mission Avenue to Vista Way, abut­
ting residential, commercial, recreational, and 
industrial uses. For most of its length, Rancho 
Del Oro is a four-lane road with two lanes in each 
direction with a median and turning lanes in the 
center. A dedicated bike lane runs on both sides 
of the street for the majority of the corridor. A 
median is present all along the street south of 
Via Rancho Road containing a mix of trees, small 
bushes, shrubs and sections of open paving. 
Sidewalk width is generally six feet, and side­
walks exist on both sides of the corridor, with 
a few gaps in the sidewalk network on undevel­
oped parts of the corridor. Verges, landscaped 
strips located between the sidewalk and street, 
are present in small sections south of Mesa 
Drive, becoming more consistent south of the 
Rancho Del Oro Sprinter Station. Street tree spe­
cies vary along the corridor and median, with a 
mixture of palm or deciduous trees. Some areas 
are missing trees altogether.
The City’s existing Circulation Element calls 
for an interchange at Rancho del Oro Road 
and Highway 78.  Due to physical constraints, 
it’s unlikely that on and offramps to and from 
the eastbound lanes of Highway 78 can be 
implemented.

College Boulevard
College Boulevard extends along the eastern 
portion of the Planning Area and abuts pri­
marily single and multi-family residential and 
commercial uses at the junctions with east-west 
corridors. For most of the corridor, it is a four-
lane road with two lanes in each direction with a 
planted median in the center and turning lanes 
found in the center or the side of the street. 
Class 2 bike lanes are found on either side of 
the street, with an access point to the San Luis 
Rey River Trail at the northern end of College 
Boulevard just south of North River Road. 
Sidewalk width is generally five feet wide and 
exists on both sides of the corridor, with small 
sections of verges found it. Street trees are spo­
radic along the corridor and median, with large 
sections having no trees at all.
DOWNTOWN
Formerly a designated redevelopment area, 
the City’s Downtown District occupies the 
City’s northwestern corner, bordered by 
Camp Pendleton, the Pacific Ocean, Wisconsin 
Avenue and various roadways to the east. The 
Downtown District includes northern portions of 
the Townsite neighborhood. Featuring a mix of 
traditional and contemporary architecture, the 
Downtown District has been the City’s center 
for commerce, culture, and recreation since its 
incorporation.1 The Downtown District is char­
acterized by a mixture of retail venues, restau­
rants, hotels, housing, and institutional uses that 
contribute to an active, visitor-oriented urban 
environment. Housing in the Downtown District 
includes a variety of attached housing types 
(both apartments and condominiums), single 
family homes (including small-lot California “row­
homes”), and institutional housing. Mixed-use is 
an increasingly common form of development in 
the Downtown District. Most of the Downtown 
District is characterized by a rectangular grid 
block pattern with block sizes varying from 
approximately 240 feet by 330 feet northwest of 
Topeka Street, with longer blocks located south­
east of Topeka Street that are dimensioned 250 
feet by 440 feet. The relatively small block size,

high connectivity, and rectangular grid are ingre­
dients for a highly walkable place, although the 
blocks are separated from the rest of Oceanside 
and beach access due to I-5 and Los Angeles-San 
Diego-San Luis Obispo (LOSSAN) rail corridor.
In the Downtown District, the street grid gen­
erally runs northwest to southeast, parallel to 
the Pacific Ocean. Most blocks have an alley 
that bisects the blocks which makes it ideal for 
keeping parking and vehicle access off of the 
main streets, further enhancing the pedestri­
an-oriented streetscape. The street character 
for residential areas is typified by narrow streets 
with a planter strip in between the street and 
the sidewalk, with residential porches, and 
entrances directly facing the street creating a 
walkable environment. Similarly, in commercial 
areas, such as along Mission Avenue, much of 
ground floor storefronts directly face the street 
and provide a comfortable pedestrian realm 
with wide sidewalks, outdoor dining, street trees 
and greenery, benches, bike racks, and trash 
receptacles. Overall, the walkability and pedes­
trian orientation of the blocks and buildings 
add to the Downtown community character and 
help make it an attractive place for both visitors

Eastside Capistrano 
The Eastside Capistrano (“Eastside”) Neigh-
borhood Planning Area (NPA) is located immedi­
ately east of Interstate 5 and north of Mission 
Avenue, and west of the Airport NPA.  Eastside 
Capistrano is divided by the San Luis Rey River, 
with the southern portion featuring a mix of 
single and multi-family housing and the north­
ern portion comprised entirely of single-family 
homes.  This neighborhood dates back to the 
early 1900s, when it was first established as 
a largely Mexican American community. The 
diversity of the neighborhood has expanded as 
the years have passed, with African American, 
Samoan, and Filipino communities moving into 
the area starting the 1950s.2  
The Eastside neighborhood is made up of a 
variety of housing types and land uses, includ­
ing large multifamily complexes, single-family 
residences, and low-intensity commercial uses. 
The neighborhood is split into two main resi­
dential sections by Highway 76 and the San Luis 
Rey River, with Capistrano Park and a variety of 
single-family residences to the north. The south­
ern portion has a compact rectangular street 
grid with a mix of multifamily apartments, single 
family homes, and commercial and restaurants 
along Mission Avenue. Most of the area is res­
idential with narrow streets, sidewalks, and 
street trees. Buildings typically have windows 
and entrances that open up to the street and 
most of the single-family homes have fenced-in 
front yards. Major community features of note 
include public schools, City facilities, and reli­
gious organizations, including Laurel Elementary, 
Mission Elementary, Jefferson Middle School, 
Balderrama Park and Chavez Recreation Center, 
Capistrano Park, the Rosicrucian Fellowship, and 
the Prince of Peace Abbey. Lawrence Canyon 
is an important natural resource In Eastside 
Capistrano.  It is anticipated that nature habitat 
in Lawrence Canyon will be restored and pro­
tected in conjunction with new development.

Airport
The Airport NPA is located immediately inland of 
Eastside Capistrano, abutting Camp Pendleton 
to the north neighborhood on the north-west 
City boundary. This neighborhood is split by the 
San Luis Rey River, with single-family residen­

to the south.  
Buffered by open space, the single-family resi­
dential development north of the river (Wanis, 
Marlado Highlands) follows a curvilinear pattern 
featuring multiple cul-de-sacs. 
Industrial uses have a strong presence in the 
Airport NPA. The airport itself is surrounded 
by light industrial uses, and the Oceanside 
Industrial Park occupies most of those por­
tions of the NPA lying south of Highway 76.  The 
industrial park accommodates a wide range of 
uses, including auto repair, self-storage, surf­
board manufacturing, food processing, and 
warehousing.  Nitto Denko, an international 
membrane manufacturer and one of the City’s 
largest private employers, maintains its head­
quarters within the industrial park.  City facilities 
within the industrial park include an Oceanside 
Fire Department training facility and the City 
of Oceanside’s Water Utilities Meter Services

corner of the industrial park features mature, 
well-maintained landscaping, the rest of the 
area has little visual appeal, with somewhat 
barren streetscapes occupied by surface park­

outdoor storage, and waste disposal bins.  An 
earthen culvert along Via Del Monte could be a 
visual amenity but currently appears neglected.
A strip of commercial zoning extends along the 
northern side of Mission Avenue, accommodat­
ing a range of retail uses.  The La Mision afford­
able housing development also lies within this 
commercial zone and is oriented around the 
Copperwood shopping center.

Townsite
Located along the Pacific Ocean coastline, the 
Townsite NPA includes the land homesteaded 
by Andrew Jackson Myers, the founder of 
Oceanside. Townsite is arguably the most his­
toric of the City’s NPAs, as well as the most 
diverse and dynamic.  This NPA has experienced 
enormous change over the past 25 years, while 
maintaining some of it most character-defining 
elements.  
Townsite features a diverse mix of develop­
ment types, including historic single-family 
neighborhoods, low-profile “main street” com­
mercial buildings, beachside attractions, public 
facilities, hotels, and new mixed-use buildings. 
Development patterns are largely based on a 
rectangular grid, with many storefronts and 
residential entryways facing the street creating 
a walkable and lively environment. Townsite 
is home to some of Oceanside’s most popular 
coastal attractions, including the Oceanside 
Museum of Art, the Oceanside Municipal Pier, 
Oceanside Small Craft Harbor, the California 
Surf Museum, the Junior Seau Amphitheater, and 
beaches.  All of the City’s Downtown District lies 
within Townsite.  
City Hall is located in Townsite.  City Hall exhibits 
an architectural style reminiscent of the work of 
Irving Gill, the designer of the City’s original City 
Hall (now the Oceanside Museum of Art) and one 
of the region’s most celebrated architects. (The 
Irving Gill style is reflected in other buildings 
within Townsite.)  The southwest corner of the 
City Hall complex features a large public plaza 
that accommodates a variety of community 
events.  
Townsite features a major regional transporta­
tion hub, the Oceanside Transit Center (OTC), 
where Amtrak, Metrolink, and NCTD rail services 
converge, and many NCTD “Breeze” bus routes 
begin and end. The OTC is served by Amtrak’s 
Pacific Surfliner, Metrolink’s Orange County 
Line, and NCTD’s Coaster and Sprinter, located 
within the Los Angeles-San Diego-San Luis 
Obispo (LOSSAN) rail corridor, the OTC is one 
of the most robust transit centers in southern 
California.  NCTD proposes to redevelop the OTC 
as a mixed-use complex featuring housing, com­
mercial uses, and “mobility hub” elements. 
Recent mixed-use and hospitality-oriented 
development in the Downtown District exhibits 
both traditional and contemporary architec­
tural styles.  It is anticipated that the Downtown 
District and the Coast Hwy Corridor will continue 
to redevelop in an eclectic manner.
The Coast Highway Corridor Study and Incentive 
District, adopted in 2019, will facilitate public 
realm improvements and new development 
subject to form-based zoning standards.  While 
these form-based standards do not prescribe 
a particular architectural style, they promote 
archetypal building forms, pedestrian-oriented 
elements, glazing, building articulation, and 
resilient building materials. Eastside Capistrano,

South Morro Hills
Located in Oceanside’s northeast corner, South 
Morro Hills is a rural area that features com­
mercial farms, estate housing, and a limited 
number of visitor-serving uses (e.g., wineries, 
wedding venues, horse stables).  South Morro 
Hills features distinctive landforms, including 
the Sleeping Indian rock formation, which can 
be seen from vantage points throughout the 
San Luis Rey Valley.  The hillsides and glens of 
South Morro Hills support a wide variety of 
crops, including citrus fruits, avocados, vegeta­
bles, , ornamentals, strawberries, coffee, and 
cannabis. Land holdings range from as much as 
300 acres to 2.5-acre home sites.  Rural residen­
tial development is largely concentrated along 
Sleeping Indian Road, which extends from North 
River Road into the unincorporated community 
of Fallbrook. Homes in South Morro Hills exhibit 
a wide variety of architectural styles.  
To support the long-term viability of farming 
in South Morro Hills, the City has amended its 
agricultural zoning standards to allow for “Tier 
1” (low intensity) agritourism uses, including 
restaurants, specialty stores, breweries, distill­
eries, and a variety of commercial recreation 
activities.  Such uses must be incidental and 
compatible with agricultural uses.  As part of the 
Oceanside General Plan Update, a Community 
Plan is being developed for South Morro Hills. 
The Community Plan seeks to facilitate addi­
tional agritourism uses while striking a balance 
between housing and farming operations. 
See the South Morro Hills Map Atlas for more 
information.

Guajome
The Guajome NPA is an auto-oriented exurban 
neighborhood located at the eastern edge of 
the City, just south of the San Luis Rey River. 
This neighborhood is primarily residential, char­
acterized by single-family detached residential 
developments, most of which were constructed 
between 1980 and 2000.   Single-family develop­
ment in the Guajome neighborhood ranges from 
small-lot (<4,000sf) subdivisions to estate resi­
dential areas with equestrian trails (e.g., Jeffrey’s 
Ranch, Marisol).  A limited amount of attached 
housing is clustered near Highway 76. 
The Guajome neighborhood includes an abun­
dance an open space.  The centerpiece of neigh­
borhood’s open space is Guajome Regional Park, 
nearly 400 acres of open space that include two 
fishing ponds, a 3.5-mile trail network, a picnic 
area, and a campground.  Natural habitat In 
Guajome Park includes woodlands, chaparral, 
wetlands, and mixed grasslands.  Other open 
space includes habitat preserves In the Darwin 
Knolls area, HOA-managed open space along 
Mesa Drive, segments of the San Luis Rey River, 
and Alamosa Park.  In deference to Guajome 
Park and other open space in the area, much 
of the Guajome NPA is within the Scenic Park 
Overlay District, which seeks to “achieve a 
visually pleasing and compatible relationship 
between buildings and structures, parking areas, 
walkways and planting areas, and the natural 
environment.”
The streetscape in most single-family areas is 
dominated by street-facing garages that take 
up the majority of the building façade. In most 
cases, sidewalks are directly adjacent to the 
roadway with inconsistent tree coverage. Other 
community features of note include the West 
Coast Tomato Growers packing plant, the Home 
Depot (only commercial use in the neighbor­
hood), and several public schools, all of which 
are part of the Vista Unified School District: 
Alamosa Elementary, Roosevelt Middle, and 
Mission Vista High School.   
A prevalent and distinctive design element in 
the Guajome neighborhood is split-rail fencing 
along bridal paths. Land use and representative 
images of Morro Hills and Guajome are shown in

North Valley
The North Valley NPA lies north of the San Luis 
Rey River, just west of South Morro Hills. The 
neighbor abuts Camp Pendleton to the north. The 
housing stock in North Valley is largely made up 
of single-family detached homes but also includes 
a mix of single-family attached homes and apart­
ments. Commercial uses are clustered in shop­
ping centers along North River Road. The neigh­
borhood includes a 49-acre crescent of industrial 
zoning between North River Road and the San 
Luis Rey River. The development pattern is simi­
lar to other suburban communities in Oceanside, 
with curvilinear streets ending in cul-de-sacs or 
looped roads, largely reflecting auto-dependent 
design. Public open space in North Valley includes 
Melba Bishop Park, Libby Lake Park, and Luiseno 
Park. The neighborhood features two golf courses 
- Oceanside Municipal and Arrowood. While the 
neighborhood is auto-centric, it is served by NCTD 
Breeze bus service that provides connections at 
the San Luis Rey Transit Center.  Public schools in 
North Valley include Libby Elementary, Reynolds 
Elementary, and Del Rio Elementary.  
Many of the residential neighborhoods in North 
Valley are typically oriented inwards, with perim­
eter walls and other features that separate them 
from major roadways.  Most single-family devel­
opment features homes with garages that directly 
face the street. 
The boundaries of the North Valley NPA are 
among the most distinct of any neighborhood in 
the City.  North Valley is bounded by natural hab­
itat to the north, south, and west and farmland 
to the east.  These habitat areas contribute to a 
sense of spaciousness. Crossings over the San 
Luis Rey River (Douglas Drive, College Boulevard) 
provide opportunities for announcing entrance 
into the North Valley NPA.
San Luis Rey
The San Luis Rey neighborhood area lies within the 
north-central part of Oceanside, south of the San 
Luis Rey River and north of the Ivey Ranch/Rancho 
Del Oro NPA. The neighborhood includes several 
historically important North County attractions, 
including Mission San Luis Rey de Francia and the 
Heritage Park Village and Museum. Other commu­
nity features include Heritage Park, the Oceanside 
Public Library Mission Branch, Ivey Ranch 
Equestrian Park, Foussat Elementary, Nichols 
Elementary, and Cesar Chavez Middle School, 
Mance Buchanon Park, and connections to the 
San Luis Rey River Trail. A variety of housing types, 
including attached and detached single-family 
homes, large mobile home communities, and 
apartments can be found in San Luis Rey. The 
development pattern is largely suburban, with 
curvilinear streets ending in cul-de-sacs or looped 
roads and limited street connectivity. There are a 
large number of community retail shopping plazas 
along Mission Avenue which are typified by large 
parking lots fronting the street and storefronts set 
to the rear.
Highway 76 essentially bisects the neighborhood, 
and the absence of grade-separated crossings 
creates access challenges for pedestrians and 
cyclists.   
San Luis Rey contains many Brazilian pepper trees, 
which were brought to the area by the Franciscan 
missionaries who founded Mission San Luis Rey. 
Brazilian peppers are also found along Mission 
Avenue and Douglas Drive.
The San Luis Rey neighborhood includes the 
Mission San Luis Rey Historic District, which is 
governed by design guidelines that promote 
Mission and Spanish Colonial Revival architec­
ture and other traditional design elements that 
complement Mission San Luis Rey.  There is sig­
nage announcing the Historic District at various 
points of entry. North Valley and San Luis Rey

Oceana
The Oceana NPA is a senior residential community 
located east of El Camino Real on a bluff that over­
looks the San Luis Rey Valley and the Ivey Ranch/
Rancho Del Oro neighborhood. Oceana features 
small single-story attached single-family homes 
on private streets that extend from a public ring 
road (Vista Campana). Established in the early 
1970s, Oceana exhibits a terraced development 
pattern with landscaped slopes and winding 
streets. Many of the residential buildings face 
inward towards common open spaces with few 
entrances or windows that directly face the street. 
Vista Campana features sidewalk and parkway 
that supports various species of mature trees. 
Some stretches of Vista Campana have more tree 
canopy than others.  Private streets do not include 
sidewalk or parkway, but adjacent yards and slope 
accommodate trees and shrubbery.
A community center is located at the entrance to 
the neighborhood.  The community center fea­
tures a collection of buildings with arched open­
ings reminiscent of the City’s original Irving Gill-
designed City Hall (now the Oceanside Museum of 
Art).
The Emerald Isle Golf Course, open to the public, 
extends along the southern boundary of the 
Oceana neighborhood.  Along with open space at 
the neighborhood’s northern boundary, the golf 
course contributes to a sense of spaciousness.
Ivey Ranch/Rancho Del Oro
Located at the center of the City, the Ivey Ranch/
Rancho Del Oro NPA is, is a diverse neighbor­
hood that includes a wide range of housing types, 
several commercial strip centers, business and 
industrial parks, and El Corazon Park, a 465-acre 
site that includes a senior center, an aquatics 
center, a regional soccer complex.  Governed by 
a specific plan adopted in 2009, El Corazon Park 
will continue to build out with additional rec­
reational amenities, an open-air sports arena, 
lodging, housing, and a range of commercial uses. 
The business and industrial parks in Ivey Ranch/
Rancho del Oro accommodate some of the City’s 
largest employers, including biotech companies 
Genentech and Gilead Sciences. Other companies 
operating in the business and industrial parks 
include Ashworth, Robert Mann Packaging, and 
Belching Beaver Brewery. Two hotels supported 
largely by sporting events and local business are 
situated along the eastern side of Rancho del Oro 
Boulevard.
Developed in the 1990s, the Ivey Ranch/Rancho 
del Oro NPA exhibits an auto-oriented develop­
ment pattern, with curvilinear streets, cul-de-sacs, 
and looped streets, and some of the largest blocks 
in the City. Detached single-family homes are the 
dominant housing type in Ivey Ranch/Rancho del 
Oro, with attached housing concentrated within 
western portions of the Old Grove Road corri­
dor.  Most residential areas in Ivey Ranch/Rancho 
del Oro feature abundant landscape, both in the 
public realm and on private property. Commercial 
retail is clustered at the intersection of Oceanside 
Boulevard and College Boulevard and Highway 
76, which is one of the busiest intersections in the 
City. Commercial development in this area does 
not exhibits a consistent architecture theme. 
Two NCTD Sprinter stations lie within Ivey Ranch/
Rancho del Oro, one at Rancho del Oro Road and 
another near College Boulevard. Both stations are 
situated along Loma Alta Creek and surrounded 
by dense riparian vegetation. 
Ivey Ranch/Rancho del Oro is home to several 
public and private schools, including El Camino 
High School, Martin Luther King Middle School, 
Ivey Ranch Elementary, Empresa Elementary 
and Coastal Academy. Other public facilities 
include the Mottino YMCA and Martin Luther 
King Park. Prior to development, much of the Ivey 
Ranch/Rancho del Oro NPA accommodated lime 
orchards. This agricultural heritage could pro­
vide opportunities for branding and public realm 
design themes.

Mira Costa
The Mira Costa NPA is located north of Hwy 78, 
east of El Camino Real, south of Loma Alta Creek, 
and west of College Boulevard.  The neighbor­
hood is bisected by Rancho del Oro Road, which 
links the Highway 78 corridor to central areas of 
the City.  Featuring sloping terrain with multiple 
canyons, Mira Costa is primarily a single-family 
residential neighborhood bounded by multifamily 
and non-residential uses to the north, south, and 
west.  
Residential areas include estate housing (e.g., 
Henie Hills, El Camino), production detached sin­
gle-family (e.g., Pacific Views, Mira Costa Estates), 
twin homes (e.g., Costa Serena), and garden apart­
ment complexes (e.g., Piazza d’Oro, Ocean Vista, 
Vue).  Multifamily development commonly lies 
between single-family areas and major thorough­
fares (e.g., College Boulevard and Vista Way). 
Commercial uses in Mira Costa extend along El 
Camino Real (professional and medical office, 
restaurants), and Vista Way (lodging, commercial 
recreation, medical office).  Commercial develop­
ment in these areas does not exhibit a cohesive 
architectural theme.
The neighborhood is home to Mira Costa College, 
which occupies a 121-acre hilltop site offering pan­
oramic views.  The Mira Costa campus features 
copious landscaping that includes a wide variety 
of tree species. The campus contributes to a sense 
of spaciousness in surrounding residential areas.  
Open space areas include El Camino Golf Course, 
Palisades Park, Sepulveda Park, and sensitive hab­
itat on the northern slopes of the neighborhood. 
Landscaped slopes and undeveloped canyons 
between residential areas also contribute to open 
space in Mira Costa.
The stretch of College Boulevard that extends 
through Mira Costa features landscape slopes 
that descend from adjacent residential neighbor­
hoods.  The visual quality of these slopes could be 
enhanced with a consistent landscape palette and 
better maintenance. Oceana, Ivey Ranch/Rancho 
del Oro and Mira Costa are shown in Figure 3-9.

Loma Alta
The Loma Alta NPA is situated amidst sloping 
terrain between Mission Avenue to the north, 
Oceanside Boulevard to the south, El Camino 
Real to the east, and I-5 to the west. While Loma 
Alta is predominantly residential, with a mix of 
housing types (including single family homes 
oriented around cul-de-sacs and curvilinear 
streets and multifamily apartment housing in 
self-contained complexes), other types of land 
uses extend along the boundaries of the neigh­
borhood and create a range of edge conditions. 
Single-family residential zones in Loma Alta 
fall along either side of Mesa Drive, with some 
descending into the Mission Avenue and 
Oceanside Boulevard corridors. Single-family 
housing in western portions of Loma Alta, largely 
developed in the early 1960s, tends to be more 
eclectic than the tract homes built in eastern 
portions of the neighborhood in the late 1970s 
and early 1980s. The neighborhood features 
both small-lot and estate single-family areas. 
The “Casino” residential area, south of Mission 
Avenue and east of Foussat Road, includes sev­
eral Mid-Century Modern/Eichler homes, which 
could provide inspiration for both renovation 
and new development in Loma Alta.
Commercial centers in Loma Alta include Mission 
Promenade, Best Plaza, and Copperwood Center. 
Smaller commercial districts are located at 
intersections along the north side of Oceanside 
Boulevard.
Industrial uses are clustered within the Industry 
Street corridor, located between Oceanside 
Boulevard and Loma Alta Creek. In western 
reaches of this corridor, many properties 
accommodate outdoor storage of equipment 
and materials, with storage areas surrounded 
by chain-link fencing.  These conditions detract 
from curb appeal and the overall visual quality 
of the area.  In eastern reaches of this corridor, 
uses are contained within buildings and develop­
ment is buffered from the street by landscaping. 
Loma Alta contains several recreational facili­
ties, including Goat Hill Golf Course, Ron Ortega 
Recreation Park, and Buddy Todd Park. Other 
public uses include the Mira Costa Community 
Learning Center, the Boys and Girls Club, several 
churches, and the Oceanside Senior Center. 
Loma Alta includes two prominent gateways 
into the City, Mission Avenue at Interstate 5 
and Oceanside Boulevard at Interstate 5.  Both 
of these gateways would benefit from aesthetic 
upgrades, including enhanced landscape and 
hardscape in the Caltrans right-of-way, redevel­
opment of private property that removes sur­
face parking from major street frontages, deco­
rative paving, decorative fencing, entry signage, 
etc.
Fire Mountain
The Fire Mountain NPA is located immediately 
east of Interstate 5, south of Loma Alta Creek, 
north of Highway 78, and west of El Camino Real. 
Characterized by large-lot single-family devel­
opment that occupies a mesa overlooking the 
Oceanside Boulevard and Vista Way corridors, 
Fire Mountain is ringed by commercial and mul­
tifamily uses along its northern, eastern, and 
southern boundaries. 
Annexed into the City in the mid-1920s, Fire 
Mountain maintains a semi-rural ambience, with 
traditional architecture, richly landscaped yards, 
curving streets without sidewalks, and a few 
remaining family farms. Over the past 30 years, 
Fire Mountain has seen a lot of subdivision, in 
most cases creating new residential lots of 
roughly 10,000 square feet.  Much of this hous­
ing lies along cul-de-sacs and panhandle lots.
The commercial, industrial, and multifamily uses 
located along the perimeter of the neighborhood 
buffer single-family areas from arterial roadways 
and provide for a variety of edge conditions, 
including along Oceanside Boulevard, Highway 
78, and partially along El Camino Real.

Commercial retail spaces along the neighbor­

South Oceanside (South O)
The South Oceanside NPA is a coastal neigh­
borhood abutting the Pacific Ocean to the west, 
Buena Vista Lagoon to the south, Interstate 5 to 
the east, and Oceanside Boulevard to the north. 
One of the City’s oldest neighborhoods, South 
Oceanside was originally subdivided in 1887. 
Much of the area was developed in the 1950s, 
and many of the homes and commercial store­
fronts are reminiscent of this era. 
Development patterns in South Oceanside are 
similar to Townsite, with a rectangular grid of 
residential streets bisected by Coast Highway. 
Residential areas to the east of Coast Highway 
are predominately single-family, while residen­
tial areas to the west of Coast Highway include 
a mix of detached and attached housing types. 
Homes are generally oriented towards the street, 
with alleyways for garage access.  This arrange­
ment allows for uninterrupted sidewalk, much 
of which is buffered from the street by land­
scaped parkway.  While parkway maintenance is 
inconsistent in some areas, this space provides 
opportunity for expanded tree canopy and other 
landscape elements.  
The stretch of Coast Highway extending through 
South Oceanside accommodates small-scale 
retail, restaurants, motels, an RV resorts, and 
a mobile home park. Much of this stretch of 
Coast Highway is lined with Mexican fan palms, 
though many segments of parkway are either 
hardscaped or untended.  Crosswalks exist only 
at signalized intersections (e.g., Morse, Cassidy), 
making it difficult for pedestrians to cross Coast 
Highway in certain areas.  
Access to the coastline from inland portions 
of South Oceanside is limited by the railroad. 
The only railroad crossings in South Oceanside 
are at Oceanside Boulevard, Loma Alta Creek, 
and Cassidy Street.  Much of the coastline in 
South Oceanside is privately owned, with a 
limited number of pedestrian access points 
between Oceanside Boulevard and the St. Malo 
community.  
The Coastal Highway Sprinter Station, located 
near the north end of the community, provides 
light rail service that extends to Escondido. 
NCTD’s Breeze 101 bus services runs along the 
coast from Oceanside to La Jolla.
Recreational amenities in South Oceanside 
include Buccaneer Park, Marshall Street Park 
and Pool, walking trails adjacent to Buena Vistal 
Lagoon (maintained by the Audubon Nature 
Center), and a footpath that extends along the 
southern edge of Loma Alta Creek. 
Historic resources in South Oceanside include 
the St. Malo communit y, the Oceanside 
Cemetery, and the Pacific Terrace Bridge over 
Cassidy Street, which is one of the neighbor­
hood’s most iconic public realm elements. Loma 
Alta, Fire Mountain and South Oceanside are

Peacock
Situated at the City’s eastern boundary and 
bisected by Oceanside Boulevard, the Peacock 
NPA is a diverse that includes a variety of housing 
types, a small commercial center, industrial parks, 
and open space preserves. 
Residential uses in Peacock largely consist of 
tract single-family homes and duplexes, with the 
latter housing type comprising the Peacock Hills 
Senior Community. A garden apartment complex 
(Island Club) lies immediately north of Oceanside 
Boulevard between Temple Heights Drive and 
Melrose Drive. Most of the housing in Peacock was 
developed in the 1980s and reflects a traditional 
auto-oriented suburban orientation with curvi­
linear streets and loop roads.  The architecture is 
eclectic, with variable finish materials, roof config­
urations, and glazing. 
The neighborhood includes a concentration of 
light-industrial uses south of Oceanside Boulevard 
(Vista Pacific/Temple Heights and Oceanside 
Gateway Business Parks and the Prescott 
Industrial Park). These industrial areas are gen­
erally made up of concrete tilt-up buildings with 
storefront glass. 
Commercial uses are concentrated in the 
Oceanside Square strip commercial center, 
which includes a variety of restaurants, personal 
improvement establishments, a convenience 
store, and a Montessori school.  The architecture 
of the center features standing seam metal roofs 
alternating with stucco mansards. Community fea­
tures of note include the Melrose Drive Sprinter 
Station, Temple Heights Elementary School, and 
the City’s operations center. 
A distinctive feature of the built environment 
in Peacock is the wide landscaped median on 
Peacock Boulevard.  The median supports mature 
pine and magnolia trees that contribute signifi­
cantly to the overall tree canopy in the area.
Tri-City 
The Tri-City NPA is located between the Oceanside 
Boulevard and s the Highway 78 corridors, east of 
College Boulevard and west of the City of Vista. 
As the name implies, this neighborhood lies at the 
intersection of Oceanside, Vista, and Carlsbad. Tri-
City is primarily made up of single-family housing 
and embodies a suburban development pattern 
of curvilinear streets, cul-de-sacs, and looped 
roads, with limited street connections. A limited 
amount of multifamily housing is located in south­
ern portions of the neighborhood.  Residential 
areas are buffered from Vista Way and Highway 
78 by commercial and institutional uses at the 
neighborhood’s southern boundary.  
Anchored by a Home Depot, commercial uses are 
clustered at the northeast corner of Vista Way and 
College Boulevard.  Another small commercial 
center is embedded in the residential area, pro­
viding essential goods and services within walking 
distance of many residents.  
Much of the development in Tri-City dates to the 
early to mid-1960s, making this neighborhood one 
of the oldest in the eastern portion of the City. 
Much of the housing is showing its age, with many 
homes in need of renovation.  This is particularly 
so in southern portions of the neighborhood.
Relative to other NPAs, Tri-City has limited land­
scape and tree canopy in the public right-of-way. 
Parkway is absent on many streets, and where 
parkway exists, it is often barren and unkempt. 
The neighborhood features various species of 
palm, with few canopy trees. 
Tri-City Medical Center, surrounded by a variety of 
medical facilities and offices, is a major landmark 
in the neighborhood.  The medical center campus 
features abundant landscape, accented with 
pine, ficus, and coral trees.  Another prominent 
feature of the neighborhood is the John Landes 
Recreation Center, which includes a wide range of 
recreational amenities, including a skate park.

Lake
Situated to the south of Highway 78 and bordered 
by the cities of Vista and Carlsbad, the Lake NPA 
is a diverse neighborhood that includes a vari­
ety of housing types, numerous retail outlets 
and restaurants, and open space along Buena 
Vista Creek. Primarily oriented around College 
Boulevard and Lake Boulevard, this neighborhood 
Residential uses include detached single-family 
homes, duplexes, apartment complexes, the 
Rancho Calevero Mobile Home Park, and the Lil 
Jackson Senior Community. 
To the east and west of College Avenue are two 
large shopping centers, Quarry Creek Plaza and 
the College Plaza Shopping Center. At Lake’s 
northern edge is the Riding School, an equestrian 
center, and the Calavera Lake and hiking trails are 
located just beyond the Lake area boundary to the 
south. 
At present, the stretch of Buena Vista Creek that 
extends through the Lake NPA is largely hemmed 
in and obscured by development, and some seg­
ments have been channelized.  The creek passes 
under Thunder Drive and College Boulevard 
before descended down El Salto Falls, a waterfall 
that holds cultural significance for the local Native 
American community.
Ocean Hills
The Ocean Hills NPA is situated in the southeast­
ernmost corner of Oceanside, where it borders 
the cities of Vista and Carlsbad. This neighbor­
hood features sloping terrain that includes San 
Francisco Peak and Santa Sinforosa Ridge, two of 
the highest points in the City.  Many residential 
properties in Ocean Hills offer extensive views of 
the Tri-City area.  
Much of the neighborhood is comprised of large 
single-family subdivisions, oriented around cur­
vilinear streets and featuring landscaped slopes 
and informal open space areas.  Most of these 
subdivisions were developed in the 1990s and 
early 2000s, making Ocean Hills one of the newest 
neighborhoods in the City.  There is a limited 
amount of attached housing in Ocean Hills, includ­
ing the Windrift garden apartments.  Much of the 
housing development in Ocean Hills is gated.
The Ocean Hills Country Club and retirement com­
munity, developed in the mid1980s, is a defining 
element of the neighborhood. Comprised of twin 
homes that rim landscape slopes and a private 
golf course, the retirement community includes 
a wide range of amenities, including a clubhouse, 
pool, tennis and pickleball courts, and shuffle­
board.  There are eight smaller “villages” within 
Ocean Hills, each of which has its own homeown­
er’s association and governing board. 
Commercial development in Ocean Hills is concen­
trated near the intersection of Melrose Drive and 
Cannon Road.  The Palm Tree Plaza Commercial 
Center (which, ironically, features very few palm 
trees) includes a wide range of businesses, includ­
ing a pharmacy, personal services providers, and 
drive-thru food service.  At present, there is no 
full-service grocery store in Ocean Hills.
Public uses in Ocean Hills include Lake Park, Lake 
Elementary School, and Madison Middle School. 
A riparian open space corridor extends along the 
southern side of Lake Boulevard, connecting to 
the Lake Calavera Preserve. The Peacock, Tri-City, 
Lake, and Ocean Hills neighborhoods are shown 
in Figure 3-11.

3.2	Urban Design and 
Architectural Themes
NATURAL FEATURES
Situated at the northwest corner of San Diego 
County, Oceanside is bounded by the Pacific 
Ocean and open space areas that together lend 
the City an expansive and panoramic ambi­
ence.  This ambience is highly prized by many 
Oceanside residents, as indicated by input 
received through surveys and other public out­
reach activities in support of the General Plan 
Update.
Within the City’s boundaries, hills, mesas, and 
canyons contribute significantly to the commu­
nity’s visual character.  These topographical fea­
tures physically delineate and define Oceanside’s 
neighborhoods and major commercial corridors. 
Elevated areas serve as landmarks and afford 
expansive views (though in many cases public 
views are obscured by private development). 
The extent to which sloping terrain contributes 
to the City’s identity is reflected in the names 
of many of its neighborhood planning areas: 
e.g., South Morro Hills, Oceanside Hills, Fire 
Mountain, Loma Alta (“high knoll” in Spanish). 
Additionally, from various vantage points within 
the City, several mountain ranges are visible 
- the Santa Margarita Mountains on Marine 
Corps Base Camp Pendleton, the San Marcos 
Mountains in the Highway 78 Corridor, San 
Jacinto Peak, and Mount San Gorgornio.
Oceanside’s visual character is also defined 
by its coastline and inland waterways.  These 
areas provide a sense of spaciousness, estab­
lish boundaries within the built environment, 
and support tree canopy and other vegetation. 
Where the inland waterways meet the coast­
line, sloughs and lagoons are ecological, rec­
reational, and scenic resources that break up 
the urban fabric of the City’s coastal area. The 
City’s coastline is developed with a small-craft 
harbor, a municipal pier, a public amphitheater, 
a community center, a public park, and a variety 
of housing types.  The City’s inland waterways 
have been channelized, and in some areas, 
hemmed in and obscured from view by adjacent 
development.
In addition to topography and views, Oceanside 
neighborhoods are also defined by their tree 
canopy cover. Tree canopy cover can dramati­
cally change the look and feel of an area and can 
reduce the surface temperature, minimizing the 
urban heat island effect. Figure 3-12 shows the 
variation in tree canopy coverage across the City. 
Neighborhoods to the north of the city such as 
North Valley and the Airport area tend to have 
less tree canopy cover. The coastal neighbor­
hoods of Townsite and South Oceanside also 
have less tree canopy cover as they are domi­
nated by development and beach access. The 
highest level of tree canopy cover is found in Fire 
Mountain and Guajome. Many of the neighbor­
hoods with high tree canopy cover, such as East 
Side Capistrano, are directly adjacent to areas 
with low tree canopy cover indicating that the 
type of development has a greater effect on tree 
canopy cover than the location of a neighbor­
hood in the City.

DEVELOPMENT PATTERNS
The diverse urban design and architectural char­
acter of Oceanside reflects changes in popular 
tastes over time, local policies and regulations, 
and natural conditions, including topography, 
waterways, and protected habitat.  While early 
development in Oceanside is generally char­
acterized by a rectilinear street grid, uniform 
lot dimensions, and eclectic architecture, more 
recent developments tend to feature curvilinear 
street patterns, variable lot sizes, and an archi­
tectural consistency associated with suburban 
production housing.  The older pattern, preva­
lent in western reaches of the City, supports a 
relatively dense, urban environment.  The newer 
pattern, prevalent in inland areas, has resulted 
in a dispersed, suburban environment.  While 
older parts of the City tend to be walkable, with 
residential, commercial, and institutional land 
uses in close proximity to one another, newer 
areas tend to be more auto dependent, with res­
idential neighborhoods being more separated 
from commercial districts, public facilities, and 
other destinations. 
As is the case everywhere, development in 
Oceanside has both adapted to and modified the 
natural environment.  
In areas with relatively flat terrain, streets tend 
to follow a grid pattern, whereas in areas with 
sloping terrain, streets tend to curve along 
ridges and ravines.  In flat areas, streets tend 
to be thoroughfares, while in sloping areas, 
streets tend to terminate in cul-de-sacs and 
t-intersections.  
In flood-prone areas, development has often 
protected by levees and other devices and/
or elevated out of designated flood zones.  As 
noted earlier, waterways in Oceanside have 
been channelized and diverted to accommo­
date development.  This can be seen in the San 
Luis Rey Valley, the Oceanside Boulevard cor­
ridor, and along Buena Vista Creek in the Lake 
Neighborhood Planning Area. 
While the City has hillside development stan­
dards that require residential subdivisions to 
minimize disruption of existing landforms and 
preserve areas exceeding 40% slope, these stan­
dards do not protect ridgelines.  With no ridge­
line protection standards in place, the City has 
seen distinctive landforms graded and devel­
oped.  Residential development occupies much 
of elevated portions of the City, leaving few 
undisturbed ridgelines and hilltops.  However, 
hillside development often utilizes sloping ter­
rain to incorporate landscaped slopes and infor­
mal open space areas, contributing to a sense of 
spaciousness in hillside residential areas.

STREETSCAPES
Streetscapes are fundamental to the look and 
feel of the built environment.  The defining ele­
ments of a streetscape include roadway width, 
traffic control devices, paving, curb design, 
parking design, pedestrian and bicycle facilities, 
outdoor furnishings, landscaping, outdoor light­
ing, public art, signage, and building design and 
orientation. Streetscape design can contribute to 
a “sense of place” that invites outdoor activity, 
social gathering, and commerce.  There is con­
siderable variation in streetscapes in Oceanside. 
•	 While some areas of the City feature “com-
plete” streets that provide for all modes of 
transportation, other areas are dominated by 
streets that largely serve only automobiles.  
•	 In some areas, streetscapes feature abundant 
landscaping, while in other areas streetscapes 
exhibit little to no landscaping.  The public 
right-of-way in some areas includes plantable 
parkway between traffic lanes and sidewalks, 
center medians with landscape or decorative 
hardscape, landscaped slopes, and other 
design elements that soften the streetscape. 
In other areas, such elements are either 
lacking or not well-maintained.    
•	 For the most part, dr y utilities within 
commercial corridors have been placed 
underground, but many residential streets still 
include power poles and overhead wires.
•	 While some commercial development is street-
adjacent, with parking located to the rear, 
most commercial development is separated 
from the street by surface parking.  In some 
cases, street-adjacent development does not 
“greet” the street but rather turns its side or 
back to it.
•	 Most street lighting reflects the ubiquitous 
“cobra” design with poles. There are some 
stylized light poles in the Downtown District. 
•	 Though still relatively uncommon, some 
streets include roundabouts, curb bulb-outs, 
chicanes, and other traffic-calming features. 
The City has recently adopted a “road diet” 
for portions of Coast Highway that will reduce 
traffic lanes, add and enhance crosswalks, and 
replace many traffic signals with roundabouts. 
Similar roadway improvements may be 
warranted and feasible in other commercial 
corridors.
•	 Alleys are prevalent in older areas of the 
City. Alleys are meant to provide for vehicle 
ingress and egress, leaving the street frontage 
for sidewalk, parkway, and other pedestrian 
elements. However, many alley-ser ved 
properties also include driveways on the street 
frontage, which, by interrupting sidewalks and 
parkways, discourage walking and reduce 
plantable area.
•	 An online survey on the City’s neighborhood 
planning areas solicited strong support for 
enhancement of visual quality within the public 
right-of-way, with many respondents calling 
for pavement repair, additional landscaping, 
sidewalk cleaning, more graffiti removal, and 
better litter control.
ARCHITECTURAL STYLES
There is no consistent, character-defining archi­
tectural theme in Oceanside.  While some devel­
opment in Oceanside authentically represents a 
particular architectural style, most development 
exhibits a more vernacular aesthetic. Vernacular 
architecture may display certain characteristics 
of popular styles but are generally simpler and 
more generic than their high-style counter­
parts. Examples of vernacular styles include 
recent midrise mixed-use development in the 
Downtown District, which generally reflects 
contemporary design but includes traditional 
elements like arched colonnades, wooden trel­
lises, and clapboard siding.  An in-depth archi­
tectural analysis is found in the Historic Context 
Statement, included as an appendix.  
While many single-family subdivisions in 
Oceanside exhibit contemporary Mediterranean 
design, with shallow-pitched tile roofs and 
stucco walls, other single-family areas feature 
ranch, craftsman, Tudor, mid-century modern, 
and other styles.  Contemporary Spanish and 
Italianate design, often featuring arched open­
ings and tower elements with hipped roofs, is 
common in retail centers, though retail develop­
ment often reflects branded corporate designs.

DEVELOPMENT STANDARDS AND 
DESIGN GUIDELINES
The City’s development standards regulate 
building height, setbacks, lot coverage/floor area 
ratio, landscape coverage, open space, parking, 
and wall plane articulation for nonresidential 
development.  These development standards 
do not prescribe proportions, architectural fea­
tures, glazing, finish materials, or other design 
elements.
The recently approved Coast Highway Incentive 
District introduces form-based development 
standards that emphasize pedestrian-oriented 
design through specific building and frontage 
typologies.  While these form-based develop­
ment standards do not prescribe particular 
architectural styles, they provide a degree of 
certainty regarding the scale, proportionality, 
and transparency that allows for a streamlined 
review process.  A similar approach to zoning 
regulations is contemplated for the City’s major 
inland commercial corridors. 
The City has prepared design guidelines only for 
the Mission San Luis Rey Historic District and the 
coastal zone.  The Historic District design guide­
lines prescribe Spanish and Mission Colonial 
Revival architecture, building orientation, and 
landscaping that complement Mission San Luis 
Rey.  The design guidelines incorporated into 
the City’s Local Coastal Program focus on the 
preservation and enhancement of coastal views, 
significant landmarks, and historic features. 
The Harbor Precise Plan addresses building 
design, landscaping, lighting, and signage.  Some 
planned development areas and homeown­
ers’ associations are governed by basic design 
guidelines, though these guidelines are not 
always consistently implemented by architec­
tural design boards.  In general, outside of those 
areas governed by design guidelines, neither 
City staff nor decision-makers recommend that 
new development embody particular architec­
tural styles.

ARCHITECTURAL HERITAGE
Examples of existing development that pro­
vide inspiration for new development include 
Mission San Luis Rey, the City’s original City 
Hall, the Oceanside Blade Tribune Building, the 
Rorick Homes, the Bunker Building, the Keisker 
Hotel, and Robert’s Cottages. The Mission has 
inspired both residential and non-residential 
development throughout the City, with its 
influence being particularly apparent in retail 
development (e.g., Marron Road commercial 
strip centers).  The Mission Revival style as inter­
preted by Irving Gill is also reflected in recent 
development in Oceanside (e.g., the current City 
Hall, Water Utilities Administration Building at 
SLR Wastewater Treatment Plant).  The Mission 
Pacific Hotel and Seabird Resort, expected 
to open in the summer of 2021, evoke the 
Craftsman design of the nearby Rorick Homes. 
This project also incorporates the so-called “Top 
Gun House,” a folk Victorian home constructed 
in 1887.  It is anticipated that historic and charac­
ter-defining development will continue to influ­
ence the design of new development.
While Oceanside is approaching “build-out” 
in the traditional sense, with little remaining 
vacant land available for new development, 
the City expects to continue to add population, 
housing, and employment.  Over the next 15 to 
20 years, it is anticipated that the City’s major 
commercial corridors will be transformed by 
midrise infill and redevelopment.  In single-fam­
ily neighborhoods, accessory dwelling units are 
expected to materialize in greater numbers, 
due to state-mandated zoning incentives and 
fee reductions.  If proposed state legislation 
allowing multifamily development in existing sin­
gle-family zones is ultimately enacted, the City’s 
single-family neighborhoods could experience 
significant change. The City will likely also see 
housing and agritourism-related development 
in the South Morro Hills Neighborhood Planning 
Area.  The South Morro Hills Community Plan, 
Smart and Sustainable Corridors Specific Plan, 
and Land Use Element present opportunities to 
manage the siting, intensity, and design of the 
evolving built environment in these and other 
areas of the City.

3.3	Visual Resources 
As noted throughout this report, both the nat­
ural and built environments in Oceanside offer 
visual resources.  The Pacific Ocean, inland 
waterways, habitat areas, distinct landforms, 
and nearby mountain ranges contribute to the 
scenic quality of the City’s natural environment. 
Within the built environment, historic buildings, 
quality architecture, landscaping, and public art 
provide visual interest and contribute to sense 
of place.
In the course of public outreach in support of this 
project, many residents have commented that 
the visual quality of Oceanside can be signifi­
cantly improved, both within public spaces and 
on private property.  Residents have called for 
restoration of natural habitat, improved mainte­
nance within the public right-of-way, refurbish­
ment of dilapidated buildings, more plantable 
area and tree canopy, better litter and graffiti 
control, and better enforcement of blighted 
conditions on private property.  Residents have 
also expressed a desire to maintain and enhance 
existing scenic views.  
HIGHWAYS
The California Department of Transportation 
(Caltrans) has designated three state scenic high­
ways within the County of San Diego, including 
Highway 78 in Anza Borrego State Park, Highway 
125 in La Mesa, and Highway 75 along the Silver 
Strand (Caltrans 2016). The I-5 highway corridor 
is part of the California Scenic Highway System 
and is eligible for designation as an Official 
Scenic Highway. I-5 provides visual access to the 
ocean for hundreds of thousands of people each 
day, a visual resource rarely experienced while 
traveling on a major urban freeway. 
SCENIC VIEWS
Currently, there is no Citywide inventory of 
scenic views. However, the City’s Local Coastal 
Program (LCP) states that the City’s important 
aesthetic resources include views of the Pacific 
Ocean, the San Luis Rey River, Buena Vista 
Lagoon, Oceanside Harbor, and Oceanside Pier.
The LCP includes policies that address the San 
Luis Rey River as a significant visual resource 
and requires new development to be sited and 
planned in a manner that retains the aesthetic 
values of the environment and to include design 
themes that complement the natural setting 
and history of the area (rustic, Spanish or Early 
California Mission design). The LCP seeks to 
establish viewing opportunities of the river and 
regulate on-site signs that would detract from 
the visual quality of the area and cause excessive 
glare or annoyance to surround properties. 
The LCP also protects visual resources by main­
taining existing view corridors through public 
rights-of-way. The LCP encourages development 
of viewing areas at the Pacific Street Linear Park, 
the Buena Vista Lagoon Fishing area (provided 
by Eaton Hill developers) and from the frontage 
road between Highway 78 and the inland por­
tion of Buena Vista Lagoon, and identifies the 
City’s visual orientation to the Pacific Ocean as 
a major identity factor for the City of Oceanside. 
State Route 78 to Vandegrift Boulevard in 
Oceanside is characterized by coastal bluffs and 
broad sand beaches lined with development. 
In general, a more urban quality appears both 
on the coast and along the freeway corridor. 
The Strand, the Oceanside Pier, and Oceanside 
Harbor establish coastal character. This unit 
also is home to the most significant architecture 
in the corridor. Mission San Luis Rey inspired 
the works of Irving Gill, which in turn inspired 
Charles Moore’s Oceanside Civic Center. All com­
bine to form a rich architectural heritage and 
historical continuity.

3.4	Key Community 
Design Issues and 
Implications
•	 Supporting and enhancing neighborhood 
identity and need for Community Plans. 
The existing General Plan has policies to 
prepare and adopt “Community Plans” to 
provide neighborhood implementation of 
Citywide policies and includes guidance to 
develop these plans. While no community 
plans currently exist, one is currently under 
preparation for South Morro Hills given its 
unique agricultural and natural resources and 
development pressures. 
The need to develop community plans for all 
parts of Oceanside is questionable for mul­
tiple reasons – most residents do not readily 
identify themselves with the boundaries or the 
names of the community plan areas; in many 
instances neighborhood needs are not so 
unique as to necessitate preparation of com­
munity plans; and lastly, more than 25 years 
have lapsed since the General Plan Land Use 
Element was comprehensively updated, and 
no community plans have been prepared while 
many of growing areas of Oceanside are now 
largely fully developed. 
	
— However, there is an opportunity for the 
General Plan to more directly address spe­
cific needs of Oceanside’s distinct neighbor­
hoods. Some areas, such as Townsite, South 
Oceanside, and San Luis Rey have very strong 
physical identities due to their histories and 
assets, whereas others, such as the Lake and 
Peacock community plan consist largely of 
residential developments without historic 
richness and diversity of uses, design, and 
building types. The Fire Mountain and South 
Morro Hills areas present their own unique 
constraints for future development due to 
their topography and natural heritage. These 
considerations should be reflected during 
development of General Plan alternatives as 
future growth possibilities are studied, as well 
in development of General Plan policies that 
address neighborhood assets and priorities. 
•	 Reinforcing Oceanside’s diverse and 
vibrant character. While the City’s oldest 
neighborhoods and the Downtown are next to 
the ocean, Oceanside today is more than just a 
beach town. Surrounded by hills and the ocean 
and containing extensive parks, trails, and 
open space, Oceanside encompasses a diverse 
array of employment areas, commercial 
centers, and neighborhoods reflecting various 
eras of the City’s development, extending 
from 18th century at the time of Mission San 
Luis Rey de Francia inception, to those nearly 
12 miles inland developed in the late 20th 
century.  The City’s employment areas are 
also diverse and continue to evolve. Oceanside 
is also a destination City, with beaches and 
a marina, a flourishing food and craft beer 
scene, and increasing cultural offerings. The 
General Plan should reinforce what is positive 
about Oceanside diverse settings and districts 
and improve the community’s overall sense of 
place.

•	 Defining urban character and form of 
major commercial corridors that run 
through Oceanside. These corridors 
connect the eastern and western portions of 
Oceanside and in the north-south direction 
neighborhoods to Carlsbad and Vista. Most 
of Oceanside’s commercial activity east of I-5 
occurs along these corridors. Because these 
corridors include the only continuous streets 
that extend through the City in the rolling 
terrain of eastern Oceanside, they are pivotal 
in shaping Oceanside’s identity and the drivers’ 
experience. 
	
— These corridors are primarily auto oriented, 
and characteristics that enhance the pedes­
trian experience, such as street trees, side­
walks, and architectural character are lacking 
in some portions. More significantly, design of 
these corridors can be dangerous to bicyclists 
or pedestrians. Policies in the General Plan 
can unify and strengthen the visual character 
of the corridors, make them more comfortable 
and safer, and improve the experience of walk­
ing, biking, and driving along these corridors. 
For instance, connecting sidewalks, improving 
pedestrian crossings, and widening the pedes­
trian right-of-way may attract more pedestri­
ans and bicyclists and better connect the City. 
In addition, concentrating infill development 
and emphasizing a pleasant ground floor 
experience may enhance the visual qualities of 
the corridors and attract more customers. 
	
— Planning for the corridors will occur principally 
through the Smart and Sustainable Corridors 
Specific Plan, which is being prepared in paral­
lel with the General Plan. However, the General 
Plan will need to ensure that planning for the 
corridors is cohesively integrated with Citywide 
planning. 
•	 Preserving and enhancing the City’s 
overall visual quality. Oceanside contains 
several designated scenic highways and a 
number of visually important resources. 
While there is no current inventory of scenic 
views, Oceanside’s current General Plan does 
specify that the City should encourage the 
preservation of significant visual open spaces. 
Most of the visual resources identified in the 
current General Plan are open spaces that 
are protected from development. Some of 
the key visual resources in the community 
are addressed through plans and programs 
specific to certain areas – the coastal resources 
by the Local Coastal Program, the corridors by 
the Smart and Sustainable Corridors Specific 
Plan, and the visual and agricultural resources 
in South Morro Hills by a Community Plan; 
preparation of the latter two is underway. 
Planning on additional steps to enhance the 
community’s visual and scenic resources is 
needed. 
	
— One of the efforts could be to enhance the 
visual quality of the San Luis Rey River cor­
ridor, especially as the River Trail is more 
developed and extended through the City. The 
Draft LCP includes policies relating to develop­
ment along the river, including defining archi­
tectural style and policies on development 
massing. However, these would only apply to 
small extent of the river and could potentially 
be extended through the rest of the City, pro­
viding a cohesive visual experience from the 
easternmost portion of the City to the ocean. 
Similar considerations could be part of plan­
ning along Loma Alta Creek, the other east-
west open space spine that extends through 
the City. 
	
— Gateway locations, including highway exits, 
can be enhanced to provide a more welcoming 
environment and improve the City’s brand. 
Key gateway locations include portals into 
the City along Interstate 5, Highway 76, and 
Highway 78.  South Coast Highway at the 
City’s southern boundary is another important 
gateway, as is the intersection of Oceanside 
Boulevard and Melrose Drive
