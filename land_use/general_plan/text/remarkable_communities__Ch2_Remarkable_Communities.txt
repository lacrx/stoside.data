Oceanside will leverage its many assets to promote quality of 
life, economic development, and environmental quality. The 
City will distinguish itself and burnish its brand by preserving 
and enhancing its cultural, historical, and recreational resources, 
unique neighborhoods, and one-of-a-kind destinations. Infill 
and redevelopment in commercial corridors will provide 
for high-quality urban design and a strong sense of place.  
Meanwhile, the functional and visual integrity of established 
single-family neighborhoods will be protected and improved.

2.1  Background
All local communities are remarkable in their own 
way, and this fact is very much in evidence in the 
City of Oceanside. With a diverse population, a 
variety of distinctive natural features, several 
unique coastal amenities, an eclectic built envi-
ronment, and a rich history that includes Native 
American settlement, Spanish and Mexican rule, 
agriculture, railroads, and an 80-year relation-
ship with Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton, 
Oceanside distinguishes itself from other commu-
nities in a multitude of ways.  
The Remarkable Community Element (RC) cele-
brates and seeks to preserve and enhance those 
characteristics that contribute to Oceanside’s 
uniqueness and positive brand. The RC also 
acknowledges and considers ways to improve 
upon conditions that detract from quality of life, 
create or perpetuate inequities, inhibit econom-
ic development, and diminish community pride. 
The RC reflects the desire of the community to 
not only maintain (and, in some cases, adapt) 
the distinctive qualities of Oceanside in the face 
of inevitable change but also embrace changes 
that are in the City’s best interests. To this end, 
the RC acknowledges the City’s dynamism and 
ongoing evolution – recognizing that there are 
many opportunities for positive change – while 
also directing attention to those enduring aspects 
of Oceanside that lend the City its own identity. 
Oceanside is a distinctive and multifaceted com-
munity that displays a unique blend of coastal and 
inland characteristics. 
In many respects, Oceanside is akin to the other 
cities within the Highway 78 corridor – i.e., Vista, 
San Marcos, and Escondido. These cities have sim-
ilar socioeconomic profiles and housing markets, 
and they all have significant Hispanic populations. 
They share watersheds, topographic features, and 
micro-climates. They all border expansive wild-
land areas. Additionally, all four of these cities 
are served by NCTD’s Sprinter rail service. The 
two oldest incorporated cities in North County, 
Oceanside and Escondido are both important 
gateways into the San Diego region.
At the same time, Oceanside resembles the neigh-
boring North County Coastal cities of Carlsbad, 
Encinitas, and Solana Beach. Connected by 
Interstate 5 and the Pacific Coast Highway, these 
cities share a coastline that affords a wide range 
of recreational opportunities. They also share 
coastal lagoons that serve to delineate the cities 
and lend a unique visual character to the North

County Coastal subregion. All of these cities are

Oceanside’s most recognizable and character-de-
fining features include three miles of coastline on 
the Pacific Ocean, the Oceanside Municipal Pier, 
the Oceanside Small Craft Harbor, Mission San 
Luis Rey de Francia, the San Luis Rey River, the 
Rosicrucian Fellowship/Mount Ecclesia, Guajome 
Regional Park, El Corazon, and the South Morro 
Hills agricultural area. These venues draw visitors 
from around the world.  
The Municipal Pier —one of the longest on the 
West Coast—is an iconic coastal amenity and an 
essential aspect of the City’s brand. The north 
and south sides of the pier feature two consis-
tent, high-quality surf breaks in Oceanside, where 
many surf contests are held.
Founded in 1798 and known as the King of the 
Missions, Mission San Luis Rey de Francia is 
Oceanside’s most notable historic resource. The 
Mission is surrounded by the Mission San Luis Rey 
Historic Area, which is governed by design guide-
lines that seek to maintain and enhance the area’s 
historic character.  
Once the site of a sand and gravel mine, the 465-
acre El Corazon property is a premier regional rec-
reational venue, featuring a youth soccer complex, 
an aquatic center, a sports arena, a nature trail, 
and several public parks. Located in the geograph-
ic center of Oceanside, El Corazon is supported by 
several nearby hotels and dining establishments 
and can be accessed from the Rancho del Oro 
Sprinter Station. El Corazon is quickly becoming 
one of Oceanside’s most well-known destinations. 
Other notable destinations contributing to 
Oceanside’s character and brand include the 
City’s segment of the Pacific Coast Highway (aka 
Highway 101), the Oceanside Art Museum, the 
California Surf Museum, Heritage Park, Buena 
Vista Lagoon Nature Center, and Bob Maxwell 
Memorial Airfield.   
Oceanside’s character and culture are greatly 
influenced by the City’s location. Situated in the 
northwest corner of San Diego County and bound-
ed by the Pacific Ocean and expansive open space 
areas, the City affords sweeping views that extend 
to distant mountain ranges and stretches of coast-
line. The City’s hills, mesas, canyons, and riparian 
corridors serve to break up the built environment 
and provide visual relief. These natural features 
physically delineate and define Oceanside’s neigh-
borhoods and commercial districts.

Oceanside encompasses a diverse array of employ-
ment areas, commercial centers, and neighborhoods 
reflecting various eras of the City’s development, 
ranging from the Spanish colonial period to the 
present day. 
For decades, development was concentrated in the 
City’s coastal area. In the 1960s, the City began 
to sprawl into the San Luis Rey River Valley, the 
Oceanside Boulevard corridor, and the Highway 78 
corridor. Replacing natural open space and farmland, 
development in these inland areas of Oceanside is 
characterized by single-family subdivisions of vari-
able density, strip commercial centers, and busi-
ness/industrial parks. To accommodate develop-
ment in these areas, the San Luis Rey River, Loma 
Alta Creek, and Buena Vista Creek have been chan-
nelized. Residential development on inland hillsides 
and ridgelines has significantly modified natural 
landforms, and manufactured slopes are common. 
However, development in the City’s inland areas is 
intertwined with recreational open space, habitat 
conservation areas, riparian corridors, and undevel-
opable slopes, all of which afford expansive views 
and contribute to a sense of openness. 
Over the past 25 years, the City’s coastal area 
has undergone extensive redevelopment. The 
Downtown District has been transformed by tran-
sit-oriented development, high-quality resort hotels, 
and a variety of public improvements. Residential 
areas to the west of Coast Highway have seen 
single-family homes replaced by contemporary 
attached housing projects (both condominiums and 
rental apartments). Enabled by density bonus law 
and other state legislation, midrise mixed-use proj-
ects with income-restricted units are materializing 
along Coast Highway alongside commercial uses 
and cultural amenities.
Traversing through the eastern portion and con-
necting this to the west are three major corridors: 
Mission Avenue, Oceanside Boulevard, and Vista 
Way/Highway 78. These three corridors, together 
with the Coast Highway, are key commercial centers 
for the City. Cohesive redevelopment of these cor-
ridors offers the possibility of not just accommodat-
ing the city’s future growth but re-shaping the city’s 
identity and improving neighborhood livability.
The northeastern corner of the City features South 
Morro Hills, an agricultural area with about 3,500 
acres of the last remnant land in the coastal San 
Diego region in active agricultural use. The dramat-
ic terrain and elevation gain in this part of the city 
provide expansive views extending to the ocean in 
many locations. General Plan policies seek to main-
tain the overall character of the area, while promot-
ing the introduction of agritourism uses to ensure 
continued viability of commercial agriculture.

PURPOSE AND INTENT
The Remarkable Community Element (RC) seeks 
to reinforce distinctive and positive attributes of 
the City, preserve historic and cultural resources, 
and enhance community identity and the sense of 
place. Policies address the shift to infill and rede-
velopment and shaping this new growth pattern 
to meet community goals relating to climate mit-
igation and adaptation, mobility, and economic 
development. 
A fundamental objective of the RC is to ensure 
that future growth and change contribute to 
Oceanside’s livability, economic prosperity, and 
visual quality. To this end, the RC outlines a broad 
framework for transforming the City’s commer-
cial corridors into walkable activity centers that 
provide new housing and employment opportuni-
ties. Additionally, in conjunction with the Efficient 
and Compatible Land Use Element (ECLU), the 
RC provides guidance for establishing appropri-
ate linkages and buffers between different uses, 
open spaces, and transportation systems, ensur-
ing harmony between the City’s built and natural 
environments. 
Community Priorities
The Oceanside community prizes the City’s 
numerous scenic, cultural, and visual resourc-
es, and the City’s overall ambiance and sense 
of place. Community members broadly support 
infill and redevelopment within the City’s major 
commercial corridors, if changes result in more 
housing options, better access to essential goods 
and services, safer streets and public spaces, 
walkability, and improved visual quality. Focusing 
the City’s growth on the corridors provides the 
added benefit of maintaining the scale and char-
acter of existing neighborhoods. Many residents 
also expressed a desire for more frequent and 
safe connections for pedestrians and bicycles 
throughout the city, as well as increased access 
to commercial centers, parks, and lively public 
spaces. There is also support for maintaining the 
rural/agricultural character of South Morro Hills, 
albeit with diverse opinions on the best way to 
achieve this goal.  
Relationship to Specific, Community, 
and Other Plans
The Smart and Sustainable Corridors Specific 
Plan, developed concurrently with the General 
Plan, outlines policies and design standards 
for the corridors in greater detail. An Active 
Transportation Plan—for supporting walking and 
biking—is also under development, synchronized 
with the General Plan. The General Plan also calls 
for preparation of a South Morro Hills Community 
Plan to outline strategies for agricultural conser-
vation and the promotion of agritourism. As per 
State law, all specific and community plans are 
required to be consistent with the General Plan.  
TOPIC AREAS
The RC element is not required under State law, but 
is included in the General Plan to celebrate, pre-
serve, and enhance characteristics that contribute 
to Oceanside’s sense of place and community liva-
bility, and to improve conditions that detract from 
quality of life, create or perpetuate inequities, 
inhibit economic development, and diminish com-
munity pride. Under state law, all General Plan ele-
ments, whether required or optional, carry equal 
weight, and must be consistent with one another 
in terms of policy direction. Topics addressed in 
the RC include community character and design, 
cultural and historic resources, Oceanside’s iden-
tity as a unique visitor destination, and the City’s 
seventeen Neighborhood Planning Areas.
The RC establishes goals, policies, and actions 
meant to support a visually appealing and safe 
urban form and to protect and enhance the places 
that make Oceanside unique. The element pres-
ents frameworks for each of the City’s neigh-
borhoods that seek to identify opportunities for 
improvement while still valuing and preserving 
the existing character of each neighborhood.

2.2  Community 
Character and Design
At the citywide scale, Oceanside’s location and 
geography play a key role in shaping the City’s 
overall form and structure. At a district or neigh-
borhood scale, key factors that affect community 
design are the overall pattern and size of blocks 
and the frequency of through streets, as well as 
the overall dispersion and intensity of uses. These 
factors in turn affect the accessibility and walk-
ability of the City – for example, more frequent 
connections can enable easier navigation between 
places, whereas less frequent connections may 
require circuitous travel (even between physi-
cally close locations). The design and the quality 
of the public realm also play a key role in shap-
ing the everyday living and working experience. 
This includes the design of streets and sidewalks, 
parks and plazas, tree canopy and shade, the rela-
tionship of buildings to streets and sidewalks, and 
building design and appearance. 
The goals and policies for community character 
and design address overall community character 
and design at different scales – from city form 
and block size to how people navigate the city and 
utilize and experience the public realm. The goals 
and policies presented in this section emphasize 
connectivity, clarity, comfort, and creativity in the 
design of buildings and the public realm.
WHAT IS THE PUBLIC REALM?
The term ‘public realm’ refers to all parts of 
the built environment where the public has 
free and unrestricted access.

SENSE OF PLACE
“Sense of place” commonly refers to how an area 
is perceived by residents and visitors, and, in turn, 
this perception largely determines how residents 
and visitors navigate and interact with the area. 
A strong sense of place not only renders an area 
readily identifiable but also inspires feelings of 
attachment and pride. Areas with a strong sense 
of place tend to hold more meaning and value for 
both residents and visitors. Conversely, people 
tend to feel less invested in areas that lack a 
strong sense of place.   
A fundamental goal of the RC is to facilitate pri-
vate development and public improvements that 
imbue a strong sense of place to not only provide 
Oceanside with a distinct and positive identity, but 
also to neighborhood identity. Fostering a strong 
sense of place throughout Oceanside can better 
connect residents and visitors to their surround-
ings, promote civic identity and engagement, and 
foster community pride.  
Areas that provide a strong sense of place tend 
to be unified around conspicuous and compatible 
visual themes, which are evoked by architectural 
details, finish materials, color schemes, lighting, 
signage, paving, landscaping, and other interven-
tions. Settings that convey a strong sense of place 
also tend to have distinct boundaries and defined 
points of entry, which not only distinguish them 
from other areas but also serve to orient and 
guide those who move through them.

A strong sense of place is often reinforced by 
interactive 
elements, 
including 
storefronts 
that allow for window shopping, public art that 
engages the intellect and the imagination, street 
furnishings that allow for observation and con-
versation, playgrounds that encourage physical 
activity, kiosks that provide information such as 
weather forecasts, transit schedules, and calen-
dars of upcoming events, and music that compels 
us to tap our feet, clap our hands, and dance. 
Additionally, settings that embrace and draw 
attention to their history also tend to create a 
strong sense of place.  
A strong sense of place provides residents and 
visitors with enriching experiences, interesting 
stories, and a sense of belonging. Residents and 
business owners in areas that exude a strong 
sense of place are typically more engaged in pro-
tecting and enhancing these areas, while visitors 
are more motivated to linger in such settings, 
return to them, and share their experience of 
them with others. These realities have important 
implications for public health and safety, commu-
nity building, and economic development.
SIGNAGE 
Signage of various types often has a prominent 
presence in the built environment, particularly in 
commercial districts and along major roadways. 
Essential for public safety, wayfinding, marketing, 
and public information, signage is protected by the 
1st Amendment of the U.S. Constitution, which 
classifies signage as a form of protected speech 
and limits the ability of local governments to reg-
ulate sign content. However, local governments 
can establish policies and standards that promote 
tasteful, high-quality signage that furthers their 
urban design goals. 
Through the thoughtful regulation of the various 
aspects of signage – i.e., amount, size, placement, 
and type – communities can create a safer, more 
navigable, and more attractive public realm. When 
not properly regulated, signage can be distracting, 
disorienting, and unsightly. Signage that impedes 
adequate sight distance can create undo risk for 
pedestrians, bicyclists, and motorists. Signage that 
obscures and/or conflicts with building architec-
ture and landscaping can diminish curb appeal and 
the surrounding streetscape. Signage exhibiting

uninspired design and low-quality materials can 
not only create a sterile and unappealing environ-
ment but also reflect poorly on the businesses the 
signage seeks to promote. Moreover, such signage 
can be more susceptible to vandalism, as it tends 
to convey a lack of care and attention. Conversely, 
well-designed and well-maintained signage can 
contribute to the safety, comfort, visual quality 
and positive branding of a community. Indeed, 
providing first-rate, context-sensitive signage is 
one of the best ways in which businesses, resi-
dential developments, public uses, and the City 
itself can add charm and vibrancy to the public 
realm and, in turn, foster community identity and 
pride. The Remarkable Community Element there-
fore encourages the City to take a conscientious 
approach to the regulation of signage, recognizing 
that the overall quality of signage in Oceanside is a 
reflection of community character. 
The City’s signage regulations for non-residential 
uses have historically been very accommodating, 
allowing a significant amount of total sign area 
and placing few limitations on design, lighting, and 
materials. As a result, some stretches of the City’s 
commercial corridors are dominated by banal and 
oversized signage that detracts from a sense of 
place. Plastic-faced, internally-illuminated cabinet 
signs are common. Pole and pylon signs often dis-
play an abundance of text in different fonts and 
colors. Sign materials and textures often differ 
from base materials and textures (e.g., plastic 
signs attached to stone or brick structures). While 
the City requires comprehensive sign programs 
for large commercial centers, to ensure some 
level of quality and consistency, these programs 
aren’t always adhered to when signs are subse-
quently added or replaced. The City can promote 
improvements over time through updated stan-
dards, diligent review of sign permit applications, 
and amortization of non-conforming signs. 
While there are different opinions about what consti-
tutes tasteful, high-quality signage, communities with 
a strong commitment to urban design tend to privi-
lege signs that exhibit the following characteristics:    
•	 Natural materials, rather than plastic and other 
composites 
	
— Metal 
	
— Stone 
	
— Wood 
	
— Porcelain and other ceramics 
	
— Acrylic and foam, when designed to mimic nat-
ural materials 
•	 Dimensional letters and logos, rather than flat panels 
	
— Channel letters 
	
— Pinned letters 
	
— Routed metal and wood 
	
— Cast metal 
•	 Painted signs 
•	 Indirect or halo lighting, rather than translucent 
face-lit cabinets or channel letters 
•	 Monument signs, rather than pole or pylon signs 
The signage types outlined above not only tend to be 
more aesthetically pleasing but also more environmen-
tally friendly and energy efficient than other signage 
types. In general, signage materials should be renew-
able and/or recyclable and lighting should utilize LED, 
compact fluorescent, and/or other technologies that 
achieve high per-watt luminosity. 
With respect to the amount, size, and type of sig-
nage allowed, the City should generally limit the total 
number and maximum dimension of signs to what is 
necessary to achieve adequate visibility. Signage should 
be proportional to the site area and the scale of devel-
opment. Pole and pylon signs should be limited to high-
way-oriented sites that would otherwise not be visible 
to potential customers. 
In terms of the placement of signs, by and large, com-
mercial signage should not be visible from or direct 
glare toward residential areas. On larger sites, sign clut-
ter should be avoided through a balance of wall signs 
and free-standing signs. Signs should be reserved for 
the most conspicuous building frontages.  
Illuminated signage should not contribute to ambient 
light or create light pollution. 
These guidelines are meant to provide for creativity 
and a diversity of styles that foster a visual environment 
that is at once rich, intelligible, and organized. The City 
is encouraged to work with applicants to ensure that 
proposed signage achieves its fundamental purpose of 
drawing attention and providing information without 
overwhelming or compromising the visual quality of its 
surroundings.

ACCESSIBILITY IN THE PUBLIC 
REALM
To create a truly equitable and enjoyable city, the 
public realm should be accessible to all community 
members, including children, elderly people, dis-
abled people, and those of limited means. People 
of all backgrounds should feel welcome in these 
areas. Oceanside’s diversity should be on display 
in the public realm – not only through the people 
who occupy it, but also through the physical fea-
tures that contribute to its ambience – architec-
ture, public art, signage, etc.  
Establishing and maintaining a welcoming public 
realm often involves the removal of both physi-
cal and psychological barriers to access and use. 
Such barriers include a disconnected street net-
work, high-speed vehicle traffic, a lack of active 
transportation infrastructure, a lack of greenery 
and tree canopy, buildings that do not engage 
the street, poor signage, poor lighting, and a lack 
of refuge for pedestrians (e.g., shade structures, 
outdoor furnishings). Sidewalks with uneven sur-
faces, a lack of curb ramps, and obstacles that 
require a lot of redirection can be difficult and 
stressful to navigate for all users, not just elderly 
and disabled people. While well-designed streets, 
sidewalks, and pedestrian connections are essen-
tial throughout the City, they are even more crit-
ical around high activity areas, such as schools, 
parks, and neighborhood commercial centers. The 
physical safety of all street users is addressed in 
full in the Integrated Mobility (IM) element.
“We have an obligation to ensure that public 
spaces are accessible, inclusive, and at the 
service of all community members who want 
to come together.” 
- Lisa Cholmondeley and Hao Ko, Designing 
Cities that Are Open and Equitable
SENSE OF SAFETY IN THE PUBLIC 
REALM
Everyone should feel safe and secure in public 
spaces. However, throughout the community 
engagement process in support of the General 
Plan Update, a significant percentage of residents 
stated that they often feel uncomfortable in public 
spaces in Oceanside, especially those that are 
unkempt or lack “eyes on the street.” This concern 
is particularly prevalent in the youth community. 
While many of Oceanside’s young people would 
like to spend more time in public spaces, they are 
wary of who they may encounter in dormant and 
un-surveilled areas. Finding ways to activate such 
areas is essential to creating a sense of safety and 
security.   
Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design 
(CPTED) is a crime prevention philosophy based 
on the theory that the proper design and effective 
use of the built environment, can lead to a reduc-
tion in the fear of crime as well as an improvement 
in the quality of life. CPTED works by decreasing 
a criminal’s ability to commit a crime and increas-
ing the chances that the crime will be seen and 
reported. CPTED goes beyond traditional security 
methods by naturally integrating security mea-
sures into the community. The goal of all CPTED 
applications is to increase the quality of life, 
decrease the fear of crime, and decrease crime. 
Because different people or groups may have 
varying perceptions of what makes a community 
feel safe, CPTED guidelines need to be tailored to 
response to a community’s context, diversity, and 
unique safety issues. CPTED principles should 
also be coupled with acknowledgment of racial 
profiling that results in disproportionate harm and 
violence to Black, Indigenous, Latinx, and Asian 
communities. 
Examples of CPTED interventions typically ref-
erence lighting, line of sight, and other visibility 
issues, clearly defined spaces, and access. For 
example, building exteriors, parking lots, and 
parking garages should be well lit. Property lines 
and private areas should be defined with plant-
ings, pavement treatments, short walls, or fences. 
Further, landscaping should be maintained and 
trimmed to avoid creating blind spots or hiding 
spots. The City of Oceanside will continue to 
apply these CPTED principles in the design of

Visual Resources
Oceanside’s coastline, natural landforms, ripar-
ian areas, parks, urban landscape, and vistas of 
nearby mountain ranges and the ocean contrib-
ute significantly to the city’s visual quality, as do 
the city’s well-kept residential neighborhoods, 
historic resources, and iconic buildings. However, 
the City’s aesthetic character is diminished by 
non-descript and poorly-maintained develop-
ment, disorganized land use patterns, inconsistent 
design themes, an abundance of surface parking 
abutting the street frontage, and a lack of tree 
canopy and plantable surface area. Moreover, the 
key gateways into the City—e.g. along Interstate 5 
and State Routes 76 and 78—are visually incoher-
ent. There are few markers that announce entry 
into the City, and those that exist generally lack 
visual interest and a consistent theme.
The City can enhance visual quality through 
greater attention to fundamental urban design 
principles, including pedestrian orientation, con-
nectivity, transparency, legibility, indoor-outdoor 
space, urban greening, and a synergetic mix of 
uses. The City can also foster a greater sense of 
neighborhood identity, encourage better stew-
ardship of both public and private space, estab-
lish form-based design standards, preserve and 
enhance scenic views, and integrate aesthetic 
upgrades into capital improvement projects.

atmosphere, capture rainwater, prevent erosion, 
protect the quality of surface waters, provide habi-
tat, make the public realm more pleasant, enhance 
a sense of place, reduce stress, beautify streets-

REMARKABLE COMMUNITY 2
A healthy urban forest, comprised of public and 
privately maintained trees throughout the com-
munity, is an integral component of a sustainable 
and equitable city. Yet trees in cities face a vari-
ety of threats, including increasing summer heat 
and frequent droughts, pests, storm events, chal-
lenges in funding maintenance costs, and some 
conflicts with underground and overhead utilities 
and development activity. Policies in this chap-
ter aim to sustain and enhance the city’s urban 
forest, a valuable environmental resource and an 
important component of a pleasant public realm 
experience.
Parkways
Many areas of the City feature parkways – i.e., 
spaces between the curb and sidewalk meant 
to accommodate landscape.  Adjacent property 
owners are responsible for maintaining parkways 
along their street frontage.  
When properly maintained, parkways provide a 
wide range of community benefits:
•	 Separating vehicle travel lanes and curbside 
parking from sidewalk, thereby enhancing 
pedestrian safety and comfort and contributing 
to walkability; 
•	 Supporting the urban forest, which provides 
shade, passive cooling, carbon sequestration, 
stormwater capture, and refuge for birds and 
other wildlife; 
•	 Maintaining a sense of openness and softening 
the appearance of the built environment; and
•	 Enhancing public health and wellness, as 
demonstrated by studies showing that spending 
time around trees and other plant life reduces 
stress, lowers blood pressure, and improves 
mood; and
•	 Increasing property values.
The City will amend its parkway ordinance to 
emphasize that parkways should be maintained as 
permeable and plantable area, with limited imper-
vious hardscape.

Visual characteristics and the effects of lighting and 
glare can have a disruptive impact on the environ-
ment and the quality of life in surrounding neigh-
borhoods. This effect is often referred to as light 
LIGHT POLLUTION COMPONENTS
Light pollution is excessive and inappropri-
ate artificial light. The four components of 
light pollution are often combined and may 
overlap:
•	 Urban Sky Glow: The brightening of the 
night sky over inhabited areas.
•	 Light Trespass: Light falling where it is not 
intended, wanted, or needed.
•	 Glare: Excessive brightness which causes 
visual discomfort. High levels of glare can 
decrease visibility.
•	 Clutter: Bright, confusing, and excessive 
groupings of light sources, commonly 
found in over-lit urban areas. The prolif-
eration of clutter contributes to urban sky 
glow, trespass, and glare.

GOALS AND POLICIES
GOALS 
2-A	
Oceanside’s built environment will be 
easy to navigate, visually appealing, and 
accommodating of social engagement, 
community building, physical activity, 
and cultural enrichment.
2-B	
Public spaces will be designed to wel-
come and safely accommodate all com-
munity members, including pedestrians, 
bicyclists, children, the elderly, and the 
disabled.
2-C	
The City’s built environment will convey 
a strong sense of place through the 
presence and thoughtful arrangement of 
essential and character-defining public 
realm improvements, fostering a sense of 
community and civic pride.
2-D	
The City’s natural environment and urban 
tree canopy will equitably enhance the 
city’s visual quality, mitigate the urban 
heat island effect, and improve the expe-
rience of the public realm. 
2-E	
Entry into Oceanside will be conspic-
uously and attractively announced at 
gateway locations and the City will work 
with neighboring jurisdictions to ensure 
that transitional areas within the built 
environment are consistently maintained 
and demarcated. 
2-F	
New development will contribute to the 
functionality, coherence, accessibility, 
visual quality, and unique character of the 
City’s built environment. 
POLICIES
Building Design
2-1	
All buildings should be sited and designed 
to engage the sidewalk and the public 
realm. While the design context and solu-
tions will vary across the city, in general 
this means ensuring that buildings are 
oriented to the street, promote pedes-
trian safety and “eyes on the street” with 
parking tucked away behind buildings, and 
provide linkages to pedestrian paths and 
direct access to transit where present. 
2-2	
Minimize the footprint of parking through 
innovative design, shared parking facilities, 
and transportation demand management 
strategies.
2-3	
Buildings on a corner should treat both 
street sides as “fronts,” adhere to the 
minimum and any maximum front setback 
requirements along both streets and 
include pedestrian-friendly building design 
features such as windows, doors, walk-
ways, and signage along both streets. 
2-4	
Street-facing 
elevations 
shall 
include 
conspicuous entrances that provide plane 
breaks and visual interest. 
2-5	
New development should exhibit “five-
sided architecture” that provides visual 
interest on all building elevations and 
visible roof areas.  
2-6	
Long wall planes shall be accented with 
fenestration and decorative elements 
– e.g., tile, grillwork, metal cladding, mold-
ings, color bands. 
2-7	
Vertical landscape elements shall be 
employed to soften the appearance of 
large buildings.  
2-8	
Trash enclosure walls and gates must 
be architecturally compatible with the 
building design and should be carefully 
integrated into the site plan.

Mixed Use Buildings
2-9	
Mixed use buildings should be responsive 
to adjacent neighborhoods and minimize 
impacts including from noise, reduced pri-
vacy, and shadows by stepping buildings 
back, placing windows to minimize privacy 
impacts, and siting noise generating uses 
away from existing residential or other 
sensitive uses. 
Retail and Commercial Buildings
2-10	
Large retail establishments on large lots 
(e.g., full-service grocery stores, home 
improvement stores, department stores) 
should utilize vertical space, incorporate 
ample fenestration, activate street front-
ages with outdoor merchandising and 
ancillary uses, and provide pedestrian 
access through the site via arcades and 
pathways.   
2-11	
In commercial districts, encourage more 
intense and higher-profile development at 
major street intersections.  
2-12	
Require conspicuous primary entrances 
that are either recessed or accented with 
canopies or other architectural elements.
2-13	
Require that buildings located at street 
corners include corner entrances, mitered 
windows, and/or other architectural ele-
ments that avoid or soften sharp angles 
at the ground level and enhance sight 
distance for pedestrians.
2-14	
To the extent feasible, screen parking from 
the street frontage. Appropriate screening 
methods include landscaping, berms, grill-
work, decorative fencing, etc.
2-15	
Promote shared parking facilities that pro-
vide a “park once” experience for patrons.
2-16	
Encourage retail façade design that both 
supports window display and discourages 
the storage of merchandise in areas visible 
from public spaces.
2-17	
Ensure that buildings are designed to allow 
for the architectural integration of signage.
Industrial Buildings
2-18	
Minimum front yard setbacks in industrial 
districts should be determined by site 
design elements. Industrial projects that 
locate parking away from the street front-
age, exceed minimum landscaping and tree 
canopy requirements, and incorporate 
enhanced pedestrian amenities should 
benefit from reduced front yard setbacks.  
2-19	
Industrial buildings shall be composed of 
sturdy materials that convey permanence 
– e.g., precast concrete, masonry, stone, 
brick.  
2-20	
Industrial projects should maximize vertical 
space. Multistory industrial development 
is encouraged.   
2-21	
Industrial buildings shall be designed to 
accommodate all industrial processes 
within enclosed and adequately insulated 
spaces.   
2-22	
Outdoor storage shall be limited and 
allowed only when fully screened from 
public spaces. 
2-23	
Loading and service areas shall be substan-
tially screened from public spaces. 
2-24	
Industrial projects shall provide conspicuous 
paths of travel for pedestrians and utilize 
enhanced paving to distinguish pedestrian 
paths within vehicle circulation areas. 
2-25	
Industrial projects should consider the 
integration of public art, consistent with the 
visual character of the surrounding area.

2.3  Cultural and Historic 
Resources
Historic resources are important to Oceanside’s 
unique identity, and few Southern California com-
munities can lay claim to the sense of place and 
history Oceanside has managed to retain during a 
century of development. The General Plan pres-
ents actions and principles to harmonize histor-
ic preservation with the demands of continued 
growth and evolution.
HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Native American Settlement
Oceanside has a rich history of Native American 
settlement dating back thousands of years. The 
area that now comprises Oceanside is part of 
the traditional territory of the Payomkawichum 
people, a Shoshone- speaking tribal group. The 
Payomkawichum are among five distinguishable 
Native American groups that were present in the 
San Diego region at the time of Spanish contact 
in 1542, when Juan Rodriguez Cabrillo and his 
crew became the first Europeans to sail into San 
Diego Bay. Archaeological evidence suggests the 
Payomkawichum primarily settled along the San 
Luis Rey River and other local watercourses. Upon 
the founding of Mission San Luis Rey de Francia 
in 1798, Spanish missionaries imposed the name 
“Luiseno” on the local Native American community.   
Spanish Colonization 
Following European settlement of the San Diego 
region, the Luiseno and other indigenous groups 
were subject to violence, disease, displacement, 
and the destruction of their customs and way 
of life. Smallpox and malaria epidemics swept 
through Native American communities in the 
region, decimating their populations. It is estimat-
ed that the Luiseno population was reduced by 90 
percent between 1769 and 1848.   
Mexican Period
During the Mexican Period (1832-1848), the 
Luiseno were forced to serve as laborers on Rancho 
Santa Marqarita y Flores, Rancho Monserrate, 
Rancho Guajome, Rancho Aqua Hedionda, and 
Rancho Buena Vista. When California became a 
state in 1850, few Luisenos had land title docu-
ments, and the tribe was afforded no property 
rights under American law. By 1870, much of 
Oceanside had been homesteaded by white set-
tlers from other parts of the country, thwarting 
efforts by the San Luis Rey Band to establish a 
federally-recognized 
reservation. 
Today, 
the 
descendants of the City’s original inhabitants – 
members of the Luiseno Band of Mission Indians 
and other Luiseno tribes in the region – continue 
to reside in the Oceanside area.

Statehood/Homesteading
In the 1890s, Oceanside became a popular week-
end retreat for rancho families living in the warmer 
inland areas. At that time, the City had roughly 
1,000 residents. The City featured three hotels (the 
South Pacific, the St. Cloud, and the Tremont), two 
drug-stores, two livery stables, two blacksmiths, a 
hardware store, a bakery, a harness shop, a lumber 
yard, a barber shop, a newspaper, and a school.    
Incorporation (1888)
Incorporated in 1888, Oceanside is the third 
oldest municipality in the San Diego region. While 
most of the City’s original building stock has 
been demolished and replaced, there are several 
buildings and structures from this period that still 
stand, including the Schuyler Building and Bunker 
House in the Downtown District, the Graves “Top 
Gun” House (now incorporated into the Mission 
Pacific Hotel), and the Rorick Houses on South 
Pacific Street.  
Camp Pendleton
Established during World War II, U.S. Marine 
Corps Base Camp Pendleton is the largest marine 
installation in the United States. In its first 20 years 
of existence, Camp Pendleton spurred significant 
population growth in Oceanside; between 1940 
and 1960, the City’s population grew from rough-
ly 4,700 residents to nearly 25,000 residents. 
REGULATORY CONTEXT
Federal Regulations
National Historic Preservation Act of 1966
The National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) was 
signed into law in 1966. The act seeks to preserve 
historic and archaeological sites across the United 
States. The NHPA solidified the role of the National 
Parks Service as lead agency in the historic preser-
vation program and created cooperative partners 
in the process, including the Advisory Council on 
Historic Preservation, State Historic Preservation 
Offices and Tribal Historic Preservation Offices.
The NHPA was amended in 1980 to create the 
Certified 
Local 
Government 
(CLG) 
program, 
administered through the California State Office 
of Historic Preservation. This program allows for 
direct local government participation and integra-
tion in a comprehensive statewide historic pres-
ervation planning process. Cities and counties 
with CLG status may compete for preservation 
funds allocated by the United States Congress and 
awarded to each state. Oceanside is currently des-
ignated a CLG.
National Register of Historic Places
The National Register of Historic Places is the 
nation’s official list of historic places. The register is 
overseen by the National Park Service and requires 
that a property or resource eligible for listing on 
the register meet one of the following four criteria 
at the national, state, or local level to ensure integ-
rity and obtain official designation.
•	 Property is associated with events that have made 
a significant contribution to the broad patterns of 
our history.
•	 Property is associated with the lives of persons 
significant in our past. Eligible properties based on 
this criterion are generally those associated with 
the productive life of the individual in the field in 
which they achieved significance.
•	 Property embodies the distinctive characteristics 
of a type, period, or method of construction or 
represents the work of a master, or possesses 
high artistic values, or represents a significant and 
distinguishable entity whose components lack 
individual distinction.

National Environmental Policy Act of 1969
The National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) 
was signed into law in 1970. NEPA created an 
environmental review process requiring federal 
agencies to consider the effects of their actions 
on the environment. Under NEPA, all federal 
agencies must carry out their regulations, policies 
and programs in accordance with NEPA’s policies 
for environmental protection, including project 
compliance with Section 106 of the NHPA, the 
purpose of which is to identify historic properties, 
assess the effects a proposed action might have, 
and provide ways to avoid, minimize, or mitigate 
any adverse effect that may occur.
State Regulations
California Environmental Quality Act of 
1970
The 
California 
Environmental 
Quality 
Act 
(CEQA) was enacted in 1970. This act requires 
that state and local agencies in California eval-
uate the environmental implications of their 
actions. Furthermore, CEQA requires that cultural 
resources be considered when assessing the envi-
ronmental impact of a proposed project, and that 
agencies avoid or reduce the project’s significant 
impact(s) to these resources.
Senate Bill 18
Enacted in 2005, California Senate Bill 18 requires 
cities and counties to notify and consult with 
California Native American tribes regarding pro-
posed local land use planning decisions for the 
purpose of protecting traditional tribal cultural 
places (otherwise known as sacred sites), prior to 
adopting or amending a general plan or designat-
ing land as open space. Once tribes are contacted, 
they have 90 days to request consultation.
California Register of Historic Resources
The California Office of Historic Preservation 
maintains the California Register, which is the 
authoritative guide to the state’s significant his-
toric and archaeological resources. The program 
provides for the identification, evaluation, reg-
istration and protection of California’s historic 
resources. The California Register has established 
criteria for evaluating the eligibility of a proper-
ty or resource for listing. The four criteria are as 
follows:
•	 The property is associated with events that have 
made a significant contribution to the broad 
patterns of local or regional history, or the cultural 
heritage of California or the United States;
•	 The property is associated with the lives of 
persons important to local, California, or national 
history; 
•	 The 
property 
embodies 
the 
distinctive 
characteristics of a type, period, or method of 
construction or represents the work of a master, 
or possesses high artistic values;
•	 The property has yielded, or is likely to yield, 
information important to prehistory or history of 
the local area, California or the nation. 
Similar to the National Register of Historic Places, 
eligibility to be listed on the California Register 
requires an establishment of physical integrity, 
including the seven aspects previously described 
for the National Register, although California’s list 
of special considerations is less stringent.

San Luis Rey Historic Area Development 
Program and Design Guidelines 
In November 1986, the City adopted the San Luis 
Rey Historic Area Development Program and 
Design Guidelines for the Mission San Luis Rey 
Historic Area. The program establishes planned 
land uses, infrastructure needs, development reg-
ulations, and design guidelines for the Mission. It 
is meant to serve as a tool to guide future planning 
efforts on the property and will aid the Mission’s 
decision makers in directing future develop-
ment. The Plan will also serve as an information 
source and regulatory framework for the City of 
Oceanside in evaluating future growth proposals. 
The Plan incorporates the best available com-
ponents and practices into a design which will 
complement existing structures and protect the 
significant historical uses and resources on the 
property. Land uses are proposed which will gen-
erate revenue to repair and maintain the Mission’s 
structures and grounds, and which are closely 
connected to the Franciscan ethic and the histor-
ical role of the Missions (i.e., care for the elderly 
and indigent, education, a place of interaction 
and multicultural learning, a location of trade and 
commerce, a stopover for travelers, and a place 
for spiritual growth and conversion).
Mission SLR and Historic Area
Known as the “King of the Missions,” Mission San Luis 
Rey de Francia was founded in 1798 by Franciscan 
missionary Fermin de Lasuen. In 1833, the Mexican 
government secularized the California missions and 
sold off much of the surrounding land. Over time, a 
small pioneer settlement emerged around the mis-
sion. Known as San Luis Rey Township, the settle-
ment had a post office, a hotel, a general store, and 
a newspaper called the “San Luis Rey Star.” Many of 
the first inhabitants of the township were buried in 
Pioneer Cemetery, located near the intersection of 
State Route 76 and Rancho del Oro Road.
Carefully restored, the mission remains an active 
center of religious worship and community events. 
In 1986, the City established the Mission San Luis 
Rey Historic Area, which extends along Mission 
Avenue between El Camino Real and Old Grove 
Road. New development within the Historic Area is 
governed by design standards that specify Spanish 
Colonial and Mission Revival architecture
Coast Highway
The Pacific Coast Highway, California U.S. Route 
101 (or Highway 1 in some areas) stretches along 
the Pacific Ocean for 790 miles, from San Diego all 
the way to the Oregon border. The stretch of Coast 
Highway through Oceanside is a locally designated 
historic resource. The City is looking to restore a 
pedestrian-friendly environment on Coast Highway 
with complete streets improvements that include a 
“road diet” and roundabout intersections.

CULTURAL RESOURCES 
In addition to important historic resources, the City 
is defined by important cultural resources that draw 
visitors and residents alike. In 2018, Oceanside was 
recognized as one of 14 inaugural Cultural Districts 
by the California Arts Council. This designation high-
lights the significance of Oceanside’s rich cultural 
diversity and unique artistic identity, embodied by 
such institutions as the Sunshine Brooks Theater and 
the Oceanside Museum of Art.
Junior Seau Pier Amphitheater
The Junior Seau Pier Amphitheater (Oceanside 
Bandshell) is a public entertainment venue locat-
ed at the foot of the Oceanside Pier. The theater 
has a total capacity of approximately 2,500 people 
(1,500 seated) and has been used to host large 
concert events, movies, awards ceremonies, high 
school graduations, and cultural events. The the-
ater was constructed in 1937 as a project of the 
federal Works Progress Administration and sig-
nificantly renovated in 1950. The City is currently 
conducting a feasibility study for the Oceanside 
Bandshell. The study will identify and analyze the 
existing site conditions and evaluate site opportu-
nities and constraints for the area.
Oceanside Museum of Art
The Oceanside Museum of Art (704 Pier View Way) 
is a fine arts museum that hosts art focused on works 
by contemporary artists of southern California. 
Works exhibited include paintings, sculpture, fur-
niture, textile art, and glass. The museum typically 
features over a dozen modern and contemporary 
art exhibitions each year. In addition to its exhibit 
offerings, the museum hosts a number of communi-
ty programs including school visits, youth and family 
programs, adult classes and lectures, and a book club.
In 2019, the museum launched a partnership with SD 
Malkin Properties, Inc. (SDMP) to curate art for the 
developer’s two beachfront resorts, the Joie de Vivre 
Hotel and the Destination Resort. The museum has 
also hosted offsite exhibitions at such locations as 
the New Village Arts Theater, L Street Gallery, and 
North Coast Repertory Theater. In 2020, the museum 
began offering virtual museum experiences including 
virtual studio visits, artist talks, and workshops. The 
museum opened its dedicated facility, two buildings 
designed by Irving Gill and Frederick Fisher, in 1997. 
The museum has received funding and support from 
a variety of sources, including the Oceanside Arts 
Commission, Oceanside Charitable Foundation, and 
James Irvine Foundation.
Sunshine Brooks Theater
The Sunshine Brooks Theater (217 N Coast 
Highway) was founded in 1936 (under the name 
the Margo) and acquired by the City of Oceanside 
in 1993. The theater hosts a youth theater arts 
academy, concerts, art exhibits, and a number of 
theatrical productions. The Oceanside Theatre 
Company is the current professional production 
company in residence at the theater.
The Sunshine Brooks Theater receives financial 
support from the public in the form of tax-deduct-
ible donations.

California Surf Museum
The California Surf Museum was founded in 1986 
to showcase the international history of surfing. 
Consisting of both permanent and revolving exhib-
its, the museum’s collections represent one of the 
most comprehensive collections of surfing history. 
Museum materials include surfboards, surf art and 
equipment, photographs, magazines, and video. The 
museum is visited by approximately 20,000 visitors 
per year.
In 1995, the City of Oceanside offered the California 
Surf Museum a space at the corner of North Coast 
Highway and Pier View Way, where it stayed until 
2009. In 2006, the City of Oceanside offered the 
museum a new and larger space at 312 Pier View 
Way. In 2009, the museum moved into the renovated 
facility. 
In addition to its exhibits, the museum has hosted 
a variety of events including book signings, events 
honoring luminaries of the surfing community, surf 
film festival, and an annual gala. In 2007, the museum 
drew upon its decades-long collection of stories and 
images to publish a photo book, Surfing in San Diego, 
the first attempt to document the history of surfing 
in the county. In 2011, the California Surf Museum 
was awarded the Museum Assessment grant by the 
American Association of Museums.

REMARKABLE COMMUNITY 2
2.4  Oceanside as a 
Visitor Destination 
Oceanside’s three miles of coastline and unique 
coastal amenities, along with a variety of inland 
attractions, make the City an attractive visitor 
destination. The City’s hospitality sector has 
burgeoned over the past two decades, with the 
emergence of new hotels, restaurants, and other 
visitor-serving uses. These visitor-oriented venues 
and services can be linked together in various ways 
to provide a rich and multifaceted visitor experi-
ence. The RC outlines goals and policies designed 
to leverage and integrate Oceanside’s many assets 
to not only attract visitors and improve the local 
economy but also to promote resource conserva-
tion, multimodal mobility options, visual quality, 
and other positive outcomes that contribute to 
quality of life and a sustainable future.
WHAT MAKES OCEANSIDE 
UNIQUE 
Regional Gateway
Situated at the northwest corner of the San 
Diego region, Oceanside is an important gateway 
through which travelers from points north and 
east access the region’s many visitor destinations. 
The city’s gateway location presents both oppor-
tunities and challenges. Oceanside is not only the 
most convenient coastal community in the region 
to reach from other parts of southern California 
but also one of the region’s most affordable visi-
tor destinations. Conversely, being situated at the 
periphery of the metropolitan area and bounded 
by the Pacific Ocean and large tracts of open 
space, Oceanside is a somewhat isolated com-
mercial market. Moreover, many visitors to the 
region simply pass through Oceanside on their 
way to San Diego Safari Park, Legoland, Del Mar 
Racetrack, Sea World, Balboa Park, and other 
renowned attractions.  
Transit Hub
The California Southern Railway put Oceanside 
on the map over 135 years ago, and today the City 
features one of the most robust rail hubs in the 
state – the Oceanside Transit Center (OTC), oper-
ated by the North County Transit District (NCTD). 
The OTC supports four separate rail services 
– Amtrak, Metrolink, and NCTD’s Coaster and 
Sprinter – along with NCTD’s Breeze bus service. 
The OTC is slated for redevelopment, with much 
of the site expected to accommodate housing, 
retail, and lodging. The City is working with NCTD 
to ensure that the redeveloped OTC supports the 
needs of transit users and provides for so-called 
“first-mile/last-mile” connections between the 
transit center and other parts of Oceanside. 
The OTC has the potential to become a key driver 
of Oceanside’s hospitality sector, providing vis-
itors with alternative means of accessing and 
moving about the city. As an important gateway 
into Oceanside, the OTC can serve to oriented vis-
itors to the City’s history, culture, and amenities.
Pacific Ocean, Coastline, and Local 
Watersheds
Oceanside boasts three miles of coastline on the 
Pacific Ocean, offering surf breaks, boating and fish-
ing opportunities, spectacular sunsets, and views of 
Santa Catalina Island. The City’s stretch of coastline 
features the outlets of three watersheds – the San 
Luis Rey River, Loma Alta Creek, and Buena Vista 
Creek and Lagoon. These watersheds provide coast-
al wetland habitat for a variety of sensitive plant 
and wildlife species. Buena Vista Creek and Lagoon 
define much of the city’s southern boundary.

Coast Highway
The Coast Highway corridor, a locally designated 
historic resource, is being reimagined as a walk-
able pedestrian corridor within Oceanside. As 
portions of the corridor redevelop, Coast Highway 
is expected to become an increasingly popular 
shopping and dining destination for residents and 
visitors alike.
Unique Coastal Amenities
Oceanside is the only city on the north coast of 
San Diego County with a harbor, a pier, and a 
public road on the beachfront (The Strand). The 
City’s waterfront also includes a community 
center and an amphitheater. The City hosts a wide 
variety of public events in the coastal area, includ-
ing the Beach Soccer Championships, Race Across 
America, and the Ironman Triathlon. 
The Oceanside Small Craft Harbor offers whale 
watching and sportfishing charters, kayak and 
small boat rentals, and a variety of retail and dining 
establishments. The harbor includes over 850 
boat slips, 85 of which provide for live-aboards.   
In addition to its coastal infrastructure, Oceanside 
offers some of the most consistent surf breaks 
in California and has become an internationally 
recognized surfing destination. The California 
Surf Museum located in Oceanside curates a 
permanent collection of surfboards and surf 
paraphernalia, offers several revolving exhibits 
each year, and hosts lectures, book signings, film 
screenings, and many other events. Oceanside 
also hosts surf contests throughout the year, 
including the Nissan Super Girl Surf Pro and the 
World Bodysurfing Championships.
Culinary Scene
Situated in one of the nation’s most celebrated 
craft brewing regions, Oceanside is home to more 
than 10 local breweries and several tap houses. 
The City’s microbreweries and tap houses bring 
locals together while also drawing visitors from 
throughout southern California. A hallmark of craft 
brewing in Oceanside and the San Diego region 
is collaboration, with many brewers emphasizing 
community over competition.   
Oceanside’s culinary scene ranges from Mexican 
takeout and traditional breakfast hangouts to 
Michelin starred eateries. Most of the City’s 
notable dining establishments embrace “flip-flop 
dining,” i.e., creative, high-quality fare served in a 
casual atmosphere. Several of the City’s restau-
rants source ingredients from local farms and fish-
eries, and farm-to-table culinary events are hosted 
by local businesses and community groups. The 
City’s culinary scene is showcased every week at 
the Sunset Market, which features dozens of food 
stands offering cuisine from across the globe.  
El Corazon
Bequeathed to the City by the U.S. Silica Mining 
Company in 1994, the 465-acre El Corazon prop-
erty is in the process of becoming one of the 
region’s premier recreational venues. The site cur-
rently accommodates a senior center, an aquatics 
center, the So Cal Sports Complex, and a trails 
network through preserved habitat areas. The 
site includes an arena with capacity for approxi-
mately 7,500 people. At its onset, the arena will 
be the home of the San Diego Sockers of the 
Major Arena Soccer League.

REMARKABLE COMMUNITY 2
Guajome Regional Park
Just eight miles from the Pacific Ocean, Guajome 
Regional Park is close to many natural and his-
toric points of interest. The park features multi-
use, non-motorized trails that meander through 
diverse Southern California habitats such as 
woodlands, chaparral, wetlands and mixed grass-
lands. Two ponds attract migratory birds and 
serve as home to a variety of fish. Guajome Lake 
Park features both day use and overnight camping 
areas with amenities including playgrounds, bas-
ketball courts, and picnic areas.
San Luis Rey River and Coastal Trails
The San Luis Rey River (SLR) Trail runs for nearly 
11 miles along the banks of the San Luis Rey River, 
starts a few blocks from the beach and the OTC and 
reaches North Santa Fe Avenue to the east. The 
multi-use trail, which is smooth and mostly flat, is 
separated from motorized traffic and used for com-
muters and visitors hoping to explore Oceanside by 
bike or on foot. While the trail is already a valuable 
asset to Oceanside, improvements in safety and 
accessibility will ensure that the trail can serve an 
even broader audience. 
The Coastal Rail Trail is a 44-mile planned bike trail 
that will connect the cities of Oceanside and San 
Diego along the railroad right-of-way. Currently, 
there are segments of the trail completed within 
Oceanside, Carlsbad and Solana Beach, including 
a portion of the trail that begins at the Oceanside 
Transit Center and continues to Oceanside Blvd. 
The completed trail in Oceanside is designated a 
class 1 bike path and is separated from vehicular 
traffic. Completing the City’s segment of the Coastal 
Rail Trail will connect pedestrians and bicyclists to 
active transportation facilities on Camp Pendleton 
and Carlsbad and thereby provide linkages to a wide 
range of coastal destinations outside of the City.
South Morro Hills/Agritourism
Situated in the northeast corner of Oceanside, the 
South Morro Hills agricultural district occupies 
nearly 15 percent of the City’s total land area.  
South Morro Hills features rolling topography 
and panoramic views of the San Luis Rey Valley 
extending through to the ocean.  In addition to 
crop production, South Morro Hills accommo-
dates estate housing, equestrian facilities, a 
winery, and a wedding venue.    
With the local farming community facing high 
input costs, labor shortages, and increasing for-
eign competition, there is considerable market 
pressure to convert farmland to residential use.  
To provide local farmers with additional revenue 
streams, the City recently amended its agricultural 
zoning standards to allow for “Tier 1” agritourism 
uses, including visitor accommodations, restau-
rants, and wineries, breweries, and distilleries.  
The City is now working with community mem-
bers to provide for catalytic “Tier 2” agritourism 
uses that render South Morro Hills a true visitor 
destination.  
Accessibility and Affordability
Relative to other cities in the San Diego region, 
Oceanside presents an accessible and affordable 
option for visitors to enjoy the bounty of southern 
California. With a range of accommodations and 
visitor destinations, as well as transit accessibility 
at the OTC, Oceanside remains a uniquely afford-
able destination in the San Diego region. While 
growth of the tourism industry in Oceanside will 
lead to some increases in average costs, the city 
will prioritize balancing affordability and growth 
as it continues to expand the hospitality sector.

2.5  Neighborhood 
Planning Areas
A fundamental goal of the Remarkable 
Community Element is to facilitate private 
development and public improvements that 
imbue a strong sense of place that not only 
provides Oceanside with a distinct and positive 
identity but also serves to distinguish the City’s 
neighborhoods and districts from one another.  
Many Oceanside residents have a strong attach-
ment to their neighborhoods and the quality of 
life they afford.  In response to an online survey 
conducted in December 2020, residents indi-
cated what they most value about their neigh-
borhoods: open space and landscaping, scenic 
views, community facilities, access to commercial 
services and dining establishments, and a distinc-
tive built environment.  In turn, they expressed 
concern about visual blight in the public right-of-
way, limited pedestrian and bicycle facilities, cut-
through traffic, and the potential impacts of new 
development. 
The City can preserve and enhance the characteris-
tic features of residential neighborhoods by imple-
menting the RC’s urban design goals and policies, 
as well as those goals and policies that specifically 
address Oceanside’s 18 neighborhood planning 
areas.  The City can also explore ways to render 
community facilities (e.g., parks, resource centers, 
schools) more useful and accessible to residents, 
who often cite these facilities as contributing sig-
nificantly to neighborhood identity and cohesion.

EASTSIDE 
Situated immediately east of Interstate 5 and 
extending inland between Highway 76 and Mission 
Avenue, the Eastside neighborhood has been home 
to a diverse working-class population for more than 
100 years.  When the neighborhood was subdivided 
in the late 1880s, it constituted the inland edge of 
Oceanside. Eastside has since welcomed Hispanic, 
Black, and Pacific Islander residents who have estab-
lished churches and other grassroots institutions.   
Key features of this NPA include Lawrence Canyon, 
Balderrama Park and the Chavez Resource Center, 
the Rosicrucian Fellowship, and a number of public 
schools.  A small neighborhood-serving commercial 
district extends along San Diego Street between 
Bush Street and Mission Avenue, and a variety of 
commercial uses occupy the north side of Mission 
Avenue between Interstate 5 and Canyon Drive.  
Eastern portions of the neighborhood include 
industrial zoning districts occupied primarily by auto 
salvage yards and recycling facilities.
The natural environment in Eastside Capistrano 
includes a segment of the San Luis Rey River, Lawrence 
Canyon, and undeveloped slopes on the south side of 
State Route 76.  These areas provide coastal sage scrub 
and riparian woodland habitat.  Portions of Eastside fall 
within the City’s Wildlife Corridor Planning Zone. 
As indicated by the CalEnviroScreen 4.0, many Eastside 
residents experience poverty, a high housing cost 
burden, low educational attainment, linguistic isolation, 
and high exposure to traffic-related pollution and haz-
ardous waste.  However, the neighborhood has seen a 
significant reduction in overall crime rates over the past 
20 years.
Western portions of Eastside have considerable devel-
opment potential, with many properties that currently 
accommodate single-family homes able to support 
attached housing at densities up to 29 dwelling units 
per acre.  Moreover, the Lawrence Canyon area could 
provide for hundreds of new housing units as well as an 
array of neighborhood and visitor-serving commercial 
uses.

CAPISTRANO
Situated immediately east of Interstate 5 and 
extending eastward between Camp Pendleton 
and the San Luis Rey River, the Capistrano neigh-
borhood features small lot single-family homes 
constructed in the early 1950s.  Much of this 
housing originally accommodated military person-
nel and their families, and the neighborhood con-
tinues to maintain strong ties to Camp Pendleton.  
Key features of the Capistrano neighbor-
hood include Capistrano Park, North Terrace 
Elementary School (located on Camp Pendleton), 
and informal trails along the northern bank of the 
San Luis Rey River.  The only public open space in 
the neighborhood, Capistrano Park provides both 
active and passive recreational opportunities in a 
tranquil and picturesque setting. 
Situated on a mesa, much of the neighborhood 
affords views of the coastline and the San Luis 
Rey Valley.  Properties along the southern and 
eastern perimeter of the neighborhood abut large 
tracts of open space.  
Relative to other Oceanside neighborhoods, 
Capistrano is somewhat isolated.  There are only 
two points of vehicular entry into the neighbor-
hood (Northbound Interstate 5 and Harbor Drive/
San Rafael Drive) and only one point of vehicle 
egress from the neighborhood (San Rafael Drive/
Harbor Drive).  Interstate 5 limits pedestrian and 
bicycle access to coastal areas, and existing pedes-
trian bridge beneath the freeway is uninviting

AIRPORT
Located within a western reach of the San Luis Rey 
Valley, the Airport neighborhood extends north-
ward from Mission Avenue to the City’s boundary 
with Camp Pendleton.  Airport is bisected by both 
the San Luis Rey River and State Route 76, which 
divide the planning area into distinct sections: sin-
gle-family subdivisions and open space to the north 
of the river, Oceanside Municipal Airport/Bob 
Maxwell Field between the river and the highway, 
and the Oceanside Industrial Park and a segment of 
the Mission Avenue commercial district in the south-
ernmost portion of the planning area. In addition to 
the Oceanside Municipal Airport, character-defining 
features of the Airport neighborhood include Prince 
of Peace Abbey, Alex Road Skate Park, Marlado 
Highlands Park, Hubbert Lake, and a segment of the 
San Luis Rey River Trail. The southeastern corner of 
the planning area is slated to accommodate a wave 
park with lodging and residential use.
There are many barriers to north-south connectiv-
ity in Airport, including Highway 76, the San Luis 
Rey River, the airport, and steep slopes.  The City is 
working with Caltrans and SANDAG to evaluate the 
feasibility of grade-separated crossings of Highway 
76, possibly at Benet Road and/or Foussat Road.  
These two roadways cross the San Luis Rey River.  
With no designated bikes lanes and only a narrow 
sidewalk on one side, neither the Benet Road Bridge 
nor the Foussat Road Bridge provide a comfortable 
experience for pedestrians and bicyclists.   
Much of the neighborhood lies within the City’s 
Wildlife Corridor Planning Zone, which is designed 
to connect the Lake Calavera Preserve in Carlsbad 
with wildland on Marine Corps Base Camp 
Pendleton.  Sensitive habitat is located within the 
San Luis Rey River, Tuley Canyon, and along the 
City’s boundary with Camp Pendleton.

land uses is therefore an important consideration.
TOWNSITE
Encompassing the 160-acre land grant acquired 
in 1883 by Oceanside’s founder Andrew Jackson 
Myers, the Townsite NPA lies west of Interstate 
5 and extends along the coastline between 
the City’s northern boundary and Oceanside 
Boulevard.  Townsite includes many of the City’s 
oldest neighborhoods and dozens of designat-
ed and potentially significant historic resources.  
Several of Oceanside’s most iconic and charac-
ter-defining features lie within Townsite – e.g., 
two miles of coastline, the Oceanside Municipal 
Pier, the Oceanside Small Craft Harbor, and the 
City’s original and current City Hall complexes.
Much of Townsite lies within the City’s coastal 
zone, which is governed by the City’s Local Coastal 
Program (LCP).  The LCP emphasizes public beach 
access, visitor-serving uses, and preservation 
of coastal resources (e.g., beach sand, sensitive 
habitat, water quality).  The California Coastal 
Commission has permit authority in areas below 
the mean high tide line on the beachfront and 
appeal jurisdiction in areas within 300 feet of the 
coastal bluff.  Consequently, close coordination 
between the City and the Coastal Commission on 
land use issues is critical.  
A stretch of California’s Pacific Coast Highway 
extends through the length of Townsite, consti-
tuting a commercial corridor over two miles in 
length.  Economic analyses have indicated that 
there is a surplus of commercial zoning within 
this corridor and that the introduction of hous-

SOUTH MORRO HILLS
Situated in the northeast corner of the City, the 
South Morro Hills neighborhood comprises nearly 
3,500 acres of designated farmland.  The neigh-
borhood is bordered by Camp Pendleton and the 
unincorporated community of Fallbrook to the 
north, the unincorporated community of Bonsall to 
the east, the San Luis Rey River to the south, and 
the North Valley neighborhood to the west.  South 
Morro Hills features sloping terrain that reach-
es an elevation of over 900 feet above sea level.  
Infrastructure in South Morro Hills is limited; while 
the area receives potable water service, there is no 
sewer service to those portions of the neighbor-
hood lying north of North River Road.
South Morro Hills accommodates both large-scale 
commercial farms and smaller avocational farming 
operations.  The majority of the land area in South

tomatoes, berries, coffee, cannabis, and ornamen-

of Conservation, the neighborhood includes prime 
farmland, farmland of statewide importance, 
unique farmland, and farmland of local importance.
While South Morro Hills is ostensibly reserved for

hood faces significant development pressure, as evi-

The “Sleeping Indian” rock outcropping in South 
Morro Hills is one of the most distinctive natural 
landmarks in Oceanside.  This landform reflects 
Oceanside’s Native American history and culture.  
Many residents want views of Sleeping Indian pre-
served and enhanced.
The City has initiated a community planning effort 
for South Morro Hills, with the intent of identifying 
and implementing practical, market-based strategies 
for conserving farmland and enhancing the econom-
ic viability of farming in the area.  Potential strategies 
include additional residential density in exchange for 
formal farmland conservation, allowances for “Tier 
2” agritourism uses (e.g., lodging, performing arts

food networks.

GUAJOME
The Guajome Neighborhood Planning Area (NPA) 
lies in the northeast corner of the City, bounded 
by the Morro Hills NPA to the north, residential 
and agricultural areas in the City of Vista and 
unincorporated San Diego County to the east, 
the Peacock NPA to the south, and the Ivey

Guajome NPA is Guajome Regional Park, a 394-

of hiking and equestrian trails, fishing lakes, picnic 
areas, playgrounds, and passive and recreational 
open space areas.  The park has a variety of pro-
tected habitats including woodlands, chaparral, 
wetlands, and mixed grasslands.  
Additional character defining features of Guajome

natural open space and recreational areas includ-

easternmost portion of the San Luis Rey River 
Trail Bike Path.  Portions of the San Luis Rey River 
meander through the northern portion of the plan-
ning area. The rolling topography of the planning 
area provides for panoramic views of the San Luis 
Rey Valley.  The use of zoning overlays, including 
the Scenic Park Overlay and Equestrian District 
Overlay, serve to protect the rural atmosphere 
and scenic areas of this planning area through the 
use of specific design standards and zoning regu-
lations.  Additionally, the Guajome NPA is part of 
the Guajome Park Sphere of Influence established

dards to protect the natural and cultural resourc-
es of Guajome Regional Park.  
Accommodating predominately residential land

sub-neighborhoods, including the equestrian 
community of Jeffries Ranch located in the east-
ern portion of the NPA and higher density housing 
clustered near Highway 76.  Primary access to the 
planning area is provided by Highway 76, which 
bisects the planning area.  Other major roadways 
providing access to Guajome NPA include College 
Boulevard, Melrose Drive, Mesa Drive, and Santa 
Fe Drive.  There are five public schools located 
within Guajome NPA: Alamosa Park Elementary

NORTH VALLEY
The North Valley NPA lies in the northeast corner 
of the City, bounded by Camp Pendleton to the 
north, the San Luis Rey River to the south, the 
South Morro Hills NPA to the east, and Airport 
NPA to the west. Land use in the North Valley 
consists primarily of detached single-family 
homes, dispersed neighborhood-serving commer-
cial centers, and a light industrial district primarily 
occupied by vehicle storage facilities.  
Character-defining features of North Valley include 
open space recreational areas: Melba Bishop Park 
and Recreation Center, Libby Lake Park, Luiseno 
Park, Oceanside Municipal Golf Course, and 
Arrowood Golf Course. The North Valley neigh-
borhood is also home to several nature preserves, 
including Whelan Lake Nature Preserve and the 
Pilgrim Creek Ecological Reserve. The neighbor-
hood’s adjacency to extensive open space along 
the City’s boundary with Camp Pendleton and 
within the San Luis Rey River provides for scenic 
vistas and recreational opportunities.
In addition to public parks, there are a number of 
other public facilities in North Valley.  The neigh-
borhood is served by several public schools: Libby 
Elementary School, Reynolds Elementary School, 
Del Rio Elementary School, and Bonsall West 
Elementary School.  The Libby Lake Resource 
Center at North River Road and Calle Montecito 
offers a wide range of community services, includ-
ing youth programs and healthcare.  The City’s 
San Luis Rey Wastewater Plant is located near 
the neighborhood’s western boundary.  The San 
Luis Rey Transit Center is near the neighborhoods 
south-eastern boundary along North River Road.
Major roadways in North Valley include Douglas 
Drive, North River Road, and Vandegrift Blvd. 
Camp Pendleton’s back gate entrance is locat-
ed at the northern terminus of Vandegrift Blvd. 
Primary access to the area is provided from Hwy 
76 via bridge crossings at Douglas Drive and 
College Blvd. Secondary access is provided by N. 
River Road through the South Morro Hills NPA. 
One of just two neighborhoods lying entirely 
north of the San Luis Rey River, North Valley is 
somewhat isolated from other parts of the City. 
Improvements to existing bridges over the river, 
including enhanced pedestrian and bicycle facili-
ties, can help to better connect North Valley with 
other areas.

SAN LUIS REY
The San Luis Rey neighborhood is bounded by 
the San Luis Rey River to the north, the Airport 
neighborhood to the west, the Loma Alta, Oceana, 
Ivey Ranch/Rancho del Oro neighborhoods to 
the south, and the Guajome neighborhood to the

Arbolitos area. Southern portions of San Luis Rey 
include condominium complexes, patio home sub-
divisions, and garden apartments. Large tracts of 
open space extend along the southern slopes of the 
neighborhood.  
The San Luis Rey neighborhood was the first area 
within Oceanside inhabited by American and 
European settlers. The neighborhood is home to 
important historic resources, including Mission San 
Luis Rey San Luis Rey Pioneer Cemetery, and a col-
lection of historic buildings located at Heritage Park.   
Much of the neighborhood lies within the Mission 
San Luis Rey Historic Area, which is governed by the 
Mission San Luis Rey Development Program and 
Design Guidelines. Development within the Historic 
Area is expected to reflect and complement the 
Mission and Spanish Colonial architecture embod-
ied by Mission San Luis Rey.
Several schools and parks are situated along the 
southern bank of the San Luis Rey River, including 
Foussat Elementary School, Nichols Elementary 
School, Chavez Middle School, and Mance Buchanon 
Park. The San Luis Rey River Trail runs along the 
neighborhood’s northern boundary. There is also 
protected natural habitat along this boundary.

additional public viewing areas, particularly in the

IVEY RANCH/RANCHO DEL ORO
Ivey Ranch/Rancho del Oro lies in the geograph-
ical center of the City, bounded by Highway 76 
to the north, the Guajome and Peacock neighbor-
hoods to the east, Loma Alta Creek to the south, 
and El Camino Real to the west.  Once occupied by 
farms and mining operations, the neighborhood 
now accommodates a wide variety of land uses, 
including a range of housing types, light industrial 
parks, regional-serving recreational facilities, large 
shopping centers, and open space areas.  The light 
industrial parks and large shopping centers make 
Ivey Ranch/Rancho del Oro one of the City’s most 
important employment centers.  
The Ivey Ranch portion of the neighborhood, sit-
uated north of Mesa Drive, is comprised primarily 
of small-lot detached single-family subdivisions 
constructed in the early 1990s, some of which are 
gated and served by private streets.
The vast majority of property within Ivey Ranch/
Rancho del Oro is governed by discrete land use 
planning documents – the Rancho del Oro Specific 
Plan, the El Corazon Specific Plan, a number of 
industrial park master plans, and multiple planned 
development plans (PDs).  Consequently, from a 
land use standpoint, the neighborhood is among 
the most highly regulated in the City, particularly 
compared to neighborhoods that have developed 
more organically over time. 
Segments of Oceanside Boulevard that extend 
through Ivey Ranch/Rancho del Oro lie within 
the planning area of the Smart and Sustainable 
Corridors Plan (SSCSP).  The SSCSP is expect-
ed to facilitate midrise mixed-use develop-
ment along the southern edge of Oceanside 
Boulevard between El Camino Real and Rancho 
del Oro Drive.  Redevelopment of this segment 
of Oceanside Boulevard will be accompanied by 
complete street improvements that render the 
roadway more accommodating to alternative

MIRA COSTA
The Mira Costa NPA lies in the southeastern 
part of the city, bounded by Loma Alta Creek to 
the north, Highway 78 to the south, the Tri-City 
neighborhood to the east, and Fire Mountain 
neighborhood to the west. Land use in Mira Costa 
consists primarily of single-family residential sub-
divisions, garden apartment complexes, and clus-
ters of retail and medical office uses along Vista 
Way. The neighborhood accommodates a seg-
ment of NCTD’s Sprinter rail line, and two Sprinter 
stations (Rancho del Oro and College Boulevard) 
are situated near the neighborhood’s northwest 
and northeast corners, respectively.  The future 
Inland Rail Trail may extend along this segment of 
the Sprinter rail line.
Character-defining features of Mira Costa include 
Mira Costa Community College, Palisades Park, 
Joe Sepulveda Park, the El Camino Country Club, 
and Loma Alta Creek.  Occupying roughly 120 
acres of land on the neighborhood’s crest, Mira 
Costa Community College contributes signifi-
cantly to workforce development, while offering 
community education programs and sponsoring 
cultural and athletic events open to the commu-
nity at-large. 
Much of the Mira Costa neighborhood rests on a 
mesa that separates the Vista Way/Highway 78 
corridor from the Oceanside Boulevard corridor.  
The mesa affords elevated residential areas with 
separation from major roadways and more inten-
sive land uses, extensive vegetated slopes, and 
expansive views.  At the same time, topography 
makes walking and bicycling more challenging for 
many residents.
The absence of an interchange at Rancho del 
Oro Road and Highway 78 forces traffic onto El 
Camino Real and College Boulevard and limits 
access to major employment hubs, El Corazon 
Park, and other destinations in central areas of 
the City.  While an interchange at this location has 
long been identified in the City’s long-range trans-
portation plans, there is significant neighborhood 
opposition to its implementation.

LOMA ALTA
Bounded by Interstate 5 to the west, Mission 
Avenue to the north, El Camino Real to the east, 
and Loma Alta Creek to the south, Loma Alta is one 
of the City’s most diverse neighborhoods, in terms 
of its demographics, land uses, and landforms. 
Translated as “High Hill” in English, Loma Alta fea-
tures sloping terrain that rises up from the com-
mercial corridors that define its northern, southern, 
and eastern boundaries.  Elevated areas are exclu-
sively residential, supporting both single-family and 
multi-family development.  Character-defining ele-
ments in Loma Alta include Loma Alta Creek, Goat 
Hill Golf Course, Buddy Todd Park, and a collection 
of recreational and institutional uses around Ron 
Ortega Park.  
Loma Alta is relatively well-served by both bus 
and rail service.  Several NCTD Breeze bus 
routes extend through the neighborhood, some 
of which link directly to the Crouch Street and El 
Camino Real Sprinter rail stations in the Oceanside 
Boulevard corridor.
Mesa Drive extends through the middle of Loma 
Alta, knitting the northern and southern portions 
of the neighborhood together and connecting 
single-family residential areas to Mission Avenue 
and El Camino Real.  Located equidistant from the 
existing San Luis Rey River Trail and the planned 
Inland Rail Trail, this secondary collector street 
could serve as an important east-west bicycle 
route if buffered or separated bicycle lanes can be 
implemented.
Interstate 5 is a significant barrier between Loma 
Alta and the City’s coastal area. The bridges over 
Interstate 5 at Mission Avenue and Brooks Street 
include limited pedestrian and bicycle facilities.  
The Oceanside Boulevard underpass at Interstate 
5 includes narrow and unbuffered sidewalk and 
bike lanes.  Within the neighborhood, topography 
can be an impediment to walking and bicycling.
Community resources such as the Boys and Girls 
Club, the Women’s Resource Center, Oceanside 
Family Counseling Center, and the Country Club 
Senior Center provide a range of public services, 
host numerous community events, and encourage 
social and civic engagement.  
Mission Avenue and Oceanside Boulevard lie within 
the planning area of the Smart and Sustainable 
Corridors Specific Plan.  Several of the most uti-
lized bus lines extend along arterial and collector 
streets of Loma Alta, such as the 303, 309, 313 and 
318, and the 313, as well as the Sprinter.

One of Peacock’s most distinct and appealing fea-

Peacock arguably lacks activity centers, having 
no public parks, no pedestrian-oriented commer-
cial areas, and no institutional facilities that pro-
vide for public gathering and interaction.   While 
Temple Heights Elementary School/THE Learning 
Academy and the Oceanside/Melrose Sprinter 
Station could potentially serve as nodal commu-
nity gathering/activity areas, these are sites over 
which the City has little authority.  The business/
industrial parks and the COC may offer the best 
opportunities to establish activity centers acces-
sible to all community members.  
Oceanside Blvd is more of a barrier than a seam 
in this area, separating residential areas to the 
north from commercial uses and transit service to 
the south.  While some of the roadway’s center 
median is landscaped, much of it is not, rendering 
certain stretches of the corridor rather stark and 
uninviting.

TRI-CITY
The Tri-City neighborhood lies in the southeast-
ern quadrant of the City, within the Highway 78 
corridor. It is bounded by the Peacock NPA to the 
north, the Mira Costa NPA to the west, Highway 
78 to the south, and the City of Vista to the east. 
One of the City’s smaller NPAs, Tri-City primar-
ily comprises single-family residences with com-
mercial and semi-public uses concentrated along 
its southern border.  The segment of Vista Way 
that extends through the neighborhood supports 
a cluster of healthcare uses anchored by Tri-City 
Medical Center.  Tri-City is thus an important 
employment center in Oceanside.  Other char-
acter-defining features of Tri-City include John 
Landes Park and the small commercial center on 
Thunder Drive. 
John Landes Park and the adjacent commercial 
center provide opportunities for social interaction 
and community-building.  However, the auto-cen-
tric design of the commercial center discourages 
pedestrian activity and gathering.  The commer-
cial center could be redeveloped as a mixed-use 
village, adding to the neighborhood’s housing 
supply and enhancing the curb appeal of the site 
while continuing to provide essential goods and 
services. 
Most residential areas of Tri-City include parkway 
between the sidewalk and the street.  However, 
much of the parkway is neglected and devoid of 
landscape.  Consequently, Tri-City has less tree 
canopy than many Oceanside neighborhoods.  
Expanding tree canopy in Tri-City would enhance 
walkability, reduce the heat island effect, and con-
tribute to visual quality.
Tri-City is currently served by several NCTD 
Breeze bus routes that  provide access to Mira 
Costa College, the College Boulevard Sprinter 
Station, and numerous commercial centers.  
However, many bus stops in the neighborhood do 
not provide seating or shade. 
Many residents of Tri-City do not feel a strong 
connection to the City of Oceanside, as their daily 
needs are often met by commercial, education-
al, and recreational uses located in the adjacent 
cities of Vista and Carlsbad.  Moreover, there is an 
absence of signage and other markers that indi-
cate the neighborhood lies within Oceanside.

LAKE
The Lake neighborhood lies in the southeastern 
corner of the City, where it is bounded by Highway 
78, the Ocean Hills neighborhood, and the cities 
of Vista and Carlsbad.  The neighborhood dis-
plays rolling topography, with southern portions 
extending into the Calavera Hills. The neighbor-
hood is essentially bisected by Lake Boulevard, 
which connects the Ocean Hills neighborhood to 
the City.  Other significant roadways in the neigh-
borhood include College Blvd and Plaza Drive.   
The neighborhood features several regional-serv-
ing commercial centers, including Quarry Creek 
Plaza, College Plaza, and Tri-City Crossroads. 
These centers and other commercial sites have 
the potential to accommodate mixed-use devel-
opment, under the provisions of the Smart and 
Sustainable Corridors Plan. The neighborhood 
supports a wide variety of residential uses: 
detached single-family homes, duplexes, garden 
apartments, and mobile home parks.  A significant 
percentage of housing in this neighborhood is 
reserved for seniors.  Much of this housing is dis-
connected from commercial districts by topogra-
phy, a circuitous and disconnected roadway, and 
limited active transportation infrastructure.   
The neighborhood includes a segment of Buena 
Vista Creek, the ecology of which is threatened 
by urban runoff, invasive plant species, and ille-
gal camping.  By and large, existing development 
turns its back on the creek; redevelopment of 
commercial sites abutting the creek will provide 
an opportunity to rehabilitate the creek, enhance 
edge conditions, and place eyes on this important 
biological and visual resource.  
Southern portions of the neighborhood abut the 
Calavera Preserve in the City of Carlsbad, which 
includes an extensive trail network.  There are 
opportunities to improve connections between 
the neighborhood and the preserve.  There are 
also opportunities to expand the trail network 
in the vicinity within an electricity distribution 
corridor.
