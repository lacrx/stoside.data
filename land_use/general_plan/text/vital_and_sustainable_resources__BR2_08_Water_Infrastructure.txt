8.1	Water Infrastructure
EXISTING SYSTEM
Water supply to the City is primarily pro­
vided through purchasing of both treated and 
untreated imported water from the San Diego 
County Water Authority (SDCWA) through five 
(5) aqueduct connections. In Fiscal Year 2019, 
88% percent of the water supply was provided 
by the SDCWA. The remaining 11 percent of 
water supply was provided by the Mission Basin 
Groundwater Purification Facility and 1% from 
recycled water supplies. Annual water supplied 
in Fiscal Year 2019 was approximately 22,645 
acre feet (AF). The distribution system consists of 
28 pressure zones, 597 miles of pipelines varying 
in size from 4 inches to 42 inches, 8 groundwa­
ter wells, 12 storage reservoirs, 9 booster pump 
stations, 5 imported water connections, and 69 
pressure regulating stations. As shown in Table 
1-1, recycled water is growing in supply both 
through advanced treatment of wastewater with 
the primary use of groundwater basin replenish­
ment as well as recycled water irrigation use on 
golf courses, the City’s sports park as well as the 
Whelan Lake Bird Sanctuary.
The City completed the last water Master Plan 
in 2015. At that time, the estimated build-
out growth potential was based on the 2002 
General Plan land use, including distribution 
of land use types and both near-term and 
long-range development plans. San Diego 
Association of Governments (SANDAG) 2050 
regional growth forecasting was used for pro­
jecting the City’s future water demand. Some 
of the South Morro Hills area was projected to 
be comprised of new low-density residences; 
however, this was considered uncertain and 
the impacts of this conversion of land from 
agricultural use to low-density residential use 
on the water distribution system were thus not 
reflected in future water demand. Significant 
amounts of redevelopment were planned for 
the Downtown area around City Hall and to the 
beachfront.
Since 2005, water usage in Oceanside has been 
reducing on a per capita basis (see Figure 8-1).

Actual water usage was 122 gallons per capita 
per day (gpcd) in 2020 compared to a 2020 
target usage of 137 GPCD. Annual water demand 
is expected to be relatively flat through 2045, 
with an ultimate annual water demand of 
24,377 acre-feet per year, (Source 2020 UWMP - 
Woodard & Curran).
As a result of the very low projected increase in 
water demand, there were no recommended 
capacity projects within the 2015 Water Master 
Plan specifically to address population growth. 
The only projects that addressed a capacity 
deficiency were related to addressing changes 
in regional standard fire flow requirements.  No 
change in regional fire flow requirements (based 
on land use) are expected for the 2020 master 
plan update.  
Existing water storage reservoirs were installed 
between 1956 and 1995. Ongoing reservoir 
upgrades and new water quality monitoring 
projects were identified in 2015 to support the 
long-term reliability of the aging infrastructure. 
Between 2015 and 2020, reservoir projects 
focused on structural rehabilitation needs.
As a primary gravity fed water distribution 
system, the City has nine (9) pressure booster 
stations, with these stations only utilized under 
emergency conditions. Projects were identified 
in 2015 to add backup generators to these sta­
tions for increased reliability.
FUTURE NEEDS
The City will be preparing the 2020 Water Master 
Plan to update and reprioritize recommend 
improvement projects. A distribution system 
water model will be used for evaluating perfor­
mance of the system. Performance criteria is 
expected to be identical to past water master 
plans.
Two areas of the distribution system have been 
recognized by the City as underperforming, 
including the Peacock Hills pressure zone served 
by the Crestview and Old Grove pressure reduc­
ing stations (PRSs) and Fire Mountain pressure 
zone, as shown in Figure 8-2. Achieving adequate 
fire flow in this area has historically been accept­
able, but near the allowable limit. While small 
changes in domestic water demand is not typi­
cally a trigger for needing to increase capacity, 
for this pressure zone, the current water pres­
sure performance and small increase in average 
annual water demand due to development may 
trigger the need for adding a new supply pipe­
line to the City water transmission pipeline.   This 
new connection would allow the pressure zone 
to be served at a higher pressure and improve 
customer satisfaction. Similar to Peacock Hills, 
in the Fire Mountain pressure zone, there is a 
potential trend to increase residential density 
by consolidating parcels.  The following figure 
shows the location of the Peacock Hills pressure 
zone and Fire Mountain pressure zone.  
For consideration of recommended changes 
in the City land use as part of the General Plan 
Update, any resultant changes in parcel land 
use will need to be assessed as part of the 
water system hydraulic modeling effort during 
the 2020 Water Master Plan update. The water 
model consolidates individual parcels into larger 
polygons for the assessment fire flows. No 
changes in land use-based fire flow criteria are 
projected as part of the 2020 Water Master Plan.
Related to Citywide future water demand, 
the City has invested in the construction of an 
advanced water treatment system at the San 
Luis Rey Water Reclamation Facility (SLRWRF) 
and is expanding the recycled water distribution 
network.  In 2018, the City expanded the tertiary 
treatment capacity of the SLRWRF to 3.0 MGD 
and allowed for future expansion of up to 10.5 
MGD. This system provides a direct supply of 
recycled water to large irrigation and commercial 
demands, supplementing current potable water 
demand. At the end of 2021, the system will 
also include the injection of advanced treated 
water into the groundwater network to further 
support the long-term sustainability of the 
groundwater basin as a source of potable water. 
The combined effort will both reduce overall 
demand of potable water and reduce reliance 
on imported potable water from the SDCWA by 
approximately 30 percent.

8.2	Sewer System
EXISTING SYSTEM
The existing sewer collection system consists of 
486 miles of gravity pipeline, 50 miles of force 
mains, 36 lift stations and two wastewater 
treatment plants. There are also 201 miles of 
private sewer laterals that connect customers to 
the sewer system. Wastewater is treated either 
at the La Salina Wastewater Treatment Plant 
(WWTP) or the San Luis Rey WWTP. Both treat­
ment plants discharge treated wastewater to the 
Pacific Ocean through ocean outfalls. The system 
collects and treats an average of 12.5 million gal­
lons per day (MGD) during dry weather, and up 
to 30.7 MGD during wet weather. Wet weather 
peaking factors are commonly around 2 to 2.5 
times average dry weather flows.
The 2015 Sewer Master Plan (SMP) identifies 
near term (2015-2020) and long term (2021-
2050) capital improvements program (CIP) 
projects designed to increase capacity, replace 
or rehabilitate deteriorating assets in order to 
provide reliable services, prevent environmen­
tal contamination, and expand the wastewater 
collection system to adequately meet current 
demands and future demands based on the 
SANDAG 2050 Regional Growth Forecast. While 
there are no overall treatment capacity issues 
currently, CIP prioritization is based on urgency 
to mitigate existing deficiencies and condition 
issues and provide capacity for future growth 
including near term development projects (e.g. El 
Corazon, Terraza RDO Village XII, Ocean Pointe, 
and The Villages of Morro Hills). Pipeline rehabil­
itation accounts for nearly 62% of the total CIP 
costs through 2050. The largest capital project 
consists of the combined new La Salina Pump 
Station, associated forcemain and improve­
ments to San Luis Rey WRF. Rehabilitation proj­
ects were identified through an age-based asset 
management model that projects useful life for 
various materials, systems and technologies. 
Since 2015, the City has made extensive efforts 
to inspect the condition of the collection system 
as well as implement a significant amount of 
projects to reduce I&I, to ensure spills do not 
enter waterways and to repair/rehabilitate sev­
eral of the larger sewer force mains.
FUTURE NEEDS
During the capacity analysis portion of the 
master planning effort, only large sewer inter­
ceptor pipelines were included in the mod­
eling process. These pipelines accounted for 
approximately 30 percent of the entire collec­
tion system. For wet weather capacity analy­
sis, pipelines were allowed to flow up to 100 
percent of capacity before being triggered as 
undersized. The evaluation criteria for the 2020 
Sewer Master Plan will stipulate the maximum 
allowable wet weather flow capacity to a depth 
to diameter ratio of 0.85. Modeled flows that 
exceed this criterion will be considered under­
sized and candidates for upsizing.
The study area for the SMP closely coincides 
with the City boundaries with the exception 
of the South Morro Hills area in the northeast 
section of the City, as shown in Figure 8-3. 
The South Morro Hills area does not have any 
sewer infrastructure installed at the time of 
the SMP evaluation and all sewage in the area 
is disposed of via septic systems. The South 
Morro Hills Community Plan being prepared by 
the City is looking at potential for agritourism 
and increased residential development in the 
area. Changes in the South Morro Hills area to 
residential land use will require extension of the 
collection system to service new development, 
and as a result the existing downstream collec­
tion system will have increased flows and may 
require upsizing to operate below the maximum 
allowable wet weather flow capacity depth to 
diameter ratio of 0.85.

FUTURE SYSTEM
The current National Pollution Discharge 
Elimination Systems (NPDES) Regional Municipal 
Separate Storm Sewer System (MS4) Permit (MS4 
Permit), under which the City is a co-permittee, 
requires new projects to install and manage 
best management practices (BMPs) onsite that 
mitigate project effects to the City’s existing 
MS4. New projects must determine the poten­
tial changes in onsite runoff due to increases in 
impervious surfaces and/or drainage area alter­
ations, then install and manage BMPs that regu­
late offsite discharges to mimic pre-project con­
ditions. This regulation is intended to maintain 
(or reduce) pre-project stormwater peak flows 
in order to reduce potential for erosion, provide 
an opportunity to enhance water quality, and to 
prevent an overflow of the stormwater system.
The current MS4 permit effectively requires any 
future development upon a parcel to ensure that 
the extent of new development does not result 
in an increase in peak stormwater runoff.  This is 
particularly relevant for new development that 
results in the construction of new impervious 
surfaces.  As a result, if the existing stormwa­
ter collection system has adequate capacity 
to convey current peak stormwater flows, the 
MS4 permit protects the stormwater collection 
system from future growth and development 
within the drainage basin.  
As stated above, and within the 2013 Master Plan 
of Drainage, there are improvements needed 
within the existing stormwater infrastructure to 
meet current peak drainage flows.  Due to the 
application of the MS4 Permit, recommended 
changes in future development patterns of the 
City identified within the 2020 General Plan 
should not result in changes to the peak storm­
water flows conveyed within the stormwater 
collection system.

Because population growth should not be a 
factor on stormwater system capacity, the 
prioritization of stormwater CIP projects 
should address facilities based on the 
minimum storm event conveyance criteria 
and consider the location of these projects 
in proximity to other, non-stormwater CIP 
projects. Any major public infrastructure 
upgrades that may require excavation within 
or along roadways and/or changes in drainage 
patterns should also evaluate whether or not a 
stormwater project identified in the 2013 MPD 
is within the proposed construction footprint, 
and if so, how or if the stormwater project can 
be addressed in a comprehensive design to 
create efficiencies.
