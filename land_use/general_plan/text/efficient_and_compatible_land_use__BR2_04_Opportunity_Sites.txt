4.1	Methodology and 
Purpose
As the City of Oceanside considers how and 
where future housing and employment growth 
can be accommodated over the next 15 to 20 
years, there is a need to identify properties that 
present an opportunity for new development 
or redevelopment.  “Opportunity sites” are 
those that have the greatest potential for 
change in land use types or intensities over the 
planning period. Given that most property is 
privately owned, and development of private 
property is contingent upon the initiative of 
property owners, it is not possible to precisely 
determine which sites will undergo changes in 
land use type or intensity. However, analysis 
of site conditions, land use and development 
standards, market forces, lease agreements, 
and other considerations can provide a strong 
indication of where such changes are likely to 
occur. 
The purpose of identifying and mapping 
opportunity sites is to understand where the 
most likely possibilities for land use change 
exist, so that public policy can be adjusted 
and leveraged to effect desired change. 
Understanding where land use change is 
possible will help to determine appropriate land 
use policies, regulations, and guidelines. 
A multi-step process has been undertaken to 
map potential opportunity sites in the Planning 
Area. As an initial step, sites were initially 
identified by mapping vacant and underutilized 
land using San Diego County Assessor’s 
data. The analysis also identified sites with 
development potential but with significant 
environmental constraint considerations, such 
as floodplains or presence of sensitive habitat.
The County Assessor’s data was used to 
preliminarily identify “underutilized” land by 
revealing parcels with a low assessed value ratio, 
or AV ratio. AV ratio is defined as the ratio of 
the value of existing permanent improvements 
(i.e., buildings) to the value of the land. Where 
this ratio is less than one, a parcel may be

considered underutilized, as a higher land 
value suggests market demand for higher and 
better use of the parcel. Accordingly, a ratio of 
less than 0.5 indicates even greater potential 
for redevelopment. In other words, the lower 
the value of permanent structures on a site 
relative to land value, the greater the propensity 
to change, especially if market conditions and 
zoning regulations allow higher development 
values to be realized.  
Also identified in the assessment are sites with 
low intensity uses, which is another indication of 
potential for intensification in the future, were 
identified. Development intensity is measured 
as floor area ratio (FAR), which is the ratio of 
building floor area to overall site area (as defined 
in Section 2.3). A low intensity means that 
buildings are small in floor area compared to the 
overall site. Sites with FAR values of 0.2 or less 
were identified as having significant potential 
for redevelopment or expansion. Some sites 
with FAR values below 0.2, as seen in Figure 4-1, 
also have low AV ratios. Sites with FAR values of 
below 0.2 and AV ratios below 0.7 have higher 
potential for redevelopment than sites with low 
FARs alone.
As shown in Table 4-1, opportunity sites 
were assigned a tier to indicate likelihood of 
redevelopment within the planning period. 
Vacant land, or parcels with no development, 
were categorized as Tier 1, indicating the highest 
development potential. Tiers 2, 3, and 4 were 
assigned based on FAR and AV ratio thresholds; 
a low FAR was defined as less than 0.2, and a 
moderate FAR was defined as between 0.2 and 
0.35; a low AV ratio was defined as less than 0.5, 
and a moderate AV ratio was defined as between 
0.5 and 0.7. 
Next, potential opportunity sites were analyzed 
for constraints, including steep slopes, 
floodplains, and wetlands. Parcels with steep 
slopes (greater than 25 percent), which make 
up 18 percent of sites meeting the FAR and AV 
ratio thresholds, or that intersect with a flood 
zone or wetland, are considered environmentally 
constrained and subtracted from the total 
acreage. Should the City seek to explore 
development potential on these sites, a finer-
grained analysis could help to determine site 
viability.

It is important to note that sites identified in this 
analysis as potential opportunity sites are just 
that—potential opportunities. These sites are 
likely to have a greater propensity for change 
if the right set of land use designations and/or 
development incentives are provided. However, 
because development decisions will ultimately 
be made by the individual property owners, 
it is possible that some of these potential sites 
may not experience change over the next 20 
years. Conversely, some sites not identified in 
this analysis may undergo redevelopment. This 
analysis is merely a tool to identify and quantify 
areas in Oceanside where future development 
is most likely to occur in the next 20 years, 
providing an order-of-magnitude perspective 
on the potential for infill and redevelopment. 
Possibilities for the future would be examined in 
further detail as part of the alternatives process.
It is also noted that in 2019 the City prepared 
an Employment Land Inventory as part of 
the Economic Development Element of the 
current General Plan. The study compared 
the availability of vacant and underutilized 
land—defined as properties with an assessed 
property value under 15 dollars per square foot 
and FAR under 0.2—with projected demand for 
commercial and industrial uses over the 2018 
to 2035 planning period. While there are similar 
implications, the methodology and findings 
of the opportunity sites analysis described in 
this chapter differ slightly from the previous 
study, including additional considerations such 
as environmental constraints that limit the 
availability of realistically developable land.

As seen in Table 4-3, opportunity sites are 
predominantly designated as Commercial or 
Residential in the current General Plan. Table 
4-3 also highlights that a majority of vacant 
land is designated for residential use. While 
Tier 1 represents the greatest potential for 
development, most of the large vacant parcels 
are currently designated as lower density 
residential uses and are not well-connected 
to transit, indicating a lower potential yield of 
housing units on these sites. As the second 
most common designation, commercial uses 
represent potential for mixed use development 
and greater intensity development along 
commercial corridors, though it is noted that 
most of these sites are categorized as Tier 4.
Additionally, as seen in Table 4-4, many 
opportunity sites are also included in the 
Housing Element sites inventory. Similar to the 
opportunity sites analysis described above, the 
Housing Element sites inventory identifies both 
vacant and non-vacant (underutilized) land with 
the potential for additional housing development 
during the 6th Housing Element cycle. However, 
the analysis of non-vacant properties in the 
Housing Element includes additional criteria to 
identify properties with realistic potential for 
additional development in light of: 1) existing 
uses on the site; 2) prevailing market conditions; 
3) recent development trends; 4) expressed 
interests in housing development from property 
owners or developers; and 5) regulatory and/
or other incentives to encourage recycling or 
intensification of existing development. As a 
result, 48 percent of the areas identified in the 
Housing Element sites inventory are included 
in the tiered opportunity sites analysis, and 
52 percent are additional sites found using a 
separate methodology.

4.3	Key Planning Issues 
and Implications
•	 Approximately 850 acres of developable 
land available in Oceanside. Overall, there 
are 853 acres of developable land within 
the Planning Area. An additional 150 acres 
are also either vacant or underutilized, but 
significant environmental constraints would 
limit development on these parcels. It is noted, 
however, that this is a preliminary overview of 
opportunity sites, and further analysis may 
be required to determine actual development 
potential of individual sites, including 
consideration of development regulations 
pertaining to environmental constraints or 
opportunities allowed by implementation of 
mitigation strategies.
•	 Most underutilized land designated as 
Commercial and located along major 
corridors. While many of these opportunity 
sites are located along Oceanside’s corridors, 
they are mostly in Tiers 3 and 4 because of 
their AV ratio and FAR ranges. However, many 
are developed and utilized well below the 
thresholds allowed under current zoning. Thus, 
these sites can accommodate considerably 
more development and land use intensity. 
Furthermore, the introduction of residential 
use on these sites could be a catalyst for the 
expansion of commercial use, as additional 
“rooftops” provide more market demand for 
commercial goods and services.
•	 Prime land for redevelopment designated 
as residential under the current General 
Plan. Tier 1, vacant land, represents parcels 
with the greatest potential for development. 
Most of the Tier 1 land is designated as 
residential under the current General Plan, 
indicating the opportunity for significant 
residential growth. Most of the largest parcels, 
however, are designated as lower density 
residential uses. To meet the City’s regional 
housing needs allocation at all income levels, 
the General Plan Update will need to locate 
appropriate sites for higher density housing 
development that is well-connected to transit.
•	 Lack of industrial land. The City’s remaining 
inventor y of vacant industrial land is 
expected to be fully saturated within the 
next few years.  The General Plan Update 
provides an opportunity to explore creative 
ways to accommodate additional industrial 
development.  The City can contemplate “flex” 
zoning in commercial areas that provides for 
low-intensity/low-impact industrial uses.  The 
City can also look for ways to incentivize more 
efficient use of developed industrial land. 
Oceanside has become an attractive locale for 
warehousing and distribution facilities, which 
typically occupy large tracts of land but provide 
relatively few jobs per acre.  The City could 
consider limitations on such uses, in order to 
provide more opportunities for industrial uses 
with higher employment densities.
•	 Preservation of existing residential areas. 
The City seeks to preserve the integrity 
of existing residential areas, particularly 
single-family neighborhoods.  Single-family 
development occupies much of the City’s 
developable land area, while Opportunity sites 
generally lie in non-residential and multifamily 
residential districts.
