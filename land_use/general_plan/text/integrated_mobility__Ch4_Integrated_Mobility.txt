In partnership with local, regional, and state transportation 
agencies, the City will expand mobility options and provide for 
safe, convenient, and efficient transitions between different modes 
of travel. Dependence on the private automobile will be reduced 
through an increasingly diverse transportation network supported

4.1	Background
A CHANGING MOBILITY 
LANDSCAPE
The Integrated Mobility Element (IM) replaces the 
City’s Circulation Element, which was last updated 
in 2012. The mobility landscape in the San Diego 
region has changed considerably over the inter-
vening decade, and more change is inevitably on 
its way, driven by evolving consumer preferenc-
es, new state policies (e.g. Senate Bill 743), new 
mobility services, expanded roadway and transit 
infrastructure, and the principles and priorities of 
the Regional Transportation Plan. In the course of 
preparing the IM, the City has been mindful of the 
following mobility trends:
•	 A nation-wide trend towards zero-emission 
vehicles, including all-electric and hydrogen fuel 
vehicles;
•	 Expansion of ride-hailing services (e.g., Uber, 
Lyft);
•	 New technologies for scheduling, coordinating, 
and paying for transit trips;
•	 New micro-mobility options (e.g., e-bikes, 
scooters, neighborhood electric vehicles);
•	 The rise of e-commerce, allowing consumers to 
access goods and services without leaving their 
homes, while placing more delivery vehicles on 
the road;
•	 The rise of telework and alternative commuting 
options;
•	 The use of “big data” and real-time traffic 
monitoring to improve the efficiency of 
roadways and transit systems;
•	 State and regional transportation agencies 
dedicating highway lanes to express buses 
and other high-occupancy vehicles, while 
contemplating congestion pricing and user fees; 
•	 Local jurisdictions implementing “complete 
streets” with traffic-calming features and 
enhanced pedestrian and bicycle facilities; and
•	 As part of bipartisan infrastructure legislation 
approved in 2021, the federal government has 
allocated substantial funding for programs and 
projects that reduce GHG emissions in the 
transportation sector. One of the initiatives 
emerging from this legislation is the National 
Electric Vehicle Infrastructure (NEVI) Formula 
Program, which seeks to build out a national 
electric vehicle charging network.

All of the above reflect a mobility landscape in 
transition, at all levels of society and government. 
It is essential that Oceanside and other local juris-
dictions in the San Diego region not only be cog-
nizant of and prepared for this transition, but also

LEARNING FROM THE PAST
Like many cities, Oceanside can look to its own past 
to find inspiration for expanded mobility options. 
Incorporated in 1888, the City was not always 
an auto-dependent community. Indeed, an auto-
mobile would have been a rare site in Oceanside 
prior to 1908, when mass production began in 
the United States. It is worth noting that some of 
the first automobiles to appear in Oceanside were 
powered by electric batteries, while others were 
powered by steam.
Prior to post-WWII urban sprawl, Oceanside was a 
relatively walkable community accessible by train 
from the north, south, and east. With develop-
ment concentrated near the coast, residential and 
non-residential uses were generally in closer prox-
imity to one another, allowing people to walk (and 
in some cases, ride horses) to their destinations. 
For a brief period in the early 1880s, the California 
Southern Railroad connected Oceanside to freight 
and passenger rail services between San Diego 
and Colton, until massive flooding near Fallbrook 
destroyed a segment of the rail line in 1891.
The Santa Fe Railroad completed the “Surf Line” 
between Oceanside and Orange County in 1888, 
establishing direct rail service between San Diego 
and Los Angeles. Most of Oceanside’s earliest resi-
dents and visitors came to the City by train. In fact, 
train service made Oceanside a popular seaside 
getaway for wealthy Angelenos, including those in 
the burgeoning motion picture industry.
The Central California Railroad provided both pas-
senger and freight services between Oceanside 
and Escondido from 1900 to 1946. Today, the 
North County Transit District’s (NCTD) SPRINTER 
service utilizes much of the original right-of-way 
dedicated to the Central California Railroad.
As the South Oceanside area began to devel-
op in the 1920s (prior to its annexation into the 
City of Oceanside), a trolley line between South 
Oceanside and Carlsbad Village was planned but 
never constructed. Nearly 100 years later, a list 
of potential transportation projects prepared 
by SANDAG in support of the 2014 Regional 
Transportation 
Improvement 
Program 
(RTIP) 
included a trolley line on Coast Highway between 
downtown Oceanside and Carlsbad Village. While 
this project did not survive SANDAG’s budgeting 
process, it speaks how new transit options could 
reduce vehicle miles traveled (VMT) and conges-
tion on Coast Highway while enhancing access to 
key coastal destinations and amenities.
Oceanside’s early history of compact develop-
ment, walkable neighborhoods, and reliance on rail 
service provides perspective on the relationship 
between land use and mobility, and hints at what 
the future might hold for the City as it promotes 
“smart growth” and embraces a broader range of 
mobility options.

KEY CHARACTERISTICS OF 
OCEANSIDE’S EXISTING 
MOBILITY NETWORK
Notwithstanding Oceanside’s peripheral location 
and predominately suburban and exurban charac-
ter, the City is an important gateway into the San 
Diego region for people coming from the north, 
as well as an increasingly popular coastal desti-
nation for residents of the Highway 78 and State 
Route 76 corridors, the Inland Empire, and other 
western states. The City is also an employment 
hub for many workers living in the North County 
subregion. In addition to the local, City-maintained 
roadway system, Oceanside is connected to other 
areas by roadways and transit services operat-
ed and maintained by other agencies, including 
Caltrans (I-5, SR-78, SR-76), Amtrak, Metrolink, and 
NCTD. Consequently, coordination between the 
City and these other agencies is critical to the suc-
cessful integration of the City’s mobility network.
Listed below are key characteristics of Oceanside’s 
existing mobility network, including facilities and 
services operated by other agencies.
•	 The local, City-maintained roadway system 
features five major north-south surface streets 
(Coast Highway, El Camino Real, Rancho del Oro, 
College Boulevard and Melrose Drive) and three 
major east-west surface streets (Mission Avenue, 
Oceanside Boulevard, and Vista Way). Together, 
these streets constitute the spine of the local 
roadway network, with other streets like North 
River Road, Mesa Drive, North Santa Fe Drive, and 
Douglas Drive providing important connections 
between them. The local network is supplemented 
by regional facilities including Interstate 5, State 
Route 76, and State Route 78, all of which are 
managed and operated by Caltrans.
•	 Traf fic congestion hot spot s include 
intersections along State Route 76, Oceanside 
Boulevard near Interstate 5, El Camino Real 
near State Route 78, and College Boulevard near 
State Route 78 and Oceanside Boulevard. 
•	 Oceanside is served by Interstate 5 and 
State Routes 76 and 78. These roadways not 
only connect the City to the rest of the San 
Diego region, but also to Orange County, the 
Interstate 15 corridor, and the Inland Empire. At 
the same time, these facilities tend to separate 
neighborhoods and create barriers to active 
transportation. For example, the absence of 
grade-separated crossings along State Route 
76 discourages walking and biking and divides 
residential areas from nearby commercial 
venues and recreational amenities. 
•	 Coast Highway has long served as an alternative 
to Interstate 5, particularly during periods 
of high traffic congestion. Complete streets 
improvements on Coast Highway – including 
a “road diet,” roundabout intersections, bike 
lanes, and enhanced pedestrian crossings – are 
expected to render this roadway a less attractive 
option for pass-through traffic.
•	 Oceanside is uniquely positioned to benefit 
from expanded rail service, with the Oceanside 
Transit Center (OTC) accommodating four 
separate rail lines – NCTD’s COASTER and 
SPRINTER, as well as Metrolink’s Orange 
County Line and Amtrak’s Surfliner. The OTC is 
also a hub for bus service, accommodating eight 
separate bus routes that connect Oceanside 
to Carlsbad, Vista, and Camp Pendleton. 
Redevelopment of the OTC with a mix of 
housing and commercial uses is expected to 
increase demand for rail and bus service and 
create a more attractive, comfortable, and 
accommodating environment for transit users.

THE USER EXPERIENCE AND 
COMMUNITY IMPACTS
In outreach events in support of the IM, communi-
ty members expressed concern about traffic con-
gestion, speeding in residential areas, the safety 
and security of transit environments, constraints 
on walking and biking in certain parts of the City, 
and traffic-induced air-quality and noise impacts. 
These concerns speak to how community mem-
bers are experiencing the mobility network as 
motorists, transit users, pedestrian and bicyclists, 
and those living and working in proximity to trans-
portation facilities and services.
As important as it is to assess the technical and 
quantitative aspects of the mobility network (e.g., 
collision data, capacity, frequency of service, 
travel times), it is equally important to understand 
the qualitative experience of those who utilize 
the network, as well as those indirectly impacted 
by transportation facilities and services. The IM 
seeks to enhance the user experience by not only 
expanding mobility options, but also ensuring that 
these options are safe, comfortable, accessible, 
affordable, and visually appealing, as long as they 
are feasible in terms of funding, adequate right-
of-way, geometrics, etc. The IM further seeks to 
minimize the impacts that transportation facilities 
and services can have on adjacent areas and the 
people who live and work in these areas. IM goals, 
policies, and actions have been informed by user 
comfort, safety, accessibility, and linkages with 
other modes, as well as education and health and 
wellness.
Assessing the performance of the mobility net-
work from the point-of-view of the user helps us 
appreciate what it takes to navigate the network, 
make the most of what it has to offer, and do so in 
a way that is expeditious, cost-effective, and pleas-
ant. Understanding how the mobility network can 
impact surrounding areas helps us determine how 
best to integrate transportation facilities and ser-
vices into the fabric of the community.

THE NEXUS BETWEEN LAND USE 
AND MOBILITY
No discussion of mobility is complete without 
consideration of the ways in which land use plan-
ning affects the nature, scale, and configuration 
of the mobility network. How localities regulate 
development, maintain open space, and deal with 
natural features (e.g., landforms, water bodies, 
ecosystems) largely determines where transporta-
tion facilities can be sited, what modes of travel 
these facilities can accommodate, and how these 
facilities are accessed and utilized. Land use pat-
terns can both provide for and constrain mobility 
options. Moreover, land use patterns can accom-
modate the mobility needs and preferences of 
some community members while leaving other 
community members without convenient and 
cost-effective mobility options. Development is 
often sited in areas where active transportation 
and transit are not viable options, and the built 
environment is often designed only to accommo-
date automobiles. Development on hillsides and 
mesas can make walking and biking less appeal-
ing, even when active transportation facilities 
are present. Separating housing from commercial 
districts, recreational facilities, and other desti-
nations can encourage residents to rely solely on 
their automobiles to access essential goods, ser-
vices, and amenities. Localities are learning from 
these problems and working to better coordinate 
land use decisions with the existing and planned 
components of their mobility networks.
The IM has been prepared in conjunction with the 
Efficient and Compatible Land Use Element (ECLU), 
the Smart and Sustainable Corridors Specific Plan, 
and the South Morro Hills Community Plan, which 
collectively establish the City’s overarching strate-
gy for accommodating both housing and non-resi-
dential growth through 2050. These three land use 
planning documents promote the common goals 
of efficient land use, an activated public realm, 
appropriate transitions between areas featuring 
different land use types and intensities, preser-
vation and enhancement of sensitive habitat, and 
conservation of farmland. Progress toward these 
land use goals supports an integrated mobility 
network by limiting the extension of roadways, 
increasing demand for transit, creating more walk-
able and bikeable environments, and allowing for 
“mobility hubs” where a range of transportation 
modes and complementary land uses converge 
to provide travelers with convenient connections 
and a range of commercial services and public 
amenities. In turn, a mobility network offering 
a wide range of transportation options supports 
efficient land use by encouraging high-density 
residential and mixed-use development in prox-
imity to transit and active transportation facilities. 
Combined, the land use strategies and multimodal 
mobility network can contribute towards shorter 
trip distances, less vehicular demand, and reduced 
greenhouse gas emissions which are consistent 
with the goals of Senate Bill 743, discussed further 
under the Legislative Context section below.
Situated at the periphery of the San Diego region 
and featuring a dispersed, low-intensity develop-
ment pattern, the City of Oceanside has been an 
auto-dependent community for generations. The 
City’s built environment extends over much of its 
43 square miles of land area, necessitating costly 
and often uneconomical extensions of roads, side-
walks, bus service, etc., making it difficult to focus 
transportation investments in those areas where 
they can provide the greatest benefit, and often 
limiting mobility options for those least able to 
afford private automobiles. Moreover, the City’s 
hilly terrain, water-based environments, and 
wildland areas create natural barriers to mobility 
and access. For example, while the hillside neigh-
borhoods of Loma Alta, Fire Mountain, and Mira 
Costa are served by NCTD’s SPRINTER rail ser-
vice, many residents are not able or willing to walk 
or bike the long distances or steep slopes between 
their homes and this transit option.

ACTIVE TRANSPORTATION PLAN 
(ATP) AND TRAILS MASTER PLAN 
The IM will be supported by an Active 
Transportation Plan (ATP) and a Trails Master 
Plan, both of which will promote improvements 
that connect pedestrians and bicyclists to com-
mercial districts and community facilities. The 
ATP will consolidate the City’s Pedestrian and 
Bicycle Master Plans and identify priority projects 
that close gaps in the City’s active transportation 
infrastructure. The Trails Master Plan will focus 
on dedicated trail connections between neighbor-
hoods and nearby recreational facilities – a key 
objective of the City’s recently updated Parks and 
Recreation Master Plan – while also facilitating the 
completion of the Coastal Rail Trail and the Inland 
Rail Trail.  
PURPOSE AND INTENT
As its title suggests, the Integrated Mobility 
Element (IM) promotes a citywide mobility net-
work that effectively integrates all modes of 
travel, including driving, walking, biking, transit 
use, ride-hailing services, and powered micro-mo-
bility options. A fundamental goal of the IM is to 
make it easier for travelers to both access and 
transition between a broad range of mobility 
modes that deliver them to the places they need 
and want to go in a timely fashion. To this end, the 
IM seeks to facilitate a resilient and reliable trans-
portation system that provides safe, convenient, 
efficient, and cost-effective travel options to all 
users, including young people, seniors, the dis-
abled, low-income earners, those living or working 
in under-resourced areas, and those who choose 
not to own private automobiles.
Legislative Context
Since the City’s last comprehensive General 
Plan Update, several key planning initiatives and 
state legislative actions have been adopted that 
redefine the way local transportation planning 
is carried out. Examples include Assembly Bill 
1358 – Complete Streets Act, Senate Bill 375 – 
Sustainable Communities and Climate Protection 
Act, and Senate Bill 743 – Environmental Quality.
AB 1358
In September of 2008, the State of California 
approved AB 1358 – the Complete Streets Act. 
Effective January 1, 2011, AB 1358 requires city 
or county legislative bodies to plan for a balanced, 
multi-modal transportation network that meets 
the needs of all users of streets, roads, and high-
ways. “All users” is defined to include motorists, 
pedestrians, bicyclists, children, persons with dis-
abilities, seniors, movers of commercial goods, and 
users of public transportation, in a manner that is 
suitable to the rural, suburban, or urban context of 
the general plan.

SB 375
SB 375 was also adopted in 2008, requiring metro-
politan planning organizations (MPO) to formulate 
a “sustainable community strategy” (SCS) as part of 
their regional transportation plan (RTP). The SCS is 
intended to identify how the region will achieve tar-
geted reductions in greenhouse gas emissions from 
automobiles and light trucks through coordinated 
development patterns and transportation systems.
Efforts at the regional level have embraced the sus-
tainable and multimodal transportation emphasis 
directed by these legislative actions. The San Diego 
Association of Governments (SANDAG) is responsi-
ble for developing the San Diego region’s SCS. The 
SCS consists of the following five building blocks:
•	 A land use pattern that accommodates our 
region’s future employment and housing needs 
and protects sensitive habitats and resource 
areas.
•	 A transportation network of public transit, 
Managed Lanes and highways, local streets, 
bikeways, and walkways built and maintained 
with reasonably expected funding.
•	 M a n a g i n g  d e m a n d s  o n  t h e  r e g i o n’s 
transportation system (also known as 
Transportation Demand Management, or 
TDM) in ways that reduce or eliminate traffic 
congestion during peak periods of demand.
•	 Managing region’s transportation system (also 
known as Transportation System Management, 
or TSM) through measures that maximize the 
overall efficiency of the transportation network.
•	 Innovative pricing policies, and other measures 
designed to reduce the number of miles 
people travel in their vehicles, as well as traffic 
congestion during peak periods of demand.
SANDAG is currently underway with the 2025 
Regional Plan which will be an update of the 2021 
Regional Transportation Plan, San Diego Forward.

the regional vision and further encourage sustain-
able development, including transportation infra-

a variety of funding sources.

The 5 Big Moves are at the heart of the Regional 
Plan vision. These key strategies envision a bal-
anced transportation network that leverages tech-
nology to create safe, efficient, and socially equi-
table transportation system that increases access 
to opportunity and helps achieve environmental 
mandates. Aligning with the Regional Plan vision 
will help position Oceanside to compete for grant 
funds and leverage regional investments. The 5 
Big Moves consist of the following concepts:
•	 Complete Corridors provide a variety of travel 
choices and use technology to manage how 
highways and major roads are used in real time. 
They provide a balance of dedicated, safe space 
for everyone, including freight vehicles and 
people who walk, bike, drive, ride transit, and 
use flexible fleets.
•	 Transit Leap could create a complete network 
of fast, high-capacity, high-frequency transit 
services that connect major residential areas 
with employment centers and attractions 
throughout the San Diego region.
•	 Mobility Hubs are places of connectivity where 
different travel options come together. They 
provide an integrated suite of mobility services, 
amenities, and supporting technologies to better 
connect high-frequency transit to an individual’s 
origin or destination.
•	 Flexible Fleets build on the popularity of shared 
mobility services such as on-demand ride-share, 
bike-share, and scooter-share. These services 
can make it easier to connect to high-speed 
transit and other important destinations by 
providing a last-mile connection.
•	 Next Operating System (OS) is a digital platform 
that uses technology and data to connect and 
manage different modes of transportation to 
improve overall efficiency and accessibility for 
people and goods to move throughout the region.
SB 743
SB 743 was signed into law in September 
2013, making several changes to the California 
Environmental Quality Act (CEQA). This bill removes 
vehicular delay, level of service (LOS), parking and 
other vehicular capacity measures as metrics of 
transportation system impacts for mixed-use, infill 
or transit-oriented development projects. Vehicle 
miles traveled (VMT) is considered the new analy-
sis metric used to measure transportation impacts. 
VMT reflects the automobile distance attributable 
to a project. The intent is to balance the needs 
of congestion management with statewide goals 
related to infill development, active transportation 
and transit use, and the reduction of greenhouse 
gas emissions. Consistent with state mandates,

Benefits of an Integrated Mobility 
Network
A more integrated mobility network should 
reduce dependence on the private automobile, 
which is the principal source of traffic congestion, 
air pollution, GHG emissions, and fatal or severe 
crashes in the San Diego region. Better integra-
tion of mobility modes should foster more active 
transportation, which in turn should contribute 
to better public health outcomes. By providing 
the workforce with greater range and flexibility, a 
more integrated mobility network should foster a 
better correspondence between workers and the 
jobs for which they are qualified, thereby enhanc-
ing overall economic productivity. Such a network 
should also contribute to worker productivity by 
reducing commute times and affording transit 
riders the option of working while en route. Given 
the myriad benefits of an integrated mobility net-
work, the goals, policies, and implementing actions 
of the IM center around and collectively further a 
vision of all modes of travel being linked as safely 
and seamlessly as possible.
Flexible Policies and Actions
Given the ongoing evolution of both the region-
al mobility network and mobility-oriented poli-
cies and programs at the federal, state, and local 
levels, the IM has been designed to be flexible 
and responsive to change. While the goals of the 
IM point in a specific direction – that is, toward 
a multimodal mobility network that is at once 
accessible, efficient, resilient, and adaptable – 
most IM policies and actions are crafted to allow 
for adjustments that address unforeseen circum-
stances, take advantage of new opportunities, and 
effectively meet the moment. Implementation of 
the IM should adhere to key principles (e.g., safety, 
equity, cost effectiveness) and, at the same time, 
remain mindful of and receptive to different ways 
of upholding and furthering these principles. As 
techniques, technologies, and funding mecha-
nisms continue to evolve, IM policies and actions 
will need to be interpreted and applied in ways 
that leverage advancements while aligning with 
the overarching IM direction.

The IM establishes a vision for the future of trans-
portation in the City of Oceanside. The IM out-
lines goals, policies, and recommendations meant 
to foster a highly integrated mobility network that 
supports safe, reliable, and convenient transitions 
between different modes of travel. Oceanside’s 
mobility network should not only connect local 
transportation facilities and services, but also link 
these facilities and services to the regional mobili-
ty network – e.g., Interstate 5, State Routes 76 and 
78, NCTD bus and rail service, Metrolink, Amtrak, 
etc. The IM identifies a wide range of actions the 
City can undertake to expand mobility options 
for residents, workers, and visitors, and provide 
ready and equitable access to all modes of travel 
– i.e., walking, micro-mobility (including bicycling), 
public and private transit, ridesharing, and the pri-
vate automobile. 
With the City’s roadway network largely built 
out, and with few opportunities to add road-
way capacity for automobiles, the IM focuses on 
improving the efficiency of the roadway network 
while encouraging community members to choose 
healthier and more sustainable modes of travel. By 
increasing active transportation, micro-mobility, 
and transit use, the City will reduce dependence 
on the automobile, and, in turn, reduce congestion 
on Oceanside’s roadways. 
The IM also promotes the transition to low and 
zero-emission vehicles, encouraging the expansion 
of electric and hydrogen vehicle charging facilities 
and exploring ways to motivate community mem-
bers to choose vehicles powered by clean and 
renewable energy. 
It is important to note that the City’s ability to 
implement the goals, policies, and recommenda-
tions of the IM depend upon numerous factors, 
including funding, physical constraints, health and 
safety considerations, and other potential limita-
tions. In keeping with state and regional mobility 
goals, standards, and initiatives, the policy direc-
tion charted by the IM is consciously ambitious 
and forward-thinking. Creating a more efficient, 
sustainable, and equitable mobility network will 
require an openness to change, a willingness to 
explore possibilities, and a commitment to bold 
and purposeful action. While some of the recom-
mendations promulgated by the IM may prove 
to be infeasible, or only partially achievable, the 
overall success of the City’s efforts to enhance 
mobility will not be contingent upon any single 
project or initiative. Rather, success will be based 
on the totality of the City’s achievements.

4.2	Active 
Transportation
Active transportation can be defined as non-mo-
torized movement powered by human energy. 
Active transportation facilities include sidewalks, 
trails, and bicycle lanes.
Active transportation can reduce travel costs, 
lessen roadway congestion, improve public health 
outcomes, and enhance public safety and securi-
ty by activating public spaces and placing “eyes 
on the street.” Communities that prioritize active 
transportation tend to be healthier, as it enables 
residents to be more physically active in their 
daily routines and provides cleaner air to breathe. 
Active transportation systems also foster econom-
ic health by creating dynamic, connected commu-
nities with a high quality of life that catalyzes small 
business development, increases property values, 
sparks tourism, and encourages corporate invest-
ment that attracts a talented, highly educated 
workforce.
Impediments to active transportation in Oceanside 
include dispersed land use patterns, the sepa-
ration of residential and non-residential uses, 
topography, state highways, gaps in sidewalk, and 
minimal or no separation from vehicular traffic 
in most environments. To meaningfully increase 
walking and bicycling, the City can expand active 
transportation infrastructure and facilitate more 
efficient and balanced land use within already 
urbanized areas (e.g., major commercial corridors). 
Where feasible, the City can provide more space 
for walking and bicycling within the public right-of-
way. The City’s robust and growing trail network, 
featuring the San Luis Rey River Trail, the Coastal 
Rail Trail, and the future Inland Rail Trail, can also 
be leveraged to promote walking and bicycling.

The pedestrian realm shapes every journey. 
Regardless of the primary mode of transporta-

walk from our origins to our destinations, or to/
from bicycles, transit stops or our own vehicles. 
Walking is also a common transportation option 
used for school commutes, with student travel 
often occurring when vehicle traffic is at its peak, 
underscoring the significance of safe and comfort-
able walking environments.
Providing enhanced pedestrian infrastructure 
throughout the City, coupled with complimentary

tunities for both transportation and recreation 
while enhancing pedestrian safety. Pedestrian 
infrastructure consists of sidewalks, crosswalks, 
curb ramps, and trails. Additional features, such 
as pedestrian countdown signal heads, curb 
extensions, lighting, and signage aid in facilitating 
the enhancement of pedestrian crossings, while 
amenities like landscaping, seating, and buffers 
can enhance overall comfort. Descriptions and

BICYCLE NETWORK
Bicycling presents a variety of benefits for individ-
uals and communities alike. It provides a practical 
option for local trips, offering a potential alterna-
tive to traditional vehicle travel while also con-
tributing to a reduction in traffic congestion and 
the associated greenhouse gas emissions. Bicycles 
play an important role in connecting people to 
transit stops and final destinations, serving as an 
integral part of first/last-mile transit connectivity. 
This is especially significant for SPRINTER stations, 
which offer a high-quality transit option that might 
be located beyond convenient walking distances.
Bicycling can also have a positive impact on school-
age children’s daily commute. By providing a reli-
able means of transportation to and from school, it 
can contribute to the physical well-being of youth 
while also alleviating vehicle trips from the trans-
portation system during peak commute hours. 
Additionally, the relatively low cost of owning 
and operating a bicycle can make it an accessible 
transportation option for youth and lower-income 
residents.
To fully realize these benefits, it is necessary 
to establish a well-connected, convenient, and 
comfortable bicycle network that accommodates 
individuals of varying skill levels. Bicycle facilities 
should be designed within the context of the sur-
rounding environment while providing convenient 
connections between origins and destinations, as 
well as from one facility to another.
Secure bicycle parking is another critical compo-
nent that can factor into one’s decision to make 
a trip via bicycle. The uncertainty of adequate, 
secure bicycle parking availability at the end of 
one’s trip can deter bicycle owners from making 
bicycle trips. The importance of bicycle parking 
has increased with the prevalence of electric bicy-
cles, which has broadened the appeal of bicycling 
to a wider audience while also increasing the value 
of the typical bicycle.
Caltrans recognizes four classifications of bicycle 
facilities: Class I Bike Path, Class II Bike Lane, Class 
III Bike Route, and Class IV Separated Bikeway 
(also called Cycle Track).
Example images and descriptions are provided for 
each classification followed by the bicycle network 
recommendations in Figures 4-4 and 4-5.
Bike Path: Also referred to as a multi-use 
path or shared-use path, a Class I facility 
provides a completely separated right-
of-way designed for the exclusive 
use of bicycles and pedestrians with 
crossflows by motorists minimized. A 
bike path can provide connections 
where roadways are non-existent or 
unable to support bicycle travel. The 
minimum paved width for a two-way 
bike path is eight feet, with a two-foot 
wide graded area adjacent to the 
pavement on both sides of the path.

4.3	Vehicular Movement
Despite ongoing efforts at all levels of government 
and civil society to increase active transportation, 
transit ridership, and other alternative modes of 
travel, most community members still rely heavily 
on the private automobile. As long as this remains 
the principal means of transportation in Oceanside 
and the San Diego region, it is incumbent upon the 
City to maintain adequate roadway capacity for 
automobiles, in order to ensure timely emergency 
response and provide sufficient access to employ-
ment, commercial goods and services, healthcare, 
public facilities, etc. 
Given that the City’s roadway network is largely 
built-out, in terms of both geographic reach and 
lane capacity, minimizing congestion and delay 
will require enhanced transportation management 
systems (e.g., signal timing optimization, consol-
idating driveways and other features that create 
traffic friction) as well as improvements and ser-
vices that make active transportation and transit 
use more practical and appealing. In short, keeping 
traffic moving on the City’s major roadways will 
require both greater efficiency and a reduction in 
per-capita vehicle miles traveled. Thus, accommo-
dating additional automobile trips cannot come 
at the expense of active transportation facilities, 
transit service, and other mobility options that can 
reduce our reliance on the automobile. 
The recommended roadway network is intended 
to align with planned multi-modal improvements, 
accommodate forecasted transportation demands 
and travel pattern shifts associated with planned 
land uses, and contribute to sustained economic 
activity and growth. Changes to the City’s network 
are intended to be complemented by interstate and 
state highway recommendations, such as managed 
lanes along Interstate 5 through Oceanside, and 
additional grade-separated crossings of SR-76.

The vehicular network utilizes a system of six 
classifications to identify the type of service each 
facility is intended to provide  : Expressway, Prime 
Arterial, Major Arterial, Secondary Collector, 
Collector, and Local Streets. The classifications are 
supported by the City of Oceanside Engineering 
Design Manual which provides design guidelines 
that dictate the physical characteristics of each 
facility. 
Expressway – An Expressway is designed to pro-
vide express direct travel through the City with 
four to six lanes in each direction. Expressways 
are divided highways with raised medians and fully 
controlled access at intersections. Grade separa-
tion of intersections can help improve multimod-
al safety and optimize Expressway operations. 
Expressways are constructed and maintained by 
Caltrans.
Prime Arterial – A Prime Arterial is designed 
to provide regional, sub-regional, and intra-city 
travel. They typically have six lanes of travel, raised 
and landscaped medians, shoulders for emergency 
parking and bike facilities, and restricted access 
(e.g., fewer intersections, no driveways). On-street 
parking is prohibited.
Major Arterial – A Major Arterial is designed to 
provide intra-city and sub-regional connectivity 
and provide some property access in select areas. 
Driveways interfacing with these roads may have 
certain restrictions to ensure proper roadway 
functions. They typically have four or six lanes of 
travel, raised, landscaped, and striped medians, 
and shoulders for emergency parking and bike 
facilities. Left-turn movements are typically pro-
tected along Major Arterials.
Secondary Collector – A Secondary Collector is 
designed for intra-city travel as a link between 
arterial and collector roadways. They are com-
monly lined with driveways providing direct access 
to abutting properties; however, that is not its pri-
mary purpose. The typical design features include 
four travel lanes with a center two-way left-turn 
lane or left-turn pockets, and bike facilities.
Collector – A Collector is designed to connect 
local streets with the adjacent arterial street net-
work. These are usually two travel lanes with or 
without a center two-way left-turn lane and may 
include bicycle facilities. On-street parking may 
be present. Collector streets frequently have 
driveways, which provide direct access to abutting 
properties (although the desire is to limit access 
where possible to reduce potential conflicts). A 
Collector can also be a two-lane one-way street, 
such as the one-way couplet on Mission Avenue 
between Coast Highway and Clementine Street 
and Seagaze Drive between Coast Highway and 
Clementine Street.
Local Street – Local Streets are low capacity, low 
speed streets whose main purpose is to provide 
direct access to abutting properties. Local Streets 
typically do not provide or encourage vehicular 
connectivity beyond providing access to the near-
est intersecting higher capacity roadway (most 
often, these are Collectors). The typical design 
standards include the provision of two travel 
lanes, parking lanes on both sides of the street, 
and direct driveway access.
The existing functional roadway classifications as of 
2021 are depicted in Figure 4-6. Table 4-1 identi-
fies recommended roadway network modifications 
while Figure 4-7 displays the planned roadway clas-
sifications citywide. Figure 4-8 shows the planned 
lane geometrics of these roadways at key intersec-
tion locations within the City. 
A Transportation Impact Study (TIS) and Traffic 
Operations Analysis (TOA) technical report was 
prepared to understand and document network 
operations under buildout of the planned General 
Plan land uses and vehicular network. Consistent 
with SB 743, the TIS utilizes a VMT-based analysis 
to identify and document potential transportation 
impacts and applicable mitigation measures. The 
TOA is a LOS based analysis of the IM roadway 
network and key intersections. Figure 4-9 displays 
the IM roadway segment and intersection LOS 
results. The full TIS/TOA document is provided as 
Appendix B.

4.4	Transit
Compared to most suburban cities in California 
and across the nation, Oceanside features an 
extraordinary range of transit services. Founded 
as a railroad town in the 1880s, the City lies along 
the LOSSAN rail corridor, which extends from San 
Diego to San Luis Obispo. NCTD’s SPRINTER rail 
line extends through the Oceanside Boulevard 
Corridor and connects the City to Vista, San 
Marcos, and Escondido. Located just outside the 
Oceanside Transit Center (OTC) is one of the most 
robust transit hubs in the state, supporting four 
separate rail services as well as NCTD’s Breeze 
bus service. It is anticipated that the pending 
redevelopment of the OTC will inspire more tran-
sit ridership by creating a more comfortable and 
appealing environment and providing “mobility 
hub” amenities (e.g., wayfinding, bicycle storage, 
Wi-Fi, retail venues).
The City has piloted on-demand shuttle service in 
coastal areas and is working with NCTD to expand 
on-demand and fixed route shuttle service around 
El Corazon. The City recognizes that reducing 
dependence on the private automobile depends 
in large part upon the provision of convenient and 
reliable “first-mile/last-mile” connections between 
residential areas, employments hubs, and transit 
facilities. Establishing and maintaining these con-
nections will require coordination between the 
City, NCTD, SANDAG, private micro-transit oper-
ators, and the local business community. 
Despite the abundance of transit service in 
Oceanside, transit ridership is low. While the 
Oceanside Transit Center (OTC) and other transit 
facilities in Oceanside accommodate several thou-
sand bus and train passengers on a daily basis, tran-
sit trips make up a very small percentage of total 
trips in Oceanside. This is in part due to the fact that 
transit service is concentrated in certain areas of the 
City (e.g., coastal areas, the Oceanside Boulevard 
corridor) and limited in other areas. The OTC lies 
on the western edge of the City, while most of the 
City’s housing stock, job centers, and public facili-
ties are located in inland areas. To increase transit 
ridership, better connections between the OTC 
and inland areas of the City must be established. 
Moreover, the bulk of the City’s future housing and 
employment growth must be channeled into those 
areas where transit service currently exists and can 
be expanded (e.g., major commercial corridors).

Transit is largely planned for at the regional level 
and guided by the Regional Plan. The current 
Regional Plan recommends numerous upgrades 
to transit infrastructure and service around the 
region. Within Oceanside, this includes recommen-
dations to improve the COASTER’s frequency to 
30-minutes during commute peak periods and the 
SPRINTER’s frequency to 10-minutes throughout 
the day. Double-tracking of the SPRINTER rail and 
grade separations at El Camino Real and Melrose 
Drive will contribute to the improved services. 
Existing Breeze bus routes 101, 303, and 315 
are planned for upgrade to NextGen Rapid bus 
routes. NextGen Rapid bus routes will feature 
10-minute frequencies all day, representing a 
significant increase in service. To improve travel 
time and operating reliability, NextGen Rapid 
bus routes typically have consolidated stop pat-
terns, improved equipment, and infrastructure to 
decrease dwell time delay, and may also be sup-
ported by transit priority treatments in the road-
way. Potential Flex Lanes have been identified 
along segments of Mission Avenue and El Camino 
Real to help improve operating reliability of the 
NextGen Rapid routes through exclusive transit 
lanes and/or transit priority signals.
The portion of Oceanside generally to the west 
of El Camino Real is designated as a Mobility Hub 
Area at the regional level. SANDAG defines mobil-
ity hubs as places of connectivity where mobility 
services, technology, and a variety of amenities 
create a landing spot for travelers to connect with 
high-frequency transit services, bike and rideshare 
options, and a variety of other modes of travel. 
The combination of transit services and land use 
patterns and intensities can be indicative of a 
need for intensified amenities to facilitate multi-
modal trips and help address “first-mile/last-mile” 
transit access. Examples of amenities to consider 
intensifying near transit stops within the mobility 
hub area include long-term and short-term bicycle 
parking, dedicated space for pick-up and drop-off, 
comfortable waiting areas, and staging of flexible 
fleet equipment (e.g., shared vehicles, bicycles, 
and scooters), and trip planning information.

4.5	Transportation 
Demand 
Management (TDM)
Transportation Demand Management (TDM) 
refers to a variety of strategies and programs 
intended to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, 
peak hour vehicular trips, and vehicle miles trav-
eled. Transportation System Management (TSM) 
is an approach to addressing roadway congestion 
without increasing roadway size. TDM measures 
can come in the form of marketing and promotion-
al programs, incentives, and transportation ameni-
ties and services. TSM strategies typically include 
operational modifications – such as traffic signal 
coordination – or minor roadway geometry chang-
es. The City of Oceanside partners with SANDAG 
to administer the regional TDM program. Examples 
of programs currently offered include:
•	 Employer Commuter Programs – Assistance is 
offered to employers to establish customized 
commuter benefits and telework programs, 
including incentives, subsidies, and free classes 
for employees who commute via sustainable 
transportation modes.
•	 Guaranteed Ride Home – People commuting via 
carpool, vanpool, transit, walking, or bicycling 
are eligible to enroll in the program which will 
provide reimbursement for a direct ride home 
from work (e.g., taxi, rideshare, rental car) in the 
case of a qualifying emergency.
•	 Commuter Vanpools – SANDAG organizes 
vanpools for groups of five or more people, 
helping commuters save money on commutes 
and enable access to High Occupancy Vehicle 
(HOV) lanes.
•	 Transit Services – SANDAG plans and promotes 
transit services at the regional level for the two 
major transit agencies in the region, NCTD and 
MTS. The Try Transit program provides eligible 
employees with a one-month pass to try transit 
options.
•	 Bike Services – Bicycling is promoted as a 
form of transportation through a regional bike 
map, secure bike parking at transit stations – 
including SPRINTER and COASTER stations, 
bicycle education programs for schools and 
employers, and funding for regional facilities 
such as the Coastal Rail Trail.

4.6	Goods Movement
The efficient movement of goods supports eco-
nomic development, synergies between local 
businesses, and convenient access to commercial 
products. It is therefore essential that the City’s 
roadway network provide adequate access for 
delivery vehicles. 
The Oceanside Municipal Airport also contrib-
utes to the local economy and offers recreational 
opportunities. The airport features a dual runway, 
located on 73-acres in northern Oceanside. The 
airport is owned by the City of Oceanside and 
offers transient fuel services, aircraft parking, 
hangar rentals, and filming opportunities. Non-
aircraft access to the airport is provided via Airport 
Road and Benet Road, just north of SR-76. The 
Airport Master Plan guides the extent, type, and 
schedule of development needed to accommodate 
anticipated future aviation demand. The City will 
accommodate changes in surface transportation 
activity resulting from future airport development. 
Oceanside is home to numerous warehousing and 
distribution facilities that generate freight vehicle 
traffic. There are also concrete batch plants, con-
struction materials yards, nurseries, and other uses 
that contribute to the presence of freight vehicles 
on City streets. With large industrial parks and 
shopping centers located in the heart of the City, 
removed from Interstate 5 and State Routes 76 
and 78, freight vehicles rely on El Camino Real, 
Oceanside Boulevard, College Boulevard, and 
other local roadways to move goods into and out 
of the City. While these roads are designed to 
accommodate such vehicles, freight vehicle traf-
fic can generate noise and other emissions that 
impact nearby properties occupied by businesses, 
homes, schools, and other uses.
City of Oceanside Traffic Code Section 12.3 pro-
hibits freight vehicles of a gross weight more than 
ten thousand pounds from utilizing specific street 
segments. In residential areas impacted by freight 
vehicle traffic, the City can post signage, intro-
duce traffic calming features, and increase police 
patrols.
