5.1	Background
Although more than 70 percent of Oceanside’s land 
area is now developed, the City maintains a diverse 
natural environment that supports a wide range 
of plant and animal life, water resources, scenic 
vistas, and recreation and educational activities. 
Oceanside also includes working lands, including 
roughly 3,500 acres of farmland in the South Morro 
Hills Neighborhood Planning Area. Much of the 
City’s open space and natural habitat connects to 
undeveloped areas in neighboring jurisdictions, 
including Carlsbad, Vista, Fallbrook, Bonsall, and 
MCB Camp Pendleton. This connectivity contrib-
utes to sub-regional wildlife corridors, recreational 
trail networks, and a profound sense of openness. 
Additionally, the City’s major waterways connect 
to the Pacific Ocean, providing for natural drainage, 
the intermingling of freshwater and marine habitats, 
and pedestrian and bicycle linkages between inland 
and coastal areas.     
Conserving and effectively managing these natural 
resources contributes to public health and well-
ness, ecological balance, community identity, visual 
quality, and quality of life. Maintaining open spaces 
also supports the City’s efforts to promote efficient 
land use within already urbanized areas. Preserving 
natural landscapes and protecting working  open 
space lands also helps to address climate change 
through carbon sequestration within the soil and 
plant life that occur there.
Of the many natural features that characterize 
Oceanside, the best known may be the City’s three 
miles of coastline on the Pacific Ocean. The City’s 
coastline features estuarine habitat, a series of dis-
tinct beaches, numerous high-quality surf breaks, 
and a wide range of coastal amenities, including 
the Oceanside Small Craft Harbor, the Oceanside 
Municipal Pier, the Junior Seau Amphitheater, and 
several public parks. Inland areas of the City feature 
riparian areas, and a variety of natural landforms. 
Inland areas accommodating both natural habitat 
and recreational amenities include El Corazon, 
Guajome Regional Park, Oak Riparian Park, Whelan 
Lake, and the El Camino Golf Course. 
Undeveloped land throughout the City is comprised 
of conserved native and non-native vegetation 
communities of varying sensitivity, including upland 
and wetland habitats, and unvegetated areas such 
as waterways, lagoons, and beaches. These vegeta-
tion communities are found primarily along the San

Luis Rey River, tributary water bodies, and scattered 
hillsides and canyons throughout the City.
Three waterways extending inland from the Pacific 
Ocean — the San Luis Rey River, Loma Alta Creek, 
and Buena Vista Creek and Lagoon — accommodate 
extensive wetland and riparian habitat, passive-use 
open space areas, and alternative transportation 
facilities (e.g., San Luis Rey River Bike Trail, NCTD’s 
Sprinter rail service). These waterways also serve 
to break up the City’s built environment and delin-
eate neighborhoods along watershed boundaries, 
thereby creating visual relief and contributing to a 
sense of place.
The City’s upland terrestrial habitats include native 
and non-native grasslands, chaparral, coastal sage 
scrub and oak woodlands, which are distinct biomes 
that accommodate a wide range of endemic plants 
and wildlife.  Native and non-native grasslands are 
dominated by grasses such as purple needlegrass, 
bromes and wild oats. These habitats often sup-
port a high number of special-status plants such as 
thread-leaved brodiaea, a Federally threatened and 
California State endangered bulbiferous perennial. 
Chaparral is characterized by dense evergreen veg-
etation that includes chamise, scrub oak, mountain 
mahogany, wild lilac, holly-leaf cherry, and toyon. 
Flora in coastal sage scrub environments tends to 
be drought deciduous, with California sagebrush, 
white and black sage, bush sunflower, California 
buckwheat, and coyote brush being predominant 
species.  Oak woodlands are primarily dominated by 
coast live oak with various grassy or shrub under-
story species. These upland habitats are inhabited 
by a variety of invertebrate, avian, reptile, and 
mammalian species, some of which are identified as 
threatened and endangered.
Approximately 12 percent of Oceanside’s total land 
area is devoted to agricultural use, virtually all of 
which lies in the northeast corner of the City. South 
Morro Hills is one of the last remnants of coastal 
farmland in San Diego County, and its rolling hills 
and scenic beauty provide a visual counterpoint 
to the City’s built environment. However, this area 
faces extreme development pressure, and the 
local farming community is challenged by the high 
cost of water, foreign competition, an uncertain 
labor market, Federal and State regulations, and

zoning allows for 2.5-acre residential subdivision,

PLANNING CONTEXT
The General Plan envisions future growth to occur 
largely through infill and redevelopment in already 
urbanized areas, enabling open space conserva-
tion and the restoration and long-term manage-
ment of sensitive habitat, farmland conservation, 
and the provision of additional public open space. 
Integrating protections for the natural environment 
into development practices—including buffers along 
creeks and natural resources, water conserving 
landscapes, natural stormwater management, air 
quality improvements, permeable paving, and ener-
gy-efficient building—will further support resilient 
natural systems. 
Community Priorities 
Extensive community engagement in support of 
the General Plan revealed broad interest in con-
serving the City’s natural resources, beaches, and 
open spaces for future generations of Oceansiders. 
The majority of community members participating 
in outreach activities expressed a strong desire 
to protect, restore, and manage sensitive habitat, 
underscoring the need to provide habitat connec-
tivity through the Wildlife Corridor Planning Zone 
(WCPZ), the City’s major watercourses, and other 
areas as opportunities arise. Community members 
encouraged the City to direct new development 
away from natural habitat to the extent feasible, 
and where new development abuts natural habitat, 
require  that all habitat impacts be fully mitigated. 
Many community members are also concerned 
about potable water supply, particularly in light 
of projected housing and non-residential growth. 
Beach erosion is another significant community 
concern.  In 2021, the City completed a sand nour-
ishment and retention feasibility study. The City is 
currently coordinating its beach restoration efforts 
through the RE:BEACH Oceanside initiative, doing 
so in collaboration  with downcoast cities to ensure 
mutual benefit.
No issue associated with the General Plan has 
generated more interest among community mem-
bers than the future of the City’s farmland. While 
opinions vary on the most appropriate and effec-
tive approach to farmland conservation, there is 
widespread community support for maintaining 
South Morro Hills as an agricultural district and 
boosting the economic viability of farming in this 
area. However, current zoning in South Morro 
Hills, established prior to this General Plan update, 
allows for residential development at a density of 
0.4 dwelling units per acre (2.5-acre lots), with no 
requirement that agricultural use be maintained. As 
production agriculture becomes increasingly diffi-
cult due to high labor costs, water shortages, and 
price undercutting from imported goods, private 
landowners may choose to subdivide their land for 
residential use rather than maintain agricultural 
operations. Some conversions of farmland to resi-
dential development are presently underway. 
Relationship to North County Multiple 
Habitat Conservation Program (MHCP)
Initially drafted in 2005 and revised in 2010 and in 
2016, Oceanside’s Draft Subarea Plan (Draft SAP) 
was meant to contribute to regional habitat con-
servation as outlined in the North County Multiple 
Habitat Conservation Program (MHCP), prepared 
by the San Diego Association of Governments. The 
MHCP seeks to conserve approximately 19,000 
acres of sensitive habitat in the cities of Carlsbad, 
Escondido, Oceanside, San Marcos, and Vista, 
roughly 2,500 acres of which lies within Oceanside.

implementation, which includes a lack of ade-

maintenance and restoration, as well as for staff 
resources, the City has decided not to pursue 
formal adoption of the Draft SAP. Instead, the City 
has incorporated the key principles and guidelines 
of the Draft SAP in the VSR. s. Specific changes to 
the City’s Code will include the development and 
implementation of a Biological Resource Protection

the policy goals and objectives of this VSR element. 
These include:
•	 Mitigation requirements for impacts to sensitive

and wildlife; and     
•	 Land use adjacency guidelines for development 
adjacent to sensitive habitats.
The Draft SAP identified properties where conser-
vation would best achieve biological goals with the 
least detrimental effects on other land use issues 
such as property rights and economic benefits. This 
approach involved careful examination of oppor-
tunities and constraints relative to incorporating 
biologically valuable lands into areas proposed for 
conservation. Alternatively, the City will update its 
conserved lands analysis, including the following 
efforts: reviewing the existing vegetation mapping, 
focused planning areas (FPAs), hardline and softline 
preserve areas, and pre-approved mitigation areas 
(PAMAs) identified in the Draft SAP. Revisions will 
then be made based on current conditions to the 
extent feasible.

Consistent with California Government Code 
Section 65040.2 and associated general plan 
guidelines, the Vital and Sustainable Resources 
Element (VSR) addresses the conservation and 
stewardship of natural resources and open space. 
The VSR outlines goals, policies, and actions that 
seek to protect, restore, and enhance the ecologi-
cal, recreational, and aesthetic value of Oceanside’s 
natural environment. The VSR also promotes the 
integration of natural elements into the City’s built 
environment (e.g., native and naturalized landscape, 
green infrastructure). The conservation and preser-
vation strategies presented in the VSR are expected 
to provide secondary benefits to the Oceanside 
community, including natural flood management 
and risk reduction, improved air and water quality, 
and enhanced community identity and well-being.

The City can better inform and support its land man-
agement decisions by understanding how the natural 
environment contributes to economic development, 
air and water quality, the resilience of the built envi-
ronment, positive public health outcomes, and other 
community priorities. Research shows that land con-
servation can enhance property values, boost tour-
ism, protect against natural hazards, promote active 
lifestyles, and reduce costs associated with health 
care, water treatment and distribution, emergency 
response, and other basic services. Moreover, the 
stewardship of conserved lands provides jobs and 
educational opportunities, while enhancing eligibility 
for Federal and State grant funding.
Humans depend upon biodiversity for survival. 
Biodiversity supplies the food we eat, the medicines 
that keep us healthy, and the materials we use for 
shelter, clothing, furnishings, etc. In an article enti-
tled “Benefits of Biodiversity to Human Health and 
Well-Being,” the National Parks Service (NPS) notes 
that in addition to supplying oxygen, storing carbon 
dioxide, purifying water, and modulating infectious

The NPS also cites studies indicating that contact

access to nature can help alleviate “nature deficit 
disorder”, a term coined by Richard Louv in his sem-

OPEN SPACE FOR CONSERVATION
Natural areas supporting sensitive plant and animal 
species extend throughout Oceanside, from coastal 
estuaries to upland habitat. Undeveloped lands (not 
including agricultural land)  that potentially support 
sensitive biological resources are generally concen-
trated along the City’s three main waterways—San 
Luis Rey River, Loma Alta Creek, and Buena Vista 
Creek—and in established preserves. These areas are 
either formally designated as open space, formally 
conserved as protected habitat, or owned and man-
aged by the City or other public or non-profit enti-
ties. In addition, privately owned lands not formally 
protected for conservation occur within Oceanside, 
and these lands may also support sensitive biological 
resources, including habitat for special-status species. 
The San Luis Rey River riparian corridor provides east-
west habitat connections across the City and to con-
served areas north of the river at Camp Pendleton. 
While there are stretches of connected open spaces 
along all three of the City’s major waterways, there 
are no major north-south open space connections, 
with the majority of open space throughout the City 
found in relatively small and fragmented patches, 
some of which are managed by homeowner associa-
tions or other residential management organizations. 
Figure 5-1 shows the City’s full network of open 
spaces including open space for conservation lands, 
open space for parks and recreation, and open space 
for agricultural use.
Conservation Framework
The formal conservation of natural resources and 
open space in Oceanside has largely occurred 
through the following mechanisms: capital improve-
ment program projects (e.g., parks, trails), the 
development review process, Federal law, and State 
statutes that protect listed species, and jurisdic-
tional waterways and tidelands through regulations 
enacted as a result of the Federal Endangered 
Species Act (FESA), the California Endangered 
Species Act (CESA), and the Clean Water Act. 
Following decades of sprawling development and 
associated public improvements (e.g., roadways, 
utilities, flood control structures), there are limited 
opportunities to add vacant property to the City’s 
open space inventory. The principal conservation 
priority in the coming decades will be maintaining 
existing open spaces, restoring habitats where they 
are degraded, and enhancing linkages between hab-
itat areas to facilitate wildlife movement. 
Figure 5-2 shows the conservation framework, 
which provides guidance for private development 
and capital improvement program projects by iden-
tifying existing and potential preserves. The follow-
ing paragraphs outline the areas identified by the 
City as important to the future of habitat conserva-
tion in the City and outline strategies for avoiding, 
minimizing, and mitigating impacts to such areas.

Focused Planning Areaas
The MHCP introduced the term “Focused Planning 
Area” (FPA) to collectively refer to biological 
resources or lands that are either already conserved 
or are targeted to be wholly or partially conserved, 
as contemplated in the Draft SAP. The FPAs are 
based on biological core and linkage areas evaluated 
in the MHCP. For purposes of this VSR , the term 
FPA includes the hardline preserves, softline pre-
serves, and pre-approved mitigation areas (PAMAs) 
from the Draft SAP.  It is inclusive of conservation 
banks, and some naturally vegetated lands within 
certain preserve planning zones targeted for con-
servation (Figure 5-2). Unlike the term “preserve,” 
the term “FPA” does not necessarily imply that all 
land within these properties will be, or needs to be, 
conserved.
Hardline Preserves
Hardline Preserves, identified in Figure 5-2, are 
high-quality habitat areas already formally con-
served and managed by land trusts. These pre-
serves are typically supported by non-wasting 
endowments. The General Plan seeks to strengthen 
and unify the management structure for these 
open spaces and ensure ongoing conservation  for 
properties the City is responsible for managing. The 
City continues to develop processes for gathering 
and tracking data associated with existing preserve 
lands managed by other entities subject to avail-
able resources. These data include the total acres 
of existing preserve lands, condition of habitat(s), 
prior or planned restoration activities, the formal 
land conservation mechanism used to protect that 
property (i.e., a recorded conservation easement), 
the status of any in-perpetuity endowment or 
other management funds, who holds and manages 
such funds, and the status of the preserve’s current 
resource management plan. 
Softline Preserves and Pre-Approved 
Mitigation Areas (PAMAs)
Areas designated as Softline Preserves and PAMAs 
in the Draft SAP have significant resource value, 
support natural vegetation communities, and/or are 
in areas that currently or may in the future support 
wildlife movement/habitat linkages. As such, these 
areas are targeted to be wholly or partially con-
served in conjunction with future development or 
capital improvement program projects. 
Softline Preserves and PAMAs as presented in 
the 2016 version of the Draft SAP were largely 
the same lands with only a few minor exceptions. 
During revisions to the Draft SAP in 2016, discus-
sions included the need to continue the Softline 
and PAMA distinction.  As such, the City no longer 
maintains a distinction between these two terms 
during future iterations of the conservation frame-
work mapping. These areas have not been updated 
since the Draft SAP; the City will revise and update 
these areas through this General Plan Update.

Conservation Approach Within FPAs
FPAs inside of the WCPZ will be the highest prior-
ity for acquisition and/or habitat restoration. FPAs 
outside of the WCPZ will be the second priority 
for acquisition. Development is allowed in softline 
preserves and PAMAs, subject to policies to avoid, 
minimize, and/or fully mitigate impacts. Onsite miti-
gation within the Softline Preserve areas and PAMAs 
is the preferred method to compensate for impacts 
to sensitive natural communities. Offsite mitigation 
is also permitted if it is directed to one of the follow-
ing areas (presented in order of decreasing priority): 
1.	  FPAs within the WCPZ and south of SR 76; 
2.	  FPAs within the WCPZ and north of SR 76; 
3.	 FPAs outside of the WCPZ; or 
4.	 A mitigation bank within the City.
The City will continue to identify potential funding 
sources for acquisition of these lands, either through 
dedicated City general funds, development fees 
when impacts occur to sensitive natural communi-
ties, or other means.  Potential funding sources for 
land acquisition may be clarified in future updates to 
this VSR Element.
Coastal Zone
Oceanside has a state-designated Coastal Zone, 
where the Federal Coastal Zone Management 
Act and California Coastal Act policies apply. 
The Coastal Act mandates the California Coastal 
Commission (CCC) to “protect, conserve, restore, 
and enhance” the State’s coastal resources.  Section 
30240 of the Coastal Act defines Environmentally 
Sensitive Habitat Areas (ESHAs) and states: “(a) 
Environmentally sensitive habitat areas shall be pro-
tected against any significant disruption of habitat 
values, and only uses dependent on those resources 
shall be allowed within those areas.” Land develop-
ment within the Coastal Zone is governed by the 
City’s Local Coastal Land Use Plan (LCLUP) and the 
Local Coastal Program (LCP); see these documents 
for more information on the Coastal Zone.  The LCP 
shall be updated by 2034 per State Senate Bill 272, 
which requires all coastal cities to develop sea level 
rise plans as part of their Local Coastal Programs.
In addition to these provisions, the City will require 
no-net-loss of coastal sage scrub, chaparral, or 
perennial native grassland within the Coastal Zone. 
Mitigation for impacts to any of these vegetation 
communities, when permitted, shall include a cre-
ation component that achieves the no-net-loss 
standard. Substantial restoration of highly degraded 
habitat whose biological functions and values have 
been lost may be substituted for creation subject 
to consultation and concurrence of the Wildlife 
Agencies and the CCC. Substantial restoration con-
trasts with enhancement, which includes weeding 
or planting of habitat that has retained its biological 
functions and values. 
Agricultural Exclusion Zone
This zone includes lands north of the San Luis Rey 
River that are designated for agricultural uses under 
this General Plan. The vast majority of this acreage is 
in South Morro Hills. Ongoing agricultural uses may 
continue in this Agricultural Exclusion Zone (AEZ) 
zone as long as they do not remove existing natural 
habitats. Discretionary actions or conversions to 
non-agricultural uses may invoke additional conser-
vation policies/guidelines, which include preserving, 
restoring or enhancing 100-foot buffers of natural 
vegetation adjacent to existing wetland vegetation 
communities. Discretionary actions may require an 
assessment of wildlife movement and habitat link-

VEGETATION AND WILDLIFE
Biological Resources
Despite the relatively built out state of the Planning 
Area, the existing open spaces play an important 
role in regional habitat connectivity and species 
conservation, due to strategic geographic loca-
tions and onsite biological resources. The most 
abundant natural habitat in the Planning Area is 
grassland (primarily nonnative/annual as opposed 
to native perennial grasslands), followed by coastal 
sage scrub, riparian habitats, and other wetlands. 
Habitats regulated by the California Department of 
Fish and Wildlife (CDFG), the US Fish and Wildlife 
Service (USFWS), and the Army Corps of Engineers 
(ACOE) within the Planning Area include coastal 
sage scrub, alkali marsh, freshwater marsh, riparian 
habitats, and habitats that are known to support 
species listed under either State or federal endan-
gered species acts.
The VSR focuses on the protection and manage-
ment of functional ecological communities, rather 
than preservation of individual species or isolated 
areas of habitat. At the same time, the VSR empha-
sizes coastal sage scrub and freshwater wetlands 
as key sanctuaries for sensitive species. Coastal 
sage scrub is concentrated near the City’s northern 
boundary (Benet Canyon, Tuley Canyon, Lawrence 
Canyon) and portions of the Oceanside Boulevard 
Corridor. Freshwater wetlands include Whalen 
Lake, Windmill Lake, Talone Lake, the San Luis Rey 
River, Loma Alta Creek, and Buena Vista Creek and 
Lagoon. Figure 5-3 shows vegetation communities 
present in the Planning Area.
The City’s approach to biological conservation is 
also informed by the statewide 30x30 initiative, part 
of an international movement to leverage nature-
based solutions to combat climate change, protect

Mitigation Standards for Impacts to 
Vegetation Communities
Where impacts to vegetation communities are 
unavoidable, mitigation through the preservation, 
creation or restoration of equivalent vegetation 
communities (i.e., those within the same habitat 
group shown in Table 5-2, below) in another area 
can provide a way to offset the negative effects. 
The standards for mitigating impacts to habitat 
differ according to where the impacted habitat is 
located. Within the WCPZ, primary emphasis is 
placed on avoiding impacts and minimizing any 
unavoidable impacts, with onsite mitigation credit 
applied to avoided habitats. This zone is also 
intended to be a “receiving area” for offsite mitiga-
tion generated elsewhere in the City. Outside of the 
WCPZ, there is less emphasis on impact avoidance, 
as long as offsite mitigation is directed to the WCPZ 
or areas designated as Softline Preserves. Table 5-2 
outlines mitigation ratios if protected vegetation 
communities are impacted as part of any devel-
opment. For example, if one acre of coastal sage 
scrub is impacted by a development, the developer 
must permanently protect (either through creation 
or substantially restoration)  three acres of coastal 
sage scrub either on site or offsite in a hardline or 
softline preserve, or another area within the WCPZ.

The VSR recognizes the importance of protecting 
special status species within the City to ensure 
continued regional-scale biodiversity. Special 
status species are those plants and animals that, 
because of their acknowledged rarity or vulner-

by federal, state, or other agencies as deserving 
special consideration.

Wildlife Crossings and Habitat 
Linkages
A major barrier to north-south regional wildlife 
movement in the WCPZ is SR 76, which has few 
opportunities for wildlife crossings. As shown in 
Figure 5-2, the WCPZ contracts to a major pinch 
point where SR 76 crosses over Mission Avenue. 
While wildlife crossings at grade across Mission 
Avenue are technically feasible, the presence of 
near-constant traffic on Mission Avenue would 
make any animal crossings here extremely risky, 
if not deadly. Prioritizing an underpass in this 
location is under consideration to provide a true 
connection between the wild lands of Camp 
Pendleton to the north and the remainder of 
Oceanside, including the San Luis Rey River corri-
dor to the south. Additional crossing options will 
be identified as part of a wildlife crossings study, 
proposed as an action item, below, and will be prior-
itized across Oceanside’s other major arterials. Any 
funds made available by outside sources or as mit-
igation for biological impacts elsewhere in the City 
(i.e., through development fees) will be prioritized to 
acquire, maintain, and enhance resource values and 
to facilitate wildlife crossings across major roadway 
barriers. 
Habitat and biological conservation efforts in 
Oceanside will continue to be incremental, oppor-
tunistic, and collaborative, taking advantage of 
grant funding, regional habitat mitigation programs, 
the development review process, hazard mitigation 
and climate adaptation efforts, public-private part-
nerships, and other environmental initiatives (e.g., 
water quality and flood control projects such as 
those along the San Luis Rey River watershed).
California Coastal Act
Enacted in 1976, the Coastal Act aims to protect and 
enhance California’s coastal resources, maximize 
public access to the shoreline, and balance coastal 
development with environmental protection. The 
Act requires local cities and counties within the 
designated “coastal zone” to prepare and implement 
a Local Coastal Program (LCP), which consists of a 
Land Use Plan and an Implementation Plan (zoning 
ordinances and maps). These LCPs must be certified 
by the California Coastal Commission as consistent 
with the policies and goals of the Coastal Act, such 
as protecting sensitive habitats, ensuring public 
access to beaches, preserving agricultural lands, 
and maintaining coastal water quality. Once an LCP 
is certified, the local government assumes primary 
responsibility for issuing Coastal Development 
Permits (CDPs) for development within its coastal 
zone, though the Coastal Commission retains a crit-
ical oversight role through its appeal jurisdiction.

5.5	Water Quality
Access to safe, high-quality water is vital for a healthy 
community. The availability, quantity, and quality of 
water are vital to plant, animal, and human habitats 
alike. Water is essential to the development of hous-
ing, commerce, industry, and agriculture; provides rec-
reational opportunities; and supports healthy fish and 
wildlife habitats. As shown in Figure 5-6, Oceanside 
features a variety of water resources that support ter-
restrial aquatic life, provide passive and recreational 
open space, and in some cases contribute to the City’s 
potable water supply. These resources include:
•	 San Luis Rey River 
•	 San Luis Rey Valley Groundwater Basins 
•	 Loma Alta Creek 
•	 Buena Vista Creek/Lagoon
•	 Calavera Creek
•	 Guajome Lake
•	 Talone Lake
•	 Foss Lake
•	 Whelan Lake
•	 Pacific Ocean
SURFACE WATER  
The California State Water Resources Control Board 
(SWRCB) sets water quality standards for surface 
water bodies in the Planning Area pursuant to the 
State’s Porter-Cologne Water Quality Control Act and 
the federal Clean Water Act. The Porter-Cologne Act 
also requires the SWRCB or a Regional Water Quality 
Control Board (RWQCB) to adopt basin plans for the 
protection of water quality. Oceanside is under the 
jurisdiction of the San Diego RWQCB, which estab-
lished regulatory standards and objectives for water 
quality in its Water Quality Control Plan for the San 
Diego Basin, commonly referred to as the Basin Plan. 
Water Quality objectives developed by the San Diego 
RWQCB are numerical or narrative limits on pollut-
ants designed to protect the beneficial uses of a given 
water body. Water bodies that are used for habitat or 
drinking water, for example, are held to more stringent 
pollution standards.  
Several of the surface waterbodies in Oceanside are 
categorized as impaired under the standards and pro-
tocols of the Federal Clean Water Act. As such, they 
are listed on the SWRCB’s 303(d) List of Impaired 
Waters, which lists waterbodies that are not meeting, 
or not expected to meet, water quality standards2. Wet 
weather runoff and illicit discharges to the City’s storm 
drain system can contribute pollutants to these water 
bodies, such as sediment, nutrients, bacteria, metals 
and trash. Reducing contaminants in impaired water 
bodies—including from sources such as road runoff, 
pesticide application, agricultural operations, con-
struction runoff, pet waste is critical to maintaining a 
healthy and diverse natural environment in Oceanside. 
Each  of the three watershed management areas in 
which Oceanside lies has impaired waters. 
•	 San Luis Rey Watershed Management Area: The 
Lower San Luis Rey River is listed as impaired for 
chloride, indicator bacteria, phosphorus, total 
dissolved solids (TDS), total nitrogen, benthic 
community effects, and toxicity. Additionally, 
the Pacific Ocean shoreline at the San Luis Rey 
River outlet in Oceanside is impaired for indicator 
bacteria and is included as a regulated waterway 
under the regional Bacterial total maximum daily 
load. 
•	 Santa Margarita River Watershed Management 
Area: The Santa Margarita River Lagoon, which 
is considered eutrophic, and the lower Santa 
Margarita River, are listed as impaired for 
enterococcus, coliform bacteria, phosphorus, and 
nitrogen.
•	 Carlsbad Watershed Management Area: The Loma 
Alta hydrological area and the Buena Vista Creek 
hydrological area have impaired waters listed for 
heightened bacterial levels and concentrations of 
nutrients, sediment, toxicity, benthic community 
effects, pesticides and eutrophication.

To promote awareness of surface water quality pro-
tection and stormwater pollution prevention, the 
City’s Green Oceanside program conducts exten-
sive environmental education outreach to the City’s 
Commercial, Industrial and Residential audiences. 
The Green Oceanside program also coordinates a 
variety of community engagement activities (e.g., 
beach and creek clean-up events) to encourage 
the Oceanside community to be better stewards of 
their natural resources.  
The Watershed Protection Program (a component 
of Green Oceanside) monitors and enforces com-
pliance with state and regional water quality stan-
dards. More specifically, the Program is responsible 
for ensuring the City’s compliance with Regional 
Municipal Stormwater National Pollutant Discharge 
Elimination System (NPDES) Permits prescribed 
by the San Diego Regional Water Quality Control 
Board. The Watershed Protection Program actively 
investigates reports of landscape irrigation runoff, 
illegal dumping, and other violations. The program 
ensures that new development meets stormwater 
treatment and flow control standards promulgated 
by the San Diego Regional Water Quality Control 
Board and that construction activities adhere to 
Best Management Practices (BMPs) for reducing 
pollutants in stormwater runoff. Additionally, this 
program undertakes habitat enhancement and 
conservation projects that protect water qual-
ity, reconnect floodplains and protect sensitive 
wildlife within Oceanside’s watersheds. The City also 
coordinates its watershed management efforts with 
neighboring jurisdictions within the San Luis Rey and 
Carlsbad Watershed Management Areas to maximize 
water quality protection benefits through Water 
Quality Improvement Plans (WQIPs). The WQIPs are 
multi-year comprehensive planning documents that 
identify critical water quality conditions in each water-
shed, including water quality improvement goals that 
responsible agencies are tasked with meetings. The 
WQIPs include watershed management strategies and 
implementation schedules to be undertaken by each 
agency in the watershed to achieve long-term improve-
ments in surface water quality. For example, in the San 
Luis Rey Watershed Management Area, the City of 
Oceanside is a joint responsible agency alongside the 
County of San Diego, City of Vista and Caltrans.

the San Luis Rey Valley Groundwater Basin (Basin No.

groundwater uses includes municipal and domestic 
supply, agricultural supply, industrial service supply, 
water recreation, and the support of freshwater and 
wildlife habitat (including habitat for rare, threatened, 
or endangered species). The City of Oceanside utilizes 
groundwater from the Mission Basin of the San Luis 
Rey Valley Groundwater Basin. Although the San Luis 
Rey Valley Groundwater Basin has been designated a 
medium priority basin under the California Statewide 
Groundwater Elevation Monitoring (CASGEM) pro-
gram, meaning that the State considers it a priority 
basin for monitoring, the Mission Basin is classified as 
a “Very Low” priority basin, per DWR’s Sustainable 
Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) Basin 
Prioritization Dashboard. As such, the Mission Basin is 
not required to have a Groundwater Sustainability Plan 
(GSP).  
Groundwater quality in the San Luis Rey Watershed is 
generally of low concern. However, general concerns 
over the region’s groundwater aquifers include saltwa-

(TDS), nitrate, iron and manganese, and toxic organic

Clean Water Act 
The 1972 federal Clean Water Act (CWA) is the 
primary federal law regulating water quality in the 
United States. Its objective is to reduce or eliminate 
water pollution in the nation’s rivers, streams, lakes, 
and coastal waters. At the federal level, the CWA is 
administered by the EPA. In California, the CWA is 
administered and enforced by the SWRCB and nine 
regional water quality control boards (RWQCBs).

National Pollutant Discharge 
Elimination System Program 
The Clean Water Act, adopted in 1972 as an amend-
ment to The federal Water Pollution Control Act, 
established the NPDES permit program to control dis-

The NPDES permit program is the primary federal pro-

discharges to waters of the United States. As an EPA-
authorized State agency, the SWRCB and nine Regional 
Water Quality Control Boards issue both general and

Porter-Cologne Water Quality Control 
Act 
The Porter-Cologne Water Quality Control Act, 
also known as the California Water Code, is 
California’s statutory authority for the protection 
of water quality. The act sets forth the obliga-
tions of the SWRCB and RWQCBs pertaining to 
the adoption of Basin Plans and establishment of 
water quality objectives. Permits adopted by the 
San Diego Regional Water Quality Control Board 
(RWQCB) require local jurisdictions, certain busi-
ness sectors and entities to comply with these 
objectives by controlling the amount of pollutants 
released to surface waters and the environment 
through their routine operations. These activities 
include municipal, industrial and construction-re-
lated stormwater pollution prevention and munici-
pal wastewater treatment.

Criteria Air Pollutants
Concentrations of ozone, carbon monoxide (CO), 
nitrogen dioxide (NO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), lead 
(Pb), and particulate matter (PM¬10 and PM2.5) 
are commonly used as indicators of ambient air

“criteria pollutants” and are regulated by the U.S. 
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and CARB 
through national and California ambient air quality 
standards (NAAQS and CAAQS), respectively. The 
primary criteria pollutants of concern in the San 
Diego Region are ozone and PM. 
Toxic Air Contaminants 
Toxic air contaminants (TACs) are a broad class of 
compounds known to cause morbidity or mortality. 
Although National Ambient Air Quality Standards 
(NAAQS) and California Ambient Air Quality 
Standards (CAAQS) have been established for criteria 
pollutants, no ambient standards exist for TACs. One 
such TAC of concern associated with the Planning 
Area is Diesel Particulate Matter (DPM), which is 
emitted from burning of diesel fuel in diesel engines.

Attainment Status 
While overall air quality in the San Diego region has 
improved over the past 25 years, the region is still 
burdened with some of the nation’s highest ozone 
(O3) and particulate matter concentrations. The 
attainment statuses for San Diego County, the area 
applicable to Oceanside, are shown in Table 5-5. 
According to the U.S. Environmental Protection 
Agency, the San Diego region ranks sixth nationally 
for high ozone days and 13th for 24-hour particle 
pollution. Ozone and particulate matter are the pri-
mary components of smog. The San Diego region is 
among the smoggiest in the nation. While there are 
many factors that contribute to the region’s smog 
levels (including climate change), nearly half of the 
pollutants that generate smog in San Diego County 
come from tailpipe emissions from motor vehicles. 
Other sources include industrial operations, power 
plants, construction sites, wildfires, wood burning, 
gravel pits, agricultural activities, and dusty roads. 
When inhaled, ozone reacts chemically with bio-
logical molecules in the respiratory tract, which can 
compromise lung function and exacerbate asthma 
and other chronic respiratory conditions. Exposure 
to particulate matter less than 10 micrometers 
in diameter can be harmful to both the heart and 
the lungs, particularly among those with cardiac or 
respiratory ailments. Moreover, particulate matter 
contributes to reduced visibility, increased acidity 
in freshwater bodies, nutrient imbalance in coastal 
waters, and impaired photosynthesis and respira-
tion in plants.

partners (e.g., San Diego Air Pollution Control 
District) to promulgate air quality goals and devise 
regulations and programs that achieve these goals, 
the City can support state and regional air quality 
efforts through addressing point and non-point 
sources of pollution in the City, shown in Figure 5-7. 
Reducing reliance on single occupancy gas-powered 
vehicles and increasing access to emissions-free 
vehicles and reliable public transit will reduce emis-

with the farming community in South Morro Hills 
can help minimize emissions associated with agricul-
tural production.

Sensitive Receptors
The NAAQS and CAAQS apply at publicly-ac-

are populated. Sensitive land uses are generally 
defined as locations where human populations, 
especially children, seniors, and sick persons, are 
located, and where there is reasonable expecta-

the averaging period for air quality standards (e.g., 
24-hour, 8-hour, and 1-hour). Typical sensitive 
receptors include residences, hospitals, and schools. 
The Planning Area consists of a wide array of land 
uses, including commercial uses, business offices, 
industrial uses, residential uses, hotels, schools, and 
parks. Many of the uses in the Planning Area are 
considered sensitive receptors.

downtown area display elevated rates of asthma 
compared to the rest of the state and can be con-

5.7	Agricultural 
Resources
Agricultural resources in Oceanside are concen-
trated in South Morro Hills, located in the northeast 
corner of the City. South Morro Hills encompasses 
a 5.4-square mile (3,500 acre) area of agricultural 
land that supports both commercial and avocational 
farming operations as well as estate residential use. 
Figure 5-8 shows areas designated for agricultural 
use under this General Plan. Over three-quar-
ters of the land in South Morro Hills is classified 
as one of the following types of farmland: Prime 
Farmland, Farmland of Statewide Importance, or 
Unique Farmland, as designated by the California 
Department of Conservation (see Figure 5-9). A 
smaller swath of farmland is located immediately 
south of South Morro Hills, along the southern 
banks of the San Luis Rey River. Prevalent crops 
within South Morro Hills include avocados, toma-
toes, berries, cannabis, and a wide variety of orna-
mentals. Wine grapes and coffee have recently 
been introduced to the area. In recent years, farm-
ers have been experimenting with new, higher-value 
crops, including coffee, cherimoya, and other exotic 
fruits and vegetables.  
The South Morro Hills area is under a large number 
of different ownerships, with parcel sizes ranging 
from less than three acres to several larger than 160 
acres. While the area’s existing Agricultural land use 
and zoning designations are ostensibly intended to 
support farming, applicable zoning standards allow 
for 2.5-acre residential subdivision. As of 2023, 
there were approximately 150 single-family homes 
in South Morro Hills. Through the City’s planned 
unit development provisions (which do not require 
that agricultural uses be maintained), higher den-
sities can be achieved, as evidenced by the North 
River Farms project approved in 2020. This project 
is projected to add nearly 400 new housing units 
to South Morro Hills. North River Farms will be the 
first development in South Morro Hills to be served 
by sanitary sewer. All other housing in South Morro 
Hills is served by septic systems, as no sewer ser-
vice currently extends north of North River Road. 
FINANCIAL PRESSURES ON 
AGRICULTURAL PRODUCTION
An evaluation conducted in early 2022 com-
pared supportable land values for single-family 
residential development in the South Morro 
Hills (SMH) community to comparable land 
sales of agricultural land in San Diego County. 
The survey of comparable land sales for agri-
cultural land demonstrated a median value of 
approximately $57,000 per gross acre, a figure 
that has been declining over the past five years. 
By comparison, a financial feasibility analysis of 
large-lot single-family residential development 
in the SMH community under existing zoning 
regulations concluded a supportable land value 
of $134,000 per gross acre. In effect, this com-
parison of agricultural versus residential land 
values demonstrates that SMH property owners 
are incentivized to sell or develop their property 
for residential use. Additionally, demand for 
housing has continued to grow throughout the 
County, as development of new housing supply 
has not kept pace with demand. Under current 
zoning, properties in SMH have more than two 
times as much value for residential use rather 
than agricultural use, providing great incentive 
to property owners to convert their land from 
agricultural to residential use.

DEVELOPMENT PRESSURES ON 
FARMLAND
The City’s agricultural lands face significant develop-
ment pressure, as evidenced by the North River Farms 
housing development, approved by the City in 2020. 
The City’s agricultural zoning district provides for 2.5-
acre residential subdivision as well as “planned devel-
opment plans” that allow property owners and devel-
opers to propose custom land use and development 
standards. These mechanisms could result in wide-
spread conversion of farmland. While further study 
is needed to identify a solution to the development 
pressures on farmland, agritourism in South Morro 
Hills represents one alternative revenue source for 
local farmers that can help maintain existing farmland.
Along North River Road, Rainbow Municipal Water 
District conveys wastewater from Rainbow Municipal 
Water District to the San Luis Rey Water Reclamation 
Facility (SLRWRF). At present, no other sewer infra-
structure exists within San Morro Hills. The area is 
served by the City’s potable water system, has very 
limited stormwater conveyance infrastructure, and 
is not yet served by the Oceanside Recycled Water 
program. Any extension would need to be paid for by 
property owners. More intensive uses in the area, such 
as agritourism facilities, may require wastewater exten-
sion, as is the case with North River Farms.
AGRICULTURAL CONSERVATION
The only way to fully guarantee that active farming 
continues in South Morro Hills is for farmlands to be 
acquired by public agencies or land trusts, or  through 
the establishment of conservation easements. Any 
City acquisition will require substantial financial out-
lays, likely necessitating special Citywide tax measures, 
which is likely infeasible. Short of this, public policy can 
be directed to ensuring that farming remains economi-
cally viable and productive, providing other supportive 
measures, and seeking grants and other available fund-
ing for conservation. 
The General Plan presents several strategies for 
strengthening Oceanside’s active agricultural areas. 
Supportive Uses 
One avenue that the City has explored is increasing 
allowances for agritourism uses on agricultural land to 
provide an additional option for revenue. In 2017, the 
City amended the Zoning Code to allow lower-intensity 
(“Tier 1”) agritourism uses such as U-Pick operations, 
petting zoos, and farm stands. One obstacle to the 
development of agritourism and other supportive uses 
that would increase the financial viability of produc-
tion farming is the lack of access to City infrastructure 
systems, financing of which would require more devel-
opment than that would result from large-lot zoning or 
Tier 1 uses. While the City is not currently proposing 
expansion of infrastructure to the South Morro Hills 
neighborhood, doing so would open up the possibility 
for the allowance of additional (“Tier 2”) agritourism 
uses such as wedding venues, event spaces, and small 
inns. 
Grants
Other agriculture preservation mechanisms exist and 
should be further explored for applicability in South 
Morro Hills. One such program, the Sustainable 
Agricultural Lands Conservation Program (SALC), 
utilizes statewide Cap-and-Trade proceeds to pro-
tect agricultural lands that are at risk of conversion 
to other uses. Protecting farmland avoids increased 
emissions associated with development and provides 
an opportunity to capture carbon in the land base. 
SALC’s mission is to support California’s need for agri-
cultural conservation, economic growth, and sustain-
able development. SALC funds projects that protect 
at-risk agricultural lands from conversion to more pol-
lution-heavy land uses such as urban or rural residen-
tial development. The program provides two types of 
awards, Planning grants and Agricultural Conservation 
Acquisition grants. 
•	 Planning grants provide funds to develop plans 
for the protection of agricultural land at risk of 
conversion to non-agricultural uses. These plans 
support agricultural land conservation by protecting 
the land base and developing regional food systems. 
Plans may also recognize and protect environmental 
co-benefits of conserving agricultural lands. 


5-49

•	 Agricultural Conservation Acquisition grants are 
used to permanently protect agricultural lands 
that support infill and compact development. 
Agricultural Conservation Acquisition grants will 
be provided to purchase conservation easements 
or fee title to agricultural land. Acquisition grants 
are deed restrictions that landowners voluntarily 
place on their property to preserve the land’s 
agricultural uses. 
LOOKING AHEAD
Looking ahead, it is crucial to implement strategies 
that preserve and provide economic incentives for 
farming, preserve biological habitats, and estab-
lish an integrated network of open spaces. While 
expanding supportive uses such as agritourism can 
help maintain the economic feasibility of farming 
in Oceanside, it is important to address the lack 
of access to City infrastructure systems in South 
Morro Hills. More intensive agritourism uses could 
be leveraged in the future to finance the expansion 
of the appropriate infrastructure systems.
In addition to maintaining active agricultural uses 
in South Morro Hills, preserving biological habi-
tats is essential for maintaining the biodiversity 
and beauty of the region. Wetlands, riparian areas, 
grasslands, and areas of coastal sage scrub must 
be conserved, and wildlife movement networks 
along riparian corridors should be established or 
strengthened.
The establishment of an integrated network of open 
spaces is crucial to wildlife movement and biodiver-
sity, and to the overall sustainability and livability of 
the area. Creating a well-planned network of trails 
and open spaces will contribute to the protection 
of natural resources, enhanced wildlife habitat con-
nectivity, and an improved quality of life for resi-
dents and visitors.
While South Morro Hills is valued by many commu-
nity members for its bucolic ambience, the sense of 
openness it lends to inland portions of Oceanside, 
and the potential it holds for local food sourcing, 
agritourism, and agricultural research, the decision 
to continue farming operations in this corner of 
Oceanside rests with property owners. It is there-
fore essential that the City work with these prop-
erty owners to sustain and enhance the viability of 
farming as an economic enterprise.  
SUSTAINABLE AGRICULTURAL 
PRACTICES
South Morro Hills is served by the City’s pota-
ble water system, has very limited stormwater 
conveyance infrastructure, and is not yet served 
by the Oceanside Recycled Water program. The 
expansion of the City’s recycled water network 
will help supplement water demands in SMH, as 
well as reduce reliance on groundwater for agri-
cultural use. 
South Morro Hills is home to several rich veg-
etation communities, particularly wetland and 
riparian areas along the San Luis Rey River and 
its tributaries. Sensitive habitats and species 
continue to be supported along wetlands and 
riparian corridors, as well as in limited areas of 
grasslands and sage scrub. Local endangered 
species, including Least Bell’s vireo, the south-
western willow flycatcher, the tricolored black-
bird, Stephan’s kangaroo rat, and the arroyo toad, 
may be present in the agricultural areas of South 
Morro Hills. It is imperative that these habitats 
be conserved, and wildlife movement networks 
along riparian corridors maintained and/or re-es-
tablished as opportunities arise.   
Agricultural cultivation has the potential to cause 
harmful health effects to surrounding communi-
ties through runoff and airborne dust and con-
taminants. For example, the areas near South 
Morro Hills have top 25th percentile statewide 
rankings for pesticide exposure. Working with 
farmers to transition to regenerative farming 
practices and phasing out pesticides and harmful 
agricultural byproducts will both improve the 
quality and sustainability of agricultural prod-
ucts, and reduce negative health and ecosystem 
impacts to the surrounding area. Please see the 
Healthy and Livable Communities Element for 
more information about health impacts near 
agricultural lands.

5.9	Coastal Resources
The City has three miles of coastline on the Pacific 
Ocean, intersected by three waterways that provide 
estuarine habitat. Offshore fisheries are supported 
by several federal and state-designated marine 
protected areas (MPAs) between Point Conception 
and the Mexican border. These fisheries support 
commercial and recreational fishing enterprises that 
operate out of the Oceanside Small Craft Harbor. 
The City’s highly developed coastline features the 
Oceanside Municipal Pier, the Oceanside Small Craft 
Harbor, the Junior Seau Amphitheater, two public 
parks, and The Strand – the only public roadway 
on the beachfront in San Diego County. Beachfront 
residential uses line the inland side of The Strand 
and extend to the City’s southern boundary. 
OCEANSIDE SMALL CRAFT HARBOR
The Oceanside Small Craft Harbor offers whale 
watching and sportfishing charters, kayak and small 
boat rentals, and a variety of retail and dining estab-
lishments. The harbor includes over 850 boat slips, 
85 of which provide for live-aboards.   
The Harbor is one of Oceanside’s many coastal fea-
tures that is vulnerable to coastal hazards, projected 
to become more severe under continued climate 
change and sea level rise. In the short term, 79 of 
the 100 acres of the Harbor fall within the poten-
tial future hazard zones, increasing to 86 acres in 
the mid-term and 93 acres in the long term (2100 
to 2140). Potential impacts of the expansion of 
coastal hazard zones include increased frequency 
of flooding and erosion, with associated water and 
flood damage; disrupted access to and from build-
ings and associated recreational and commercial 
services; and disrupted access and damage to boats 
and docks.
For more information on the Harbor and policies 
relating to public access, see the Healthy and 
Livable Community Element.

beaches under the control of the City. These 
beaches are beloved by Oceanside residents and 
visitors alike; according to the 2018 Sports, Fitness, 
and Leisure Activities Topline Participation Report, 
visiting the beach is the number one outdoor recre-
ation activity in Oceanside. Approximately 54 per-
cent of Oceanside residents visit the beach at least 
once a week. 
Since the construction of the Camp Pendleton Boat 
Basin in 1942, Oceanside has experienced extensive 
beach erosion, as sand from the Santa Margarita 
River and other upcoast sources that would other-
wise replenish the City’s beaches is largely trapped 
behind the jetty and breakwater that protect the 
Boat Basin and the Oceanside Small Craft Harbor. 
As of 2023, less than a third of the City’s coastline 
provided dry sand areas, and nuisance flooding 
was common during storm and high tide events.

a highly developed coastline (which prevents 
the natural processes of bluff erosion and beach 
retreat), the damming and channelizing of the San

Rey River. A persistent sandbar created by sed-
iment transfer from the river provides a good 
surf break. However, this beach is much narrower 
than Harbor Beach. Much of the inland edge of 
this beach is lined by The Strand, the only public 
beachfront roadway in San Diego County. Public 
amenities at Breakwater Way include showers, 
restrooms, picnic tables, barbecue grills, and a 
volleyball court. There are two public parking lots 
in the vicinity.
Tyson Street Beach and Park
Adjacent to the Oceanside Municipal Pier and the 
Junior Seau Amphitheater, Tyson Street Beach and 
Park is essentially an extension of the City’s revi-
talized downtown. Several resort-style hotels are 
located within walking distance of this beach. The 
pier produces consistent, high-quality surf breaks, 
making this area a popular spot for both profes-
sional and scholastic surfing competitions. In the 
summer, visitors to Tyson Beach can enjoy a wide 
range of food and retail concessions managed by 
MainStreet Oceanside. Other public amenities in 
this area include public restrooms and showers, 
covered picnic areas, play structures, and a police 
substation. This stretch of coastline is served by 
several public parking lots, including two that can 
be accessed from The Strand.

Wisconsin Street Beach is located near the termi-
nus of The Strand at Wisconsin Street, which is an 
important coastal access roadway. As of 2023, this

Buccaneer Beach
A small, family-oriented space popular with local 
residents, Buccaneer Beach serves as a transition 
point between higher density, tourist-oriented devel-
opment to the north and single-family development 
to the south.  This beach lies next to the mouth of 
Loma Alta Creek, the mouth of which is managed 
(pending permits in 2025) to lessen the likelihood 
of upstream flooding.  Additionally, the City’s Water 
Utilities Department operates an ultra-violet light 
water treatment system within the Loma Alta Slough 
to control water levels in the Slough by treating 
water and safely releasing it to the ocean.  If com-
pleted, the Loma Alta Slough Wetlands Enhancement 
Project will provide both carrying capacity and water 
quality improvements to the creek and adjacent 
beach. A walkway along the southern edge of Loma 
Alta Creek connects this beach to the Coast Highway 
corridor.  As of 2023, Buccaneer Beach had been 
reduced to a steep cobble bank by wave uprush and 
high tides.
BEACHES BETWEEN BUCCANEER AND 
THE CITY’S SOUTHERN BOUNDARY
As of 2023, this stretch of beachfront was largely 
devoid of dry sand areas, with the ocean extending 
into the coastal revetment on a daily basis. Public 
beach access in this area is somewhat limited, with 
only three accessways along its one-mile length. 
The most accommodating of these accessways 
lies at the terminus of Cassidy Street. This access-
way includes a viewing area and bicycle parking. 
The southernmost portion of this area is occupied 
by St. Malo, a gated single-family community that 
extends to the mouth of the Buena Vista Lagoon. 
Surrounded by water on three sides, this commu-
nity is among the most vulnerable to sea level rise 
and associated hazards.
Oceanside Municipal Pier
At 1,564 feet in length, the Oceanside Municipal 
Pier served as one of the longest piers on the west 
coast of the United States for many years.  Popular 
with residents and visitors alike, the pier anchors 
a stretch of beach that offers a variety of coastal 
amenities.  On April 25, 2025, the far west end of 
the pier caught fire, destroying the former Ruby’s 
Diner.  Once the west end is rebuilt, the pier has 
the ability to accommodate a restaurant use, fishing 
activities, a bait shop, lifeguard tower, and a series 
of viewing areas furnished with benches.  The City’s 
lifeguard operations center is nestled under the 
pier immediately seaward of The Strand.  The pier 
is connected to North Pacific Street by a concrete 
abutment comprised of twin conventionally rein-
forced bridges and a ramp down to the beach. The 
abutment is slated for repair.
THE STRAND
The Strand is the only public roadway in San Diego 
County that extends along the beachfront. The 
Strand serves pedestrians, bicyclists, and motorists.

CLIMATE CHANGE VULNERABILITY
Oceanside’s coastline and marine life is vulnerable to 
the pressures of climate change. Sea level rise exacer-
bates existing flood risks and provides the potential 
for more intense storms and damage along the coast. 
In the short term, 79 of the 100 acres of the Harbor 
fall within potential future hazard zones, increasing to 
86 acres in the mid-term and 93 acres in the long term 
(2100 to 2140). Potential impacts of the expansion of 
coastal hazard zones include increased frequency of 
flooding and erosion, with associated water and flood 
damage; disrupted access to and from buildings and 
associated recreational and commercial services; and 
disrupted access and damage to boats and docks.
Warming oceans that are more acidic and less oxy-
genated affect the reproduction and survival of 
marine species. Reducing greenhouse gas emissions 
in Oceanside is key to the protection of marine life 
and coastal resources and is addressed in detail in 
the City’s Climate Action Plan. In addition to the 
risks associated with human induced climate change, 
human activities can harm marine wildlife through 
boat collisions, excessive noise, harmful fishing prac-
tices, and pollution. One way to protect marine wild-
life from human-induced harms is to designate areas 
as national marine sanctuaries under the National 
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), a 
designation that provides protections including speed 
limits, noise controls, and coordinated monitoring and 
management of marine resources. 
Oceanside’s coastline and marine resources are 
addressed in detail in the City’s Local Coastal Land 
Use Plan and the Local Coastal Program, state-man-
dated planning and implementation documents meant 
to demonstrate local consistency with the California 
Coastal Act (CCA). The CCA promotes public access 
to the coastline, visitor-serving uses, mitigation of 
coastal hazards, and preservation of scenic views, 
natural landforms, and biological resources. Updates 
to the City’s Local Coastal Land Use Plan and LCP are 
required to be completed by 2034 and will address 
issues of inundation of vulnerable areas and planning 
for future sea level rise.

GOALS
5-R	
The City will work with regulatory and 
resource agencies to achieve a balance 
between natural resources, economic ben-
efit, and recreational opportunities along 
the coastline, proactively planning for sea 
level rise, erosion, and other climate-related 
coastline changes under the guidance and 
requirements of the Coastal Commission 
and Ocean Protection Council. 
5-S	
The City will improve harbor infrastructure 
to support both recreational access, local 
commercial fishing operations, and low-
er-cost visitor accommodations.
5-T	
The City will prioritize beach restoration 
and preservation in the near term while 
acknowledging that other coastal adaptation 
strategies will be necessary to effectively 
address sea level rise and other climate-re-
lated impacts.
