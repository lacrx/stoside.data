COMMUNITY
Oceanside will provide and maintain ample, accessible, and 
equitably programmed community facilities that support physical 
activity, personal enrichment, social interaction, and civic engagement. 
These facilities and associated services will be available to all segments of 
the community, helping residents thrive and pursue their healthiest and happiest 
lives. Through a transparent and inclusive decision making process that utilizes 
state of the art methods and technologies, the City will ensure that all community 
members have a voice in guiding the City toward a prosperous and sustainable 
future. Public resources will be fairly distributed, and traditionally underserved 
populations will not face undue environmental, economic, or social burdens.

7.1	 Background
Community health, quality of life, and access to 
opportunity are all shaped by the natural, built, 
and social environments that define the neighbor-
hoods in which we live. Oceanside is known for 
its pleasant climate, three miles of beaches, trails, 
and community parks and facilities. These features 
all contribute to an environment that promotes 
public health and improves the quality of life for 
Oceansiders. Public facilities such as libraries, 
schools, water infrastructure, energy and waste 
systems, and telecommunication networks are less 
visible, but equally important to ensuring a healthy 
and livable community.
The Healthy and Livable Community element (HLC) 
also acknowledges the significance of incorporating 
sustainability practices and resilience measures into 
the City’s public facilities and infrastructure, rec-
ognizing the need for environmentally-conscious 
design, energy efficiency, climate adaptation strat-
egies, and the utilization of innovative technologies 
to ensure the long-term viability and functionality 
of Oceanside's infrastructure and other systems.
Historically, Oceanside has been one of the more 
affordable cities in San Diego County and has pro-
vided integrated neighborhoods and equal oppor-
tunities for its diverse mix of residents and business 
owners. As Oceanside continues to grow and 
develop, it is important to maintain this affordabil-
ity and to ensure that access to the City’s ameni-
ties and facilities remains equitable. With much of 
the City’s anticipated residential growth occurring 
along the three major east-west corridors—Mission 
Avenue, Oceanside Boulevard, and Vista Way—new 
community facilities and infrastructure along these 
corridors may be required to accommodate that 
growth.
By prioritizing community health and quality of life in 
policy-making and development, Oceanside can create 
environments that promote healthy behaviors and 
social and economic well-being, ultimately leading to 
better health outcomes and quality of life for all.
PURPOSE AND INTENT
As Oceanside continues to develop and the popu-
lation changes and evolves, it is vitally important 
that the City’s amenities and infrastructure grow 
and adapt to meet the population’s changing needs. 
The HLC outlines the steps that the City will take 
to ensure equitable access to services and to facil-
itate the development of safe and well-maintained 
public facilities that support the physical, mental, 
and social well-being of Oceanside's residents. The 
goals and policies presented in this element consider 
the changing demographics, anticipated growth, and 
evolving healthcare needs of the community.

Community Priorities
Throughout the outreach process, Oceansiders 
have expressed desires for community facilities 
and services that are easily accessible by walking 
or biking. Increased access to community amenities 
such as trails, dog parks, farmers markets, splash 
pools, community gardens, and art are priorities for 
the Oceanside community. Many residents appreci-
ate the existing facilities and want to see additional 
services and expanded access and hours at schools, 
libraries and community centers. Community 
members also want to see excessive noise from 
highways, loud vehicles, and Heating, Ventilation 
and Air Conditioning (HVAC) systems addressed. 
Oceanside residents also have a desire to leverage 
the existing City infrastructure and systems to 
enhance the natural environment and improve the 
City’s sustainability and resilience in the face of a 
changing climate. 
Parks and Recreation Master Plan
In 2019, the City adopted a new Parks and 
Recreation Master Plan (Parks Master Plan). The 
Parks Master Plan provides guidelines for the 
orderly development of future park, recreation, and 
open space facilities and programs in order to meet 
the community’s current and future needs. The 
Parks Master Plan is intended to provide a realistic 
view of the City’s parks and recreation system and 
to develop a vision for the parks and recreation 
system through 2030. The General Plan builds on 
the work done in the Parks Master Plan to present 
a comprehensive view of City’s parks and recre-
ation resources through the 2050 planning horizon.
TOPIC AREAS
The HLC addresses a variety of factors related to 
community health and quality of life, including the 
state mandated topic of noise and its effects. The 
HLC outlines actions the City will take to promote 
public health, engage community members in deci-
sion-making, ensure high quality public facilities 
and infrastructure throughout the city, implement 
smart city technologies, and enrich the quality of 
life for all residents. It focuses on addressing ineq-
uities and empowering all residents to reach their 
full potential.
Several other factors related to community health 
and livability are addressed in other Oceanside 
General Plan elements. For example, the Housing 
Element includes strategies to increase opportu-
nities for affordable housing and homeownership, 
rehabilitation assistance to maintain high-quality 
housing conditions, and programs to address needs 
of people experiencing homelessness. The Efficient 
and Compatible Land Use Element supports creat-
ing vibrant, walkable, visually appealing, and engag-
ing mixed-use districts so that people can easily 
access what they need. The Integrated Mobility 
Element aims to foster safe environments that 
make active mobility choices, including biking and 
walking, more convenient and comfortable. Finally, 
the Vital and Sustainable Resources Element con-
tains policies that protect and preserve Oceanside’s 
natural resources and environment, including the 
air, land, and water.

7.2	Community Health
The community environment we inhabit has a pro-
found influence on our health outcomes, shaping 
both our daily lives, and our longer-term well-being 
and longevity. A healthy community is one that pro-
vides a nurturing environment with safe and afford-
able housing, clean air and water, access to quality 
healthcare, and opportunities for education and 
employment. A community that prioritizes physical 
activity, access to fresh and healthy food options 
and recreational areas, can also support healthy 
behaviors and habits. Not all of these factors are 
in control of City government – for example, City 
government does not directly provide healthcare 
or education. However, several other factors are 
within local control, which this element seeks to 
influence. 
Additionally, neighborhood conditions can vary 
greatly throughout the City and can lead to an 
inequitable geography of health risks and health 
outcomes. These conditions can include varying 
levels of exposure to pollutants, walkability, tree 
canopy coverage, access to healthy food options, 
and more, all of which contribute to health out-
comes. Oceanside residents experience higher 
rates of diabetes, food insecurity, and adult and 
child obesity, and depression than the county as 
a whole.1 Within the City, populations that live 
in certain census tracts, especially those close to 
downtown Oceanside, have rates of asthma and 
high blood pressure that are higher than 60 percent 
of census tracts in California. 
While health-promoting policies related to walk-
able neighborhoods, access to parks, environmental 
health, and others are included in other elements, 
this element includes strategies to increase access 
to medical services and healthy, nutritious, and cul-
turally familiar foods.

HEALTH SERVICES
Health insurance is a major determinant of access 
to necessary health services, including preventive 
care. According to the CDC, certain socioeconomic 
conditions such as lack of health insurance and 
poverty are associated with poor health status 
and chronic disease. Physical barriers to access 
may also prevent certain individuals from receiving 
proper medical care, particularly for those with less 
mobility. When health care facilities are accessi-
ble via public transit, medical care is more readily 
accessible to those who do not drive or own cars.
Figure 7-1 shows the percentage of the adult popu-
lation between ages 18 and 64 by census tract that 
does not have health insurance. While Oceanside’s 
population generally has good health insurance 
coverage, there are some census tracts that have 
rates of lack of insurance that score higher than 
the national average, including the Airport/San Luis 
Rey area. Figure 7-1 also shows the locations of 
hospitals, medical centers, and healthcare facilities 
in and around Oceanside. These facilities range 
from large hospitals and medical complexes oper-
ated at the Tri-City Medical Center and the Naval 
Hospital at Camp Pendleton, to mid-size clinics 
run by Scripps Health and Kaiser Permanente, 
and smaller health centers run by TrueCare, the 
County of San Diego, and Vista Community Clinic, 
which operates four Federally Qualified Health 
Centers (FQHC) in Oceanside at 4002 Vista Way, 
517 N Horne Street, 818 Pier View Way, and 4700 
North River Road. FQHCs are required to serve all 
patients regardless of their ability to pay and offer a

sliding fee scale based on income and family size to 
ensure that services are affordable for all patients. 
These facilities are well-distributed, and many 
are located within or near tracts that have higher 
rates of lack of insurance. The City of Oceanside 
will continue to coordinate with these important 
healthcare organizations on changing health needs 
and addressing barriers to care. Coupled with other 
responsive City efforts, such as supportive services 
for people experiencing homelessness, mental

continue to address and improve public health.

FOOD ACCESS
Having good food access means that food is afford-
able, nutritious, and available between a half mile 
and a mile from home2. Generally, Oceanside resi-
dents can access healthy, nutritious foods at super-
markets and smaller grocery stores, and to a lesser 
extent, at some convenience stores. Residents 
also benefit from proximity to South Morro Hills 
and can purchase fresh produce directly from 
local growers at several farmers markets and farm 
stands in the City.

The presence of a supermarket in a neighborhood 
is linked to higher fruit and vegetable consumption, 
as well as a reduced incidence of obesity. As shown 
in Figure 7-2, the City of Oceanside contains sev-
eral large chain supermarkets, with most clustered 
in commercial shopping centers. Large (over 6,000 
square feet) and smaller grocery stores are mainly 
located in downtown Oceanside, the major com-
mercial corridors of Vista Way and Mission Avenue, 
and to a lesser extent, Oceanside Boulevard. 
Within city limits, farmers markets are primarily 
located downtown, Fire Mountain, and near South 
Morro Hills. Outside of these areas, there is a lack 
of convenient healthy food options within walkable 
distance, especially for residential neighborhoods 
between the corridors. 
While the economic feasibility of a greater den-
sity of grocery stores is subject to market real-
ities, such as pressure from delivery services and 
other trends, policies that support smaller grocery 
stores, neighborhood markets, local food stands, 
and other neighborhood-serving options could 
increase walkability and healthy food accessibility 
for an increased number of Oceanside residents. 
Increasing the number of households in an area, 
particularly within the corridors, could also help to 
provide a larger market needed for grocery store 
viability. As the corridors are redeveloped, the 
net loss of existing grocery stores will be mini-
mized in part through commercial FAR minimums 
in Mixed Use areas, a strategy that is discussed in 
more detail in the ECLU. In addition to incentives 
for larger grocery stores and reducing a net loss of 
existing grocery stores, strategies in this element 
also aim to build capacity of smaller food retailers 
and convenience store owners to sell healthy, fresh 
food. A combination of improved food access and 
more walkable, complete neighborhoods can help 
to create conditions that support healthy living and 
reduced rates of obesity.

HOMELESSNESS
There are several factors that contribute to the 
prevalence of homelessness in Oceanside. Like 
other coastal cities, Oceanside has a mild climate 
that makes outdoor living more comfortable and 
less dangerous. The City is a transit hub that serves 
as the southern terminus of the Metrolink’s Orange 
County Line, the western terminus of NCTD’s 
Sprinter, and the northern terminus of NCTD’s 
Coaster; for many homeless individuals utilizing 
these transit services, Oceanside is effectively 
the end of the line. In addition, the San Luis Rey 
River and other riparian areas provide refuge and 
concealment. Finally, the City’s close proximity to 
and long association with Marine Corps Base Camp 
Pendleton may contribute to the City’s veteran 
homeless population. While there is a common 
perception that it is the provision of services and 
shelter that primarily attract unhoused individuals 
to the City, in reality a variety of factors contribute 
to the relatively higher numbers of unhoused indi-
viduals in Oceanside. 
In Oceanside and other communities, there has 
been a growing interest and movement toward 
food autonomy, a concept that emphasizes commu-
nity control over their own food systems, as well 
as the importance of small-scale, locally-based, 
sustainable food networks. Data from the 2020 
San Diego County Food Vision 2030 indicated 
that 83 percent of Oceanside respondents were 
interested in growing their own food, with strong 
emphasis on community ownership and commu-
nity growing spaces. Policies in this element set 
out a plan to take stock of potential vacant or 
City-owned land and make it easier for Oceanside 
residents to develop their own spaces for urban 
farming and community gardens. Recent state laws 
have also emphasized the importance of reducing 
food waste. This element builds upon the success 
of Oceanside’s Zero Waste Programs, including 
the El Corazon public-private recycling program, a 
landmark green waste compost facility; the Edible 
Food Recovery Program that educates businesses 
on food recovery requirements; and the Green 
Oceanside Kitchen, a state-of-the-art food recov-
ery and preservation facility designed to foster a 
sustainable food system and offer culinary learning 
opportunities dedicated to reducing food waste 
and feeding the community. 
Finally, community partnerships and healthy food 
education can play an important role in improving 
the food system. For example, healthy food edu-
cation can help community members develop the 
knowledge and skills needed to make healthy food 
choices, including nutrition education, cooking 
classes, and community workshops. Community 
partnerships can promote sustainable agriculture 
practices by supporting local farmers who use 
environmentally friendly methods of farming and 
educate youth on the importance of local agricul-
ture in Oceanside. Partnerships could also include 
initiatives such as farm-to-school programs, which 
connect local farmers with school cafeterias to pro-
vide fresh, locally-grown produce.

Since 2005 the San Diego region has conducted 
annual Point-in Time (PIT) counts of the homeless. 
The PIT survey, conducted in late January each 
year, represents a snapshot of the total homeless 
population, both sheltered and unsheltered. The 
2023 PIT count recorded over 10,000 sheltered 
and unsheltered individuals in the region. The most 
recent Point-in-Time Count, which was completed 
on January 26, 2023, revealed that there are 290 
unsheltered individuals residing in Oceanside. This 
is an 8.8% decrease from the number of unshel-
tered individuals accounted for in 2022. According 
to the Point-in-Time Count, there were 298 shel-
tered individuals, with 140 people in emergency 
shelter and 158 people in transitional housing.3
The City and its nonprofit partners have a compre-
hensive approach to addressing homelessness that 
focuses on outreach programs, providing access 
to supportive services, and securing housing. The 
City’s focus is to connect people experiencing 
homelessness with the resources they need to 
achieve stability and well-being. The Housing and 
Neighborhood Services Department provides a 
myriad of homeless services to help provide shelter 
to the unsheltered population. This includes the 
Navigation Center on Oceanside Boulevard, tran-
sitional housing facilities, and the Housing Choice 
Voucher Program (formerly known as Section 8 
housing). Additionally, Neighborhood Services 
provides supportive services and resources for vet-
erans, seniors, and people experiencing domestic 
violence and child abuse. Through these efforts, 
the City aims to prevent and reduce homelessness 
in the community. The City’s approach to ensuring 
access to housing for all individuals is described in 
more detail in the City’s Housing Element.
The City also funds two Oceanside Police 
Department Homeless Outreach Teams (HOT) 
that travel with social workers to aid people expe-
riencing homelessness. The HOTs provide sup-
port and enforcement through public safety and 
trauma informed care. The mission of the HOT is 
to ensure the rights and dignity of people experi-
encing homelessness are respected and protected 
while maintaining public order and safety for all 
Oceanside residents.

GOALS AND POLICIES
GOAL
7-A	
All members of the community will 
have access to housing, public and 
private healthcare services and facilities, 
healthy food, supportive services, and 
community resources. 
7-B	
Through its participation in local and 
regional efforts to address homeless-
ness, the City will continue to provide 
for the homeless population in a 
compassionate, proactive, and holistic 
manner, leveraging the resources of 
multiple City disciplines, other public 
agencies, community-based organiza-
tions, and the business community to 
promote coordinated and equitable 
approaches to both serving the needs 
of unhoused individuals and addressing 
the impacts of homelessness on local 
communities.
7-C	
The public health, public safety, envi-
ronmental, social, and economic impacts 
of homelessness will be mitigated in a 
manner consistent with constitutional 
rights, relevant federal and state law, 
and the City’s core values.

7.3	 Civic Engagement
A civically engaged city is one where residents 
become active stewards of their shared future, 
nurturing policies and initiatives that reflect their 
aspirations and address unique challenges in each 
community. Residents' ability to engage in com-
munity life also contributes to feelings of social 
cohesion and well-being. Inclusive policymaking 
also promotes environmental justice by providing 
residents with regular opportunities to share their 
environmental and health concerns and make their 
voice heard in the policymaking process.  
When designing community engagement efforts, 
it is crucial to identify potential barriers including 
transportation or childcare barriers, inaccessible 
meeting locations, venues, and times; economic or 
educational barriers, and other factors. While there 
is no single “one size fits all” engagement strategy, 
the City will maintain the perspective that every-
one affected by an issue that impacts their com-
munities should have a say in the decision-making 
process and will implement community engage-
ment methods accordingly, working to encourage 
inclusive, participatory processes that are culturally 
appropriate and accessible in all dimensions.  
Of particular issue in civic participation is linguis-
tic isolation, where households speak little to no 
English. Adults who are unable to communicate 
in English often have trouble talking to medical 
professionals and providers of social services, and 
consequently may not get the medical care or social 
support they and their children need. Additionally, 
members of linguistically isolated households may 
not hear or understand important information 
when there is an emergency, or an important com-
munity issue is under consideration. Approximately 
five percent of Oceanside's households are linguis-
tically isolated and lack fluency in English. Of these 
linguistically isolated households, 67 percent speak 
Spanish. Areas with the greatest linguistic isola-
tion are shown in Figure 7-3. Diversity, inclusion, 
and representation are fundamental to the City 
of Oceanside’s employment policy, and the City 
currently offers eligible employees a bilingual pay 
differential. To further increase language access, 
the City may explore development of a Language 
Access Plan, a documented strategy that helps 
ensure meaningful access to government services 
and information for individuals with limited English 
proficiency by providing language assistance ser-
vices and removing language barriers.
An important and related concept to addressing 
civic engagement barriers is the engagement and 
partnership of community organizations that are 
representative of all Oceanside communities. 
Community groups often serve as the voice of 
marginalized and underrepresented populations, 
amplifying their concerns and advocating for their 
rights. Partnership and collaboration can lead to 
more comprehensive and effective solutions, as 
community groups bring valuable local knowledge, 
expertise, and resources to the table. Finally, com-
munity partnerships can help the City build trust, 
empower individuals, and promote social cohesion. 
To increase community partnership, the City will 
support establishment of community liaisons and 
a Community Relations Commission that can help 
advocate on behalf of Oceanside neighborhoods.

7.4	 Parks and 
Community 
Facilities
Community members place high value on 
Oceanside’s public parks and community facilities 
and see these amenities as contributing to the 
character, cohesiveness, and livability of the City’s 
neighborhoods. There is considerable interest 
in programming the City’s parks and community 
facilities to meet current and emerging recreational 
preferences and to accommodate fair and equal use 
by all residents, including the disabled. The HLC 
includes goals and policies addressing park mainte-
nance, programming, and access.
PARKS
The City of Oceanside Parks and Recreation Division 
provides park and recreation services throughout the 
City. The City currently has approximately 642 acres 
of park land. As of 2020, the City’s parks and recre-
ation facilities consist of 17 community and 16 neigh-
borhood and pocket parks, one community garden, 
one regional park, three recreation centers (Junior 
Seau Community Center, Joe Balderrama Recreation 
Center, and Melba Bishop Recreation Center), a 
YMCA and Boys and Girls Club, two senior centers, 
five skate parks, and three pools. Distributed through-
out the City, these parks accommodate playgrounds, 
sports fields, tennis and pickleball courts, basketball 
courts, volleyball courts, picnic areas, and multipur-
pose areas. A number of the City’s parks include nat-
ural habitat, while others abut waterbodies, canyons, 
and hillsides that support native flora and fauna.  
Residents can also enjoy more than 115 acres of 
school play areas as provided through Memorandums 
of Understanding (MOUs) with the Oceanside Unified 
School District. The City also includes two public golf 
courses (Oceanside Municipal and Goat Hill Park) and 
three private golf courses (Emerald Isle, El Camino 
Country Club, and Arrowood). Other facilities, includ-
ing Oceanside's 3.5 miles of beach, harbor, pier, and 
additional community amenities are described in 
more detail in the Remarkable Community and Vital 
and Sustainable Resources elements.
In November 2019, the City of Oceanside adopted 
the Parks and Recreation Master Plan (Master Plan). 
The Master Plan provides a snapshot of park and rec-
reation conditions as of 2019 and, coupled with an 
assessment of community needs and priorities and a 
discussion of trends in park and recreation now and 
in the future, is intended to develop a vision for the 
parks and recreation system through 2030. 
The General Plan defines five major categories of 
park types: Neighborhood Parks, Community Parks, 
Community Centers, Regional Parks, and Special Use 
Parks.

•	 Neighborhood Parks are generally smaller 
parks that provide both passive and limited 
active recreation but tend to focus on passive 
recreation. They are typically less than five acres 
in size. They generally do not include citywide 
facilities, such as gyms, pools, or sports fields.
•	 Community Parks serve daily recreational 
needs of the community as well as the local 
broader neighborhood. They are generally 
larger than five acres in size. Citywide sports 
fields, pools, and court sports are concentrated 
in these locations. Community gardens is 
another category of open space, which could be 
provided in conjunction with or independent of 
parks. 
•	 Community Centers are community buildings 
that provide a wide range of activities serving 
the community as a whole. These centers 
often accommodate special events, recreation 
programs, offices, and community services. 
These facilities can pull users from all over the 
community.
•	 Regional Parks are parks that are larger than 30 
acres, serve the region, and provide a range of 
activities including passive and active recreation 
opportunities and often include open space, 
cultural, and/or natural resources. The sole park 
classified as regional is the 75-acre Guajome 
Regional Park. 
•	 Special Use Parks are a broad category of 
facilities which focus on specific functions, 
themes, or user groups. They include facilities 
such as Heritage Park, Oceanside Harbor and 
Oceanside Pier, and swim facilities.
•	 Golf Courses are counted separately from other 
categories of parks. The City currently has two 
municipal golf courses, the Oceanside Municipal 
Golf Course and the Center City Golf Course/
Goat Hill Park.

KEY REGIONAL PARKS
El Corazon
The 465-acre El Corazon property, bequeathed 
to the City by the Silica Sand Mining Company 
in 1994, currently accommodates a regional 
youth soccer complex, a senior center, an 
aquatics center, and a trail network. Western 
portions of the site are largely comprised of 
protected natural habitat, including riparian 
and wetland habitat within Garrison Creek. 
As outlined in the El Corazon Specific Plan, 
this centrally located property is evolving into 
a regional recreational venue through a series 
of phased improvements. Once complete, the 
buildout of El Corazon will significantly increase 
the City’s overall park acreage. 
Guajome Regional Park
Operated by the County of San Diego, Guajome 
Regional Park surrounds Guajome Lake and 
includes a campground, a 4.3-mile loop trail, 
amphitheater, and several playgrounds and 
picnic areas. Occupying 394 acres near the 
City’s eastern boundary, Guajome Regional 
Park serves as a key link corridor between the 
San Luis Rey River and upland habitat to the 
south and east. Out of the park’s full acreage, 
40 percent of the developed areas, or 75 acres, 
are counted towards the parkland provisions. 
Over 180 bird species have been spotted in 
the vicinity of Guajome Lake, including the 
endangered California Least Tern and the Light 
Footed Clapper Rail.

Future Parkland Need
Buildout of the General Plan would result in a total 
population of 224,200 by 2050. Based on the stan-
dard of five acres per 1,000 residents, this would 
result in a total Neighborhood, Community and 
Special Use parkland need of 1,120 acres, nearly 
double the existing 640.6 acres of parkland. The 
net additional need is 479.4 acres out of which 
26.1 acres should be dedicated to community gar-
dens. To reach this goal, it is imperative that new 
developments in the city include publicly accessible 
parks and open spaces. As the City is largely built 
out and much of the anticipated new development 
will be infill, additional park land will likely take the 
form of small parks, plazas, and linear parks that are 
integrated into new development. Where parkland 
cannot be created on site as a part of new develop-
ments, park impact fee funds should be directed to 
areas lacking park access.
PARK ACCESSIBILITY
The General Plan endeavors to ensure that parks 
are evenly distributed throughout the City by main-
taining parkland accessibility standard of 10-minute 
walk for Neighborhood Parks and 20-minute walk 
for Community and Special Use Parks. Community 
Parks also serve as Neighborhood Parks. Much of 
the City currently lacks access to a park within a 
20 minute walk. Areas of the City with the most 
access to parks include much of the area along 
Mission Avenue and Mesa Drive. All of the City’s 
Neighborhood Planning Areas (NPAs) have areas 
that lack access to parks. NPAs with the lowest 
access to walkable parks include Fire Mountain, 
Mira Costa, Peacock, South Morro Hills and 
Eastside Capistrano. Access to parks by driving is 
higher but does not account for Oceanside resi-
dents who are unable or unwilling to drive to reach 
their Community Parks and does not align with 
the preferences that residents have expressed 
throughout the planning process for walkable com-
plete neighborhoods.
New community parks and recreation facilities 
should be centered in areas that are currently 
underserved and/or that are expected to see 
higher density development. Under this General 
Plan, the majority of new development will be infill 
along the corridors, which have very little vacant 
land available for parks and open space. This 
necessitates a more creative approach to incorpo-
rating parks and park amenities into new devel-
opment, including emphasizing smaller parks and 
public spaces such as publicly accessible rooftops, 
plazas, linear parks, and parklets, as well as City 
initiative to proactively acquire sites for parkland 
development in strategic areas.

PARK AMENITY PROVISIONS
The General Plan does not specify park amenity stan-
dards; Standards specified in the Parks Master Plan are 
presented below for reference only. Table 7-3 shows 
standards for typical park amenities such as sport and 
game areas, restrooms, and multipurpose fields. These 
recreation facility metrics were developed based on 
national and regional standards and refined to reflect 
the input and demographics of the City of Oceanside 
and should be reevaluated in the next update to the 
Parks Master Plan. The park amenity standards include 
school recreation facilities that have MOUs for joint 
use with the City, but due to the limited hours of public 
access, facilities are counted at 40 percent. Although 
Guajome Regional Park is a county park, its amenities 
are fully counted because it is located entirely within 
the City limits.
Specific amenities that are projected to meet or 
exceed suggested standards through 2050 include 
playgrounds, basketball courts, and diamond fields. 
Multi-purpose fields, outdoor aquatic centers, com-
munity centers, and gymnasiums all currently meet the 
suggested standards, but are projected to fall short by 
2050 based on the buildout presented in this General 
Plan. Amenities that do not currently meet the stan-
dards include picnic shelters (25+ people), trails (paved 
and non-paved), skate parks, skate spots, tennis, volley-
ball, and pickleball courts. While there is limited space 
in the City available for additional parks and park ame-
nities, new developments can include publicly acces-
sible amenities as a part of a public benefits package 
where they are eligible. Additionally, as outlined in the 
Parks Master Plan, existing parks can be renovated and 
improved to include additional amenities.
TRAILS
A well-connected trails network is a critical com-
ponent of a livable community, promoting active 
recreation and a healthy lifestyle for Oceanside 
residents and serving as a point of attraction for 
visitors. Throughout the General Plan process, 
many Oceansiders expressed their desire for a 
more safe and comprehensive system of trails to 
link the City together and provide access to safe 
recreation and alternative modes of transportation.
A designated Bicycle Friendly City, Oceanside 
includes the San Luis Rey River Trail (SLRRT) and 
the Coastal Rail Trail (CRT). Implementing the 
western reach of the regional Inland Rail Trail (IRT) 
through the Oceanside Boulevard Corridor would 
provide the City with improved access to Loma Alta 
Creek and opportunities to link the existing trails 
and enhance the City’s recreational and transporta-
tion amenities. The City is preparing a Trails Master 
Plan that will focus on completing the City’s “spine” 
trails (SLRRT, CRT, IRT) and linking these trails to 
parks and other key destinations through a second-
ary trails network.  
Along Mission Avenue, the SLRRT is an asset to 
the City, providing opportunities for recreation and 
alternative modes of transportation, but improve-
ments to the feeling of safety along the trail and 
enhancements to trail amenities could encourage 
more people to use the trail for recreation and 
transportation.

COMMUNITY FACILITIES
In addition to parks, the City of Oceanside contains 
a wide variety of community facilities and rec-
reational amenities. Quality community facilities 
ensure that Oceansiders have access to a range of 
recreational amenities and event spaces. The City 
can support these community assets by continuing 
to offer relevant programming, expanding services 
and hours, and ensuring continued funding is avail-
able for community facilities.
Information related to cultural venues is presented 
in the Remarkable Community (RC) Element.
Recreation and Community Centers
The City of Oceanside has four recreation 
and community centers: Junior Seau Beach 
Community Center, John Landes Recreation 
Center, Joe Balderrama Recreation Center, Melba 
Bishop Recreation Center. A branch of the YMCA 
and the Boys and Girls Club are also located in 
Oceanside to serve residents, however they are 
not funded or operated by the City. These facil-
ities are designed to meet Oceanside’s diverse 
recreational needs and include features such as 
gymnasiums, meeting rooms, kitchens and host 
activities such as fitness classes, teen programs, 
childcare, sports activities, and special events. 
Senior Centers
Oceanside includes two dedicated Senior Centers 
to support Oceansiders as they age. The Oceanside 
Senior Center, located on Country Club Lane, 
provides a range of supportive programming for 
seniors including programs that support access to 
food, transportation, and recreation. 
Part of the El Corazon development, the first 
phase of the El Corazon Senior Center (ECSC) 
opened in 2009, providing services and facilities 
for Oceanside’s 55 and over community. The ECSC 
offers supportive programming, facility rentals, and 
gym memberships.
Oceanside Municipal Airport
The Oceanside Municipal Airport, also known 
as the Bob Maxwell Memorial Field, is a 43 acre 
public-use general aviation airport that houses 
one runway and offers fuel services. The airport 
also has a small helipad. The airport is owned by 
the City of Oceanside and operated, maintained, 
and developed by Airport Property Ventures, LLC 
(APV).4
Additional information about the Airport and rele-
vant safety protocols are included in the Safe and 
Resilient Environment (SRE) Element.
Public Right of Way
The public right of way, or the streets and side-
walks that are publicly owned and accessible, rep-
resent a valuable community facility that the City 
can leverage to improve quality of life and provide a 
sense of place. By providing adequate and well-de-
fined space for active transportation and incorpo-
rating wayfinding elements, the City’s rights of way 
can improve pedestrian and bicycle connections 
between community facilities and other resources. 
Existing parkways and sidewalks in key areas can 
leverage improvements to become open space 
areas or outdoor dining areas.
Additional information on the public right of way 
as a contributor to a high quality public realm 
are included in the Remarkable Community (RC) 
Element.

SCHOOL FACILITIES
Within City boundaries, Oceanside children 
are served by five schools in the Vista Unified

BEACHES
Along Oceanside’s three miles of coastline, 
approximately 35 acres of usable beaches are pub-
licly accessible to the community for recreational 
usage. Oceanside’s beaches are a valuable and 
beloved public amenity, and many Oceansiders 
visit the beach at least once a week for swimming, 
surfing, picnicking, walking, and more. Oceanside’s 
beaches are not covered in this element of the 
General Plan. For further discussion of the City’s 
beaches and the challenges and opportunities that 
they face, see the Vital and Sustainable Resources 
Element.
OCEANSIDE HARBOR 
Oceanside Small Craft Harbor (Harbor) is operated 
as a public marina, run by the Oceanside Small 
Craft Harbor District (SCHD). Formed in 1960, 
the SCHD is responsible for public marine facili-
ties and leasehold improvements associated with 
the north and south basins of the Harbor and the 
adjacent shoreline within the City of Oceanside. 
SCHD operations are confined to approximately 
100 acres associated with the Harbor and leased 
from the City.
The SCHD maintains the Harbor as a high-qual-
ity recreational boating center; a transient boat 
stopover point; and a major patrol, search, 
and rescue base. The Harbor is managed by 
the Harbor and Beaches Division of the Public 
Works Department. The Harbor is served by the 
Marine Safety Unit (MSU) of the Oceanside Fire 
Department Lifeguard Division. They provide 
marine and land-based enforcement services to

The U.S. Coast Guard opened a new shore facil-

of the USCG Point Hobart from 1969 to 1999. 
In 1993, the Oceanside Harbor District was 
annexed by the City of Oceanside and all District 
employees became City employees, including the 
Oceanside Harbor Police. In 1999, Point Hobart 
was decommissioned, and the Oceanside Harbor 
Police became the only rescue vessels between 
Dana Point Harbor and Mission Bay. Currently, the 
Oceanside Police Harbor Unit remains the primary 
response vessel for over 37 miles of coastline, 
assisting in mutual aid mutual aid calls for service 
from San Mateo to Del Mar.
Today, the Harbor is home to a hotel, several 
restaurants, gift shops, a deli, and an ice cream 
and sweet shop. Construction of a new harbor

yacht club, commercial sportfishing fleet and com-

tours, and sailing excursions. The SCHD is also 
responsible for administering leases for the tour-
ist and restaurant facilities. Oceanside Harbor is 
first in southern California to sell only R99 renew-
able diesel fuel (sourced solely from animal and 
plant waste). This greatly reduces harmful diesel 
exhaust emissions.
The SCHD is dependent to the City of Oceanside; 
the Oceanside City Council acts as the District 
Board of Directors. A permanent Harbor and 
Beaches Advisory Committee advises the Board of 
Directors. District operations and slip rental ser-
vices are overseen by City personnel. Additional 
information on coastal amenities is presented 
in the Vital and Sustainable Resources (VSR)

LIBRARIES
A multifaceted, inclusive, and well-resourced library 
system provides equitable access to educational 
materials, cultural experiences, civic events, social 
activities, meeting spaces, and other resources that 
support self-actualization and community-building. 
In addition to fulfilling its traditional role as a repos-
itory of books to borrow, a quality library system 
can provide community members with internet 
access, online databases, tutoring, academic and 
vocational programs, a seed exchange, equipment 
rental, historical records, and connections to essen-
tial services (e.g., healthcare, housing, literacy, job 
placement). Libraries can support the work of 
local artists, craftspeople, storytellers, teachers, 
researchers, etc. In so doing, libraries can foster 
a deep and lasting appreciation for the arts, the 
written word, and lifelong learning. Libraries can 
also provide resources for the deaf and visually-im-
paired, neuro-diverse individuals, and those with 
limited mobility.  
Open to everyone, libraries are public spaces that 
provide comfort, connection, and solace for com-
munity members. Moreover, during emergencies, 
libraries can meet basic needs by providing shelter 
from storm events, extreme heat, poor air quality, 
etc.
The City’s library system is a department cur-
rently known as the Oceanside Public Library. 
Following its new strategic plan, its name will be 
updated to the Oceanside Library and Cultural Arts 
Department. The department currently operates 
three established public libraries in Oceanside: Civic 
Center Library (at 330 N Coast Highway), Mission 
Branch Library (at 3861-B1 Mission Avenue), and 
John Landes Community Center Library (at 2855 
Cedar Road); as well as Oceanside READS Learning 
Center (at 804 Pier View Way, #101), which pro-
vides literacy tutoring and related services for 
adults and their families. These facilities are shown 
in Figure 7-6. The Library also provides two Mobile 
Libraries, vehicles that deliver library services and 
materials to Oceanside residents in underserved 
neighborhoods, at regularly scheduled public stops, 
and Home Delivery service as an option for most 
homes in Oceanside. 
As its new name will indicate, the Oceanside 
Library and Cultural Arts Department also has 
many responsibilities related to the Oceanside Arts 
Commission and Oceanside CA Cultural District. 
The department’s website, currently oceansideli-
brary.org, provides information about all aspects 
of its arts, library and other services, with links to 
apps for the Library’s e-books, information on the 
arts in Oceanside, sign-up for programs, and every-
thing else it offers.
Arguably the most important assets in a library 
system are the staff members who acquaint users 
with library resources, organize these resources so 
they are easy to access and navigate, assist with 
research tasks, plan and host a wide range of edu-
cational programs, and update and “weed” their 
book collections, databases, and other offerings. 
Library staff are often the first point of contact for 
community members seeking information critical to 
their well-being, quality of life, and civic participa-
tion. As such, library staff are important emissaries

As Oceanside grows and changes under this General 
Plan, citywide demand for educational facilities may 
change as well. Changes in demand for school facil-
ities would result not only from new population in 
new housing, but also from changes in population 
demographics and resulting change in school-age

not provide or manage schools, enrollment, or plans 
for future school facilities, this General Plan seeks to 
ensure that the City works closely with local school 
districts to ensure adequate provision of educational 
facilities for all Oceansiders. 
Oceanside students in kindergarten through 12th 
grade are served by the Oceanside Unified School 
District (OUSD), the Vista Unified School District 
(VUSD), the Bonsall Unified School District (BUSD), 
the Carlsbad Unified School District (CUSD), and 
a variety of parochial and secular private schools. 
OUSD operates 23 schools: 16 elementary schools 
(three of which are located on Camp Pendleton), four 
middle schools, two comprehensive high schools, and 
one alternative high school. These facilities are shown 
in Figure 7-6.
Oceanside students may also attend BUSD, CUSD, 
or VUSD just outside the City. The VUSD serves 
Oceanside students at four elementary schools, 
two middle schools, and one high school. The BUSD 
operates one elementary school in Oceanside. The 
CUSD does not have facilities in Oceanside but 
does serve Oceanside residents. Both the OUSD 
and VUSD operate adult education programs.
Located in the City’s Tri-City Neighborhood 
Planning Area, MiraCosta Community College 
has an enrollment of approximately 10,000 stu-
dents and offers a range of degree and certificate 
programs. The community college also offers a 
wide range of job training programs for veterans, 
active duty military, military spouses, and the 
unemployed. For local employers, MiraCosta offers 
professional development and technical training 
courses through its Technology Career Institute. 
Vocational schools in Oceanside include California 
Career School and the Healthcare Academy of 
California.

7.6	 Water, Wastewater, 
and Stormwater 
Utilities and 
Infrastructure
Long-term growth and redevelopment strategies 
within the City need to consider the potential 
impacts on existing utility infrastructure and avail-
ability of resources to meet increasing demands. 
Wastewater infrastructure should have sufficient 
conveyance and treatment capacity to meet 
future sewer flows, provide recycled water for 
sustainable growth and produce advanced treated 
water for aquifer injection to reduce reliance on 
imported water. Stormwater infrastructure should 
be designed to prevent loss of life and property 
from flooding and adapt to changes in water quality 
regulations that protect surface waters. In addition, 
local water infrastructure needs to adapt to meet 
the challenges of increasing access and availabil-
ity of local supplies while addressing the demand 
impacts from new regulations.  
In the San Diego region, as in many other parts 
of the world, limited fresh water supplies are 
stretched as a growing population demands more 
of it, and new buildings and roads simultaneously 
reduce its quality (through runoff) and availability 
(through reduced groundwater infiltration and 
decreasing snowpacks). At the same time, the 
practice of importing drinking water has become 
less desirable because of lack of long-term sustain-
ability and the energy used in transporting water—
water-related energy use consumes about 12 per-
cent of California’s total energy use every year.5 In 
the San Diego County Water Authority’s (SDCWA) 
service area region, local sources provided only 28 
percent of the water used in 2020.6

INSTITUTIONAL AND 
REGULATORY FRAMEWORK 
California Graywater Regulations 
In 2009, as part of the Governor’s declared State of 
Emergency, Chapter 16A: Nonpotable Water Reuse 
Systems was incorporated into the 2007 California 
Plumbing Code. Chapter 16A establishes minimum 
requirements for the installation of graywater systems 
in residential occupancies, providing guidance and 
flexibility designed to encourage the use of graywater. 
The standards allow small graywater systems to be 
installed in homes without a building permit, substan-
tially reducing the barriers to installing small residential 
graywater systems in California. The purpose of the 
regulations is to conserve water by facilitating greater 
reuse of laundry, shower, sink, and similar sources of 
discharge for irrigation and/or indoor use. 
California Water Efficiency 
Partnership
In 2018, the California Urban Water Conservation 
Council (CUWCC) transitioned to the California 
Water Efficiency Partnership (CalWEP) renewing 
and expanding its role as an innovative leader, 
voice and expert on water efficiency in California. 
CalWEP is committed to providing cutting-edge 
expertise on California water issues, challenges, 
and opportunities within a broad collaborative 
framework. The City is a member of CalWEP and 
collaborates with other members and stakeholder 
agencies to advance water efficiency in California. 
Integrated Regional Water 
Management 
The Integrated Regional Water Management 
(IRWM) program is a local water resources manage-
ment approach aimed at securing long-term water 
supply reliability within California by first recogniz-
ing the inter-connectivity of water supplies and the 
environment, and then pursuing projects yielding 
multiple benefits for water supplies, water quality, 
and natural resources. The San Diego IRWM pro-
gram is an interdisciplinary effort by water retailers, 
wastewater agencies, stormwater and flood man-
agers, watershed groups, the business community, 
Native American tribes, agriculture, and regulatory 
agencies to coordinate water resource manage-
ment efforts and to enable the San Diego region to 
apply for grants tied to the Department of Water 
Resources IRWM program. The City of Oceanside 
has participated in the program as a member 
agency, and in planning, coordination, and support-
ing watershed activities related to the IRWM.

STORMWATER
Stormwater Collection and 
Management
Stormwater runoff within the City from developed 
areas is conveyed through storm drains into creeks 
and rivers that flow from east to west towards 
the ocean. The City is responsible for ensuring its 
storm drains function as designed by performing 
routine maintenance and fixing/replacing damaged 
or undersized storm drains. Proactive storm drain 
management increases the useful life of City assets, 
protects water quality and helps prevent loss of life 
and property from flooding.
The City completed a 2013 Master Plan of Drainage 
which details the hydrologic conditions of the City 
and hydraulic capacity of the storm drain system 
to convey simulated storm events. The master 
plan identified inadequate storm drains as those 
that cannot convey either the 10-year, 25-year, or 
100-year storm event. Inadequate facility capacity 
can be caused by the downstream storm drains 
filling up during model simulation and causing a 
backwater effect upstream, or the size of a facility 
(pipe segment(s)) is constricting flow and insufficient 
to convey a specific design storm event scenario. 
In total, over 40,000 linear feet or approximately 
10% of the storm drain system was identified to 
have inadequate capacity and recommended to be 
upsized. A prioritized list of projects based on issue 
severity is being completed through the City’s CIP. 
Stormwater policies that regulate discharge from 
new and re-development projects are designed 
to limit impacts on the downstream storm drain 
system and receiving water quality. The City owns 
and operates a municipal storm drain system and 
is therefore required to obtain coverage under a 
regional National Pollution Discharge Elimination 
System (NPDES) permit for municipal stormwater 
discharges. This permit, issued by the San Diego 
Regional Water Quality Control Board, requires the 
City to implement stormwater management design 
standards that mimic a pre-development runoff pat-
tern. These design standards include site features 
and treatment systems that limit the concentration 
and timing of stormwater discharges from new and 
re-development projects that exceed certain imper-
vious area thresholds. Increases in impervious area 
(like parking lots and rooftops) will speed up and 
concentrate stormwater runoff which could exceed 
the designed capacity of the downstream storm 
drains. Limits on the timing and concentration of 
stormwater runoff through the use of detention/
retention practices will allow the existing storm 
drain infrastructure to function as designed regard-
less of upstream increases in impervious areas.
Water quality impacts from new and re-develop-
ment projects are regulated both through the City’s 
municipal ordinances and the regional municipal 
stormwater NPDES permit. Water quality objectives 
established in the regional NPDES permit are met 
using low impact development (LID) that use biolog-
ical and/or mechanical filtration systems to target 
pollutants that may impact the receiving water 
quality. The use of stormwater policies that regulate 
discharges from new and re-development projects 
has proven to be an effective strategy to promote 
ecosystem health and reduces the impacts on exist-
ing infrastructure from increased urban density.

7.7	 Energy and Waste
ENERGY
Reductions in energy consumption and waste 
production are key to reducing Oceanside’s over-
all GHG emissions. Use of fossil fuels for energy 
is the primary contributor to GHG emissions. The 
United States, with less than 5 percent of the world 
population, consumes about 20 percent of global 
energy. Among states, California is the second 
largest consumer of energy, second only to Texas. 
However, California’s per capita energy consump-
tion is relatively low, due to both mild weather that 
reduces energy demand for heating and cooling, 
and the government’s energy-efficiency programs 
and standards. Petroleum and natural gas currently 
supply most of the energy consumed in California.
The City’s Energy and Climate Action Element 
(ECAE), adopted in 2019, provides policy direction 
for energy efficiency and renewable energy. The 
ECAE considers how energy demand is driven by 
land use patterns, transportation, economic activ-
ity, consumer choices, and climate change. The 
goals and policies of the ECAE are supported by 
specific greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions reduction 
measures outlined in the City’s concurrently pre-
pared and adopted Climate Action Plan (CAP). 
Oceanside receives electricity and natural gas 
from the investor-owned utility San Diego Gas and 
Electric (SDG&E), which procures and distributes 
these resources and maintains the distribution net-
works. Assembly Bill 117, adopted in 2002, allows 
local governments to procure electricity from sup-
pliers of their choice, including the aggregation of 
community electrical loads to purchase alternative 
sources of power within its jurisdiction. In recent 
years, many local governments in California have 
chosen to create or join “community choice aggre-
gations” that secure their own electrical energy 
supply contracts, often to control the percentage 
of renewable power in their energy portfolios. In 
contrast to investor-owned utilities, community 
choice aggregators function as public agencies 
operating as Joint Powers Authorities made up of 
the member agencies. While the community choice 
aggregator procures power contracts from a mix 
of traditional and renewable energy generators, 
SDG&E continues to provide billing services as well 
as maintenance of the electrical transmission grid. 
By aggregating buying power, this model allows the 
Joint Powers Authority to supply a higher percent-
age of renewable power to its service area com-
pared to the investor-owned utility. 
In 2022, the Oceanside City Council voted to join 
Clean Energy Alliance (CEA), a community choice 
aggregation comprised of other North County 
cities. Oceanside’s participation in CEA is, in part, 
motivated by a GHG emission reduction measure 
in the CAP that calls upon the City to procure at

SOLID WASTE AND RECYCLING
Solid waste generated by households and busi-
nesses in the community that is not recycled or 
composted goes to a landfill. Californians have 
long been committed to recycling out of a desire 
to reduce litter, conserve natural resources, and 
decrease reliance on landfills. Landfill diversion rate 
is a metric used to calculate how much waste is 
recycled or recovered and doesn’t end up in land-
fills and demonstrates progress to reducing waste 
accumulation. For example, a 100 percent landfill 
diversion rate would indicate that all given waste 
was able to be recycled or reused, thereby prevent-
ing it from ending up in a landfill.
Regulatory Context
In October 2014 Governor Brown signed AB 1826 
Chesbro (Chapter 727, Statutes of 2014), requir-
ing businesses to recycle their organic waste on 
and after April 1, 2016, depending on the amount 
of waste they generate per week. This law also 
requires that on and after January 1, 2016, local 
jurisdictions across the state implement an organic 
waste recycling program to divert organic waste 
generated by businesses, including multifamily res-
idential dwellings that consist of five or more units 
(please note, however, that multifamily dwellings 
are not required to have a food waste diversion 
program). Organic waste (also referred to as organ-
ics throughout this resource), for the purposes of 
AB 1826, means food waste, green waste, land-
scape and pruning waste, nonhazardous wood 
waste, and food-soiled paper waste that is mixed in 
with food waste.
In 2016, the State Legislature passed SB 1383 
which seeks to recover edible food from being dis-
carded into a landfill. The intention is to reduce gas 
emissions from decomposing food while reducing 
waste and providing food to people in need. The SB 
1383 regulations require that jurisdictions conduct 
education and outreach on organics recycling to all 
residents, businesses (including those that gener-
ate edible food that can be donated), haulers, solid 
waste facilities, and local food banks and other 
food recovery organizations.
In 2010, the Oceanside City Council adopted 
Resolution No.10-R0636-1 calling for a 75 percent 
to 90 percent diversion rate from landfill by 2020. 
To support this goal, the City Council adopted a 
Zero Waste Strategic Resource Management Plan 
(Zero Waste Plan) in 2012. As of 2019, the City’s 
diversion rate was 67 percent. In order to continue 
working towards the goals of waste reduction and 
diversion, and to maintain compliance with State 
regulations, the City adopted an updated Zero 
Waste Plan.
On March 17, 2021, City Council approved the 
2020 Zero Waste Plan, which expands upon exist-
ing programming outlined in the 2012 Zero Waste 
Plan to allow the City to meet its zero waste goals 
and maps out the next decade of zero waste pro-
gramming, services, and compliance with State 
laws.

7.8	 Noise
PLANNING CONTEXT
The primary source of noise in Oceanside is vehi-
cle traffic on the highways and streets within and 
adjacent to the Planning Area. The Planning Area 
is also affected, to a lesser extent, by noise from 
trains and aircrafts in the region. Localized sources 
of noise, such as car washing facilities; places of 
entertainment (music venues, bars, and restau-
rants); construction and agricultural activities; 
landscaping and industrial equipment; and heating, 
ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) equipment, 
are also present in the Planning Area, typical of any 
urbanized area.
There are several types of measurements that are 
used to characterize the time-varying nature of 
sound. These measurements include the equiva-
lent sound level (Leq), the minimum and maximum 
sound levels (Lmin and Lmax), percentile-exceeded 
sound levels (such as L10, L20), the day-night sound 
level (Ldn), and the community noise equivalent level 
(CNEL). Ldn and CNEL values differ by less than 
one dB, and, as a matter of practice, Ldn and CNEL 
values are typically considered to be equivalent. 
Figure 7-8 shows the decibel levels associated with 
different common sounds.
NOISE REGULATIONS OVERVIEW
Federal, State, and local agencies regulate different 
aspects of environmental noise. Generally, the fed-
eral government sets noise standards for transpor-
tation-related noise sources that are closely linked 
to interstate commerce. These sources include 
aircraft, locomotives, and trucks.
Figure 7-8: Noise Measurement 
WHAT IS NOISE? 
Noise is commonly defined as unwanted 
sound that annoys or disturbs people and 
potentially causes an adverse psychological 
or physiological effect on human health, 
whereas sound is mechanical energy (vibra-
tion) transmitted by pressure waves over 
a medium such as air or water. Sound is 
characterized by various parameters that 
include the rate of oscillation of sound 
waves (frequency), the speed of propa-
gation, and the pressure level or energy 
content (amplitude). In particular, the sound 
pressure level is the most common descrip-
tor used to characterize the loudness of an 
ambient (existing) sound level.
Although the decibel (dB) scale, a logarithmic 
scale, is used to quantify sound intensity, it does 
not accurately describe how sound intensity is 
perceived by human hearing. The human ear is not 
equally sensitive to all frequencies in the entire 
spectrum, so noise measurements are weighted 
more heavily for frequencies to which humans are 
sensitive in a process called A-weighting, written as 
dBA and referred to as A-weighted decibels.

The State government sets noise standards for 
transportation noise sources such as automobiles, 
light trucks, and motorcycles. Title 24, Chapter 12 
of the California Code of Regulations California 
Noise Insulation Standards regulate interior noise 
levels for residential habitable rooms and non-resi-
dential structures. Under these regulations, interior 
noise levels attributable to exterior noise sources 
cannot exceed 45 day-night noise levels (Ldn) in 
any habitable room for residential uses or 50 dB(A) 
Leq for occupied areas of non-residential buildings 
during any hour of operation.
Noise sources associated with industrial, commer-
cial, and construction activities are generally sub-
ject to local control through performance standards 
in municipal codes or noise ordinances. Chapter 38 
of Oceanside’s Municipal Code is the City’s Noise 
Control Ordinance which regulates unnecessary, 
excessive, and annoying noises from all sources. 
Furthermore, General Plan policies included in this 
chapter are intended to guide and influence devel-
opment plans and provide land use compatibility 
standards for noise. 
EXISTING AND PROJECTED 
ROADWAY NOISE
The dominant source of noise in the Planning Area 
and in most urban areas is noise from vehicle traf-
fic on roadways. There are several major highways 
in and adjacent to the Planning Area, including 
Interstate 5 and State Routes 76 and 78. The local, 
City-maintained roadway system features five 
major north-south surface streets (Coast Highway, 
El Camino Real, Rancho del Oro, College Boulevard 
and Melrose Drive) and three major east-west 
surface streets (Mission Avenue, Oceanside 
Boulevard, and Vista Way). Traffic congestion hot 
spots include intersections along State Route 76, 
Oceanside Boulevard near Interstate 5, El Camino 
Real near State Route 78, and College Boulevard 
near State Route 78 and Oceanside Boulevard. 
Figure 7-9 shows existing noise conditions in the 
Planning Area from roadway traffic sources. Twenty-
four-hour Ldn from all roadways in the Planning 
Area range from a low of 60 dBA to a high of 75+ 
dBA. Figure 7-10 shows the noise contours for 2050 
based on projected traffic conditions, based on traf-
fic analysis conducted for the General Plan. 
AIRPORT NOISE
The Oceanside Municipal Airport, also known as 
the Bob Maxwell Memorial Field, is a public-use 
general aviation airport located two miles north-
east of the City’s downtown, and is owned by 
Airport Property Ventures, LLC (APV). State Route 
76 is located immediately south of the airport. As 
discussed in Section 6.6, portions of the Planning 
Area are located in all six of the designated Safety 
Zones which delineate land use standards and land 
development restrictions based on the zone’s prox-
imity to the airport.
In the Oceanside Municipal Airport Land Use 
Compatibility Plan (ALUCP), adopted in 2010, 
contemporaneous and future noise levels in the 
area around the airport were modeled and visu-
ally depicted in contour maps. Based on these 
airport contour maps, no residential portions of 
the Planning Area are located within the modeled 
airport noise contours. The quietest modeled 
contour, 60 CNEL, is considered the maximum 
acceptable level in the ALUCP for new residential 
uses. With the exception of a few commercial par-
cels and open space areas, all Planning Area uses 
are outside of this contour and thus experience 
noise levels that are lower than 60 CNEL from the 
Oceanside Municipal Airport. Occasional aircraft 
overflight noise may be audible to people in the 
Planning Area, however, 24-hour average noise 
levels from airport operations in the Planning Area 
are compatible with the ALUCP.

RAILROAD NOISE
Oceanside Transit Center (OTC) accommodates 
four separate rail lines in the Planning Area – North 
County Transit District’s (NCTD) Coaster and Sprinter 
as well as Metrolink’s Orange County Line and 
Amtrak’s Surfliner. The Coaster commuter rail service 
operates between the Oceanside Transit Center and 
the Santa Fe Depot in San Diego. The route follows 
the South Coast Highway (101) within the City of 
Oceanside. The Sprinter hybrid-rail system con-
nects Oceanside at the Oceanside Transit Center to 
Escondido via the Highway 78 corridor. The Orange 
County Line is a commuter rail line that runs from the 
Oceanside Transit Station through Orange County to 
Union Station in Downtown Los Angeles. The route 
alignment generally follows the Interstate 5 corridor 
to Union Station. The Inland Empire–Orange County 
(IEOC) Line is a commuter rail line that runs from the 
Oceanside Transit Center through Orange County 
to San Bernardino. The Pacific Surfliner is an inter-
city rail line that runs between the Santa Fe Depot 
in Downtown San Diego along the Highway 101 
corridor to the Oceanside Transit Center and then 
to the north through Orange County, Los Angeles, 
and Ventura County, terminating in San Luis Obispo. 
For land uses in the Planning Area near the railroad 
tracks, noise from the train horn, bell, and crossing 
gates are part of the ambient noise environment.
NOISE SENSITIVE RECEPTORS 
There are a wide variety of land uses located 
throughout the Planning Area, including noise- sen-
sitive land uses. Noise-sensitive land uses are those 
that are most sensitive to high noise levels, includ-
ing residences, religious facilities, schools, childcare 
centers, hospitals, long-term healthcare facilities, 
convalescent centers, and retirement homes. All 
of these land use types occur within the Planning 
Area and are generally distributed throughout the 
City. For more about existing land use patterns and 
General Plan land use designations, see Chapter 3: 
Efficient and Compatible Land Use.

7.9	 Smart City 
Technologies
INVESTMENTS IN TECHNOLOGY
Evolving technologies and data sources have 
the potential to inform and improve Oceanside’s 
municipal operations. Collecting and analyzing data 
on city systems and operations can contribute to 
the City in a variety of ways: air and water quality 
monitoring can help identify health risks and pollu-
tion sources so that the City can address adverse 
conditions; real time traffic monitoring can improve 
road operations and access for emergency services; 
motion activated dimmable streetlights can save 
energy and reduce light pollution; and more. 
Ensuring that the data that is collected and man-
aged by the City is up to date and publicly accessi-
ble through an open data portal can improve inter-
agency coordination and encourage innovation and 
streamline access to funding sources for projects in 
Oceanside.
TELECOMMUNICATIONS
Reliable access to the internet and telecommunica-
tions systems plays an increasingly important part 
in daily and civic life, helping people to work, learn, 
access services, and stay connected to friends and 
family. It is also critical for municipal operations and 
the health of the local economy. Expanding access 
to reliable, high-speed internet service is a priority 
for Oceanside. The City currently has seven inter-
net service providers that offer services, ranging in 
subscription cost and download speeds. 
With high-speed broadband connectivity, res-
idents can leverage a wide range of education, 
employment, and entertainment resources. In 
order to increase access to high speed internet, 
the City Council approved a contract in 2022 with 
siFi Networks Oceanside LLC, a high-speed fiber 
internet company, to install an estimated 900 miles 
of fiber conduit throughout the City. This will rep-
resent an approximate $200 million investment in 
Oceanside and will provide significant public bene-
fit. Once the network is complete, every property 
on a public street in Oceanside would have access 
to Gigabyte-speed internet service and would 
greatly enhance connectivity for residents and 
businesses.
BRIDGING THE DIGITAL DIVIDE
According to the 2020 American Communities 
Survey, 7.5 percent of Oceanside households lack 
internet access, and 4.4 percent lack access to a 
computing device in the home. Households with 
an annual income of less than $20,000 have the 
lowest rate of internet subscriptions. Ensuring 
that residents can easily and affordably connect to 
the internet is a priority for the City and will help 
improve quality of life and access to education and 
resources for Oceansiders.
In 2022, Oceanside developed a Digital Equity Plan 
aimed at understanding and addressing the City’s 
digital divide by advancing three key factors for 
digital equity: access to reliable internet; access to 
digital services; and the expansion and promotion 
of digital literacy skills training. The Plan outlines 
goals and objectives that build on the work that 
Oceanside is already doing to ensure equitable 
access to digital resources for its community and 
provides centralized lists of existing digital equity 
programs related to device access, digital literacy, 
digital services and internet access. 
The Oceanside Public Library currently provides 
several digital equity services including provid-
ing free on-site access to Wi-Fi and computers 
(desktop or laptop), and holding digital literacy 
and drop-in tech help classes at the Mission 
Branch, Civic Center and John Landes Libraries, 
and Oceanside READS Learning Center, some as 
frequently as weekly. Oceanside’s Senior Services 
division and MiraCosta College also offer free com-
puter training classes for older adults at several 
locations. The Digital Equity Plan aims to expand 
these efforts and serve as a roadmap to further 
increase access to the internet, digital devices, and 
digital literacy in Oceanside.
