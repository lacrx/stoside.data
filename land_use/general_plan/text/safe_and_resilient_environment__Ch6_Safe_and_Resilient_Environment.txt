6.1	Background
As a coastal community with a substantial wild-
land interface, floodplains, sloping terrain, farm-
land, and a variety of microclimates, Oceanside is 
exposed to a wide range of natural hazards that 
threaten public safety and the built environment. 
Additionally, as is true of any local jurisdiction, 
the Oceanside community is vulnerable to vari-
ous human-induced threats, including the release 
of hazardous materials, civil unrest, and crime. 
The Safe and Resilient Environment Element 
(SRE) assesses the City’s exposure to natural and 
human-induced hazards and outlines goals, pol-
icies, and actions designed to mitigate the risk 
of injury and property damage associated with 
these hazards.  
Wildfire is a relatively common occurrence in 
Oceanside, with the 2017 Lilac Fire being the 
most significant wildfire in recent history. The 
City’s highly eroded coastline is susceptible to 
wave uprush and inundation during king tides 
and high surf events; many beachfront homes 
and public facilities have been damaged by wave 
uprush and inundation in recent years. Properties 
adjacent to Loma Alta Creek experience flooding 
during major rain events, as do segments of State 
Route 78 in proximity to Buena Vista Creek. The 
segment of Coast Highway that crosses Buena 
Vista Lagoon can become impassable during 
major rain events. Droughts have threatened 
agricultural operations in the South Morro Hills 
area, where farmers struggle with the high cost 
of water. Landslides have damaged and destroyed 
single-family homes in Rancho del Oro and other 
neighborhoods. In short, much of Oceanside 
is vulnerable to natural hazards, with the City’s 
coastline, northern and eastern boundaries, hill-
sides, and areas near Loma Alta Creek and Buena 
Vista Creek and Lagoon being particularly at risk. 
Given that many of the physical conditions that 
present risk to life and property also contribute 
to community character, economic development, 
and quality of life, the SRE seeks to strike a bal-
ance between containment of natural hazards 
and preservation of natural features that provide 
ecological balance, recreational opportunities, a 
sense of openness, and a unique visual character. 
A fundamental goal of the SRE is to protect life 
and property without compromising the City’s 
ecological, recreational, and aesthetic assets.  
Oceanside’s location, climate, vegetation, and 
development patterns render the City vulnerable 
to riverine flooding, coastal storm and tidal events 
(exacerbated by sea level rise), wildfire, urban fire, 
and geologic and seismic hazards. Substantial

In the face of climate change and increasing risk 
of flooding, coastal storms and extreme tides, 
urban heat, fire, and geologic hazards, Oceanside 
is required by State law to develop General Plan 
goals and policies that serve to minimize its vul-
nerability to these phenomena and ensure ade-
quate emergency response in the event of natural 
disaster. A resilient community is one that is both 
proactive in identifying and warding off natural 
hazards and prepared to address the impacts 
of before has put in place both preventive and 
responsive measures. Although many of these 
hazards affect Oceanside at a citywide scale, the 
impacts of climate change and associated envi-
ronmental and public safety hazards may be more 
profound in certain areas, raising questions about 
environmental justice. While the most vulnera-
ble properties in Oceanside tend to be situated 
in higher-income neighborhoods (e.g., beach-
front, estate residential near the City’s northern 
and eastern boundaries), there are less affluent 
populations in the North Valley and Capistrano 
Neighborhood Planning Areas that face the 
threat of wildfire with limited evacuation routes. 
There is also farmworker housing in South Morro 
Hills in close proximity to a State-designated high 
fire hazard severity zone. In light of threats facing 
low-resource communities in Oceanside, the SRE 
includes goals and policies that call upon the City 
to view natural hazards through the lens of envi-
ronmental justice.

STORM-INDUCED FLOODING 
Flooding is the inundation of normally dry land 
as a result of a rise in the level of surface waters 
or the rapid accumulation of stormwater runoff; 
it becomes a hazard when the flow of water has 
the potential to damage property and threaten 
human life or health. Substantial portions of the 
Planning Area, including the immediate coastal 
boundary and areas within historic floodplains, 
can be affected by flooding, particularly during 
the winter rainy season. The City encompasses 
approximately 42 square miles and is divided into 
four major watersheds (San Luis Rey, Loma Alta, 
Buena Vista, and Agua Hedionda). Most of the 
Oceanside drains to waterways that flow to the 
Pacific Ocean.4 Consequently, the City is exposed 
to both riverine and coastal flooding, particular-
ly when major rain events coincide with storm 
surge, large swells, and extreme high tides.
Riverine flooding occurs when streams and rivers 
exceed the capacity of their channels to accom-
modate flow and crest their banks, spilling out 
into adjacent land. A levee system along the 
San Luis Rey River, installed by the Army Corps 
of Engineers in the early 1990s, has significant-
ly reduced flood risk in the San Luis Rey Valley. 
Currently, the most flood-prone waterway in the 
city is Loma Alta Creek, which has been hemmed 
in by development and expansion of the railroad 
that was constructed along the Loma Alta valley in 
the early 1900s. Rainfall is the main contributing

factor to flooding, thought the impact of rain-
fall is contingent upon soil moisture conditions 
before the storm, the extent of urban develop-

Coastal flooding is usually the result of a large 
storm and/or high tides. Floodwaters are driven 
ashore by high winds, an event known as storm 
surge. High velocity wave action and extreme 
tides can cause significant damage to coast-
al development and infrastructure. With a 
west-facing coastline exposed to the open ocean, 
the City experiences large swells throughout the 
year and extreme tides in early summer and early 
winter. Many beachfront residential properties 
in Oceanside experience regular overtopping of 
shoreline protective devices, which erodes pri-
vate and public beach areas, undermines hard-
scape, and occasionally pushes seawater into 
inland areas.
Flood Hazard Zones 
According to the County’s MJHMP, FEMA FIRM 
data was used to determine hazard risk for floods 
in the county. FEMA defines flood risk primari-
ly by a 100-year flood zone, which is applied to 
those areas with a 1 percent chance, on average, 
of flooding in any given year. Any area that lies 
within the FEMA-designated 100-year floodplain 
is designated as high risk. Any area found in the 
500-year floodplain is designated at low risk. 
Base flood elevations (BFE) were also used in the 
flood hazard modeling process. A BFE is the ele-
vation of the water surface resulting from a flood 
that has a 1 percent chance of occurring in any 
given year (i.e., the height of the base flood).
Based on FEMA Records, the San Diego region 
has not suffered severe repetitive loss (residen-
tial properties that have at least four NFIP pay-
ments over $5,000 each with the cumulative 
claim exceeding $20,000 or at least two separate 
claims payments with the cumulative amount 
exceeding the market value of the building) since 
1974. However, in Oceanside, there have been 15 
repetitive losses (losses of at least $1,000 each). 
Based on the historical record, the likelihood of 
flooding in the city is highly likely.

TSUNAMI AND SEICHE 
A tsunami is a series of high-energy waves that 
radiate outward like pond ripples from an area 
where a generating event occurs, arriving at 
shorelines over an extended period. Tsunamis 
can be induced by earthquakes, landslides, and 
submarine volcanic explosions. According to the 
County’s 2017 MJHMP, the impact of tsunamis on 
the San Diego coastline has been low, but inunda-
tion maps developed by the California Office of 
Emergency Services and the California Geologic 
Survey show the potential for moderate damage 
along low-lying areas. The largest tsunami effect 
recorded in San Diego since 1950 was May 22, 
1960, when an earthquake off the coast of Chile 
generated maximum wave heights of 2.1 feet. In 
this event, 80 meters of dock were destroyed, and 
a barge sunk in Quivera Basin.6 
According to mapping produced in the MJHMP, 
the highest risk zones in San Diego County are 
located within the coastal zone. The entirety of 
Oceanside’s coastline is designated as a VE Zone, 
which is defined by FEMA as the coastal area sub-
ject to a velocity hazard (wave action). Further, 
maximum tsunami runup is projected to extend 
up the San Luis Rey River to the vicinity of the 
Oceanside Municipal Airport, landward of Coast 
Highway where this roadway crosses Loma Alta 
Creek and Buena Vista Lagoon. In the City’s coast-
al area, a total of 790 residents as well as 1,265 
residential and 198 commercial buildings could be 
exposed to a tsunami.7 This map, prepared by the 
Bureau of Land Management, shows areas within 
the City that would be impacted by maximum tsu-
nami runup.
The greatest type of tsunami risk in San Diego 
County is material damage to small watercraft, 
harbors, and some waterfront structures, with 
flooding along the coast. While the likelihood of 
large-scale devastation in Oceanside resulting

DAM FAILURE AND INUNDATION
Many dam failures in the United States have 
been secondary results of other disasters. The 
most common causes are earthquakes, land-
slides, extreme storms, equipment malfunction, 
structural damage, foundation failures, and sab-
otage. Poor construction, lack of maintenance 
and repair, and deficient operational procedures 
are preventable or correctable by a program of 
regular inspections. Terrorism and vandalism are 
serious concerns for which all operators of public 
facilities must plan. Given the evolving nature of 
such threats, they are under continuous review 
by public safety agencies. 
In San Diego County, most dams are greater than 
50 years old and are characterized by increased 
hazard potential due to downstream develop-
ment and increased risk due to structural deteri-
oration and inadequate spillway capacity.10
The California Department of Water Resources’ 
Division of Safety of Dams reviews and approves 
inundation maps for extremely high, high, and sig-
nificant hazard dams. As detailed in the County’s 
MJHMP, there are no dams in Oceanside that 
present a significant downstream hazard risk.

east base of Mount Palomar and at the head-

SEA LEVEL RISE
Oceanside is bordered to the west by three miles 
of Pacific Ocean coastline. While the coast is an 
important biological, cultural, recreational, and 
economic resource, it also poses an environ-
mental risk to residents, visitors, and properties 
located near the waterfront. Sea level rise (SLR), 
the rise in global sea level accompanying other 
effects of global climate change, has already 
increased water levels measured at a station in La 
Jolla at a rate of six inches over the last century.12 
As sea levels rise further due to global warming, it 
will increase the flooding hazard from the ocean, 
especially during storm events. 
Both private property and public facilities on the 
City’s beachfront experience wave uprush and 
inundation on a regular basis, particularly during 
high surf and king tide events. While most of these 
impacts have thus far been minor and temporary, 
some properties and facilities have sustained sig-
nificant damage. Additionally, sinkholes, under-
mined beach access ramps, and unstable revet-
ment have created public safety hazards.
The County’s MJHMP projects up to two feet of 
sea level rise by mid-century. For five feet of SLR 
(Figure 6-3), which represents an intermediate 
to high end-of-century SLR scenario,13 the City’s 
exposure to 100-year coastal flooding is signifi-
cantly greater relative to current conditions, with 
the beachfront and estuarine areas being partic-
ularly vulnerable. In 2019, the City prepared a 
Coastal Hazards Vulnerability Assessment (VA), 
which illustrates how coastal properties and 
assets would be impacted under various sea level 
rise scenarios. Existing shoreline protective devic-
es along the beachfront may not be adequate to 
withstand an additional two feet of sea-level rise; 
without enhanced protection, many beachfront 
properties and assets could become increasingly 
vulnerable to frequent wave uprush and potential 
damage.

Recent studies suggest a narrower range of pro-

The 2022 National Oceanic and Atmospheric 
Administration (NOAA) report, Global and

extreme water level probabilities along U.S coast-
lines.14 According to the report, relative sea level 
along the contiguous U.S. (CONUS) coastline is 
expected to rise on average of 0.8-1.0 feet over 
the next 30 years (2020–2050). The 2100 scenar-

CONUS coastline are about 2.0–7.2 feet. With 
increasingly accurate sea level rise projection 
scenarios, local jurisdictions can further devel-

Sea level rise will continue to exacerbate impacts 
associated other events that temporarily increase 
sea level – e.g., high astronomical tides, strong 
winds, high surf, storm surge, and thermoexpan-
sion, and thermo-expansion created by phenom-
ena like El Nino. San Diego County’s shoreline will 
see increasingly more hours of high sea levels as 
the century progresses. High sea level events are 
defined as the 99.99th percentile hourly sea level 
calculated for the period of 1970-1999. These 
high sea levels are caused by factors including 
high astronomical tides, wind, waves, and storm 
surges. In the present climate, San Diego expe-
riences an average of one hour of high sea level 
per year. By 2030, high sea levels are expected to 
occur 12 hours per year on average. By mid-cen-
tury, this increases to 62 hours per year. These 
high sea levels put natural ecosystems (beaches, 
cliffs, wetlands) and development at risk of expo-
sure to flooding and wave action.15

SOCIAL VULNERABILITY 
Flood hazards, dam breach inundation areas, 
and sea level rise impacts in the City are gen-
erally concentrated within the coastal and riv-
erine areas. As illustrated in maps included in 
Oceanside General Plan Update Background 
Report #2, lower-income areas may be particu-
larly affected by flooding hazards. The Townsite 
neighborhood in Oceanside contains a high-pov-
erty census tract, parts of which may be at risk of 
damage from coastal flooding and sea level rise. 
Mobile home parks adjacent to Loma Alta Creek, 
which accommodate many lower-income house-
holds, are vulnerable to both coastal and riverine 
flooding. Two recreational vehicle parks adjacent 
to Loma Alta Creek, both of which qualify as low-
cost visitor-serving accommodations, also face 
significant flood risk.
Vulnerable populations (e.g., poor, elderly, chil-
dren, and unhoused individuals) may also live in 
areas that are more prone to flooding and envi-
ronmental pollution. Flooding can compromise 
existing infrastructure, health services, and 
contaminate inundated areas with hazardous 
substances which leads to exacerbated health 
impacts on vulnerable groups. Further, unhoused 
populations may lack a secure place to shelter 
during a flood, and they are often more difficult 
to reach via emergency alert systems.16 
Goals and policies developed by the City will work 
to mitigate flooding and sea level rise hazards, 
while prioritizing adaptation for socially-vulnera-
ble groups through the development of local poli-
cies and programs consistent with state guidance. 
Such policies will foster community and regional 
engagement for sea level rise planning. Coupled 
with policies on future sea level rise monitoring, 
findings from these engagement efforts will help 
the City establish planning thresholds for new 
development, sea level rise adaptation strate-
gies, and other coastal adaptation measures. As 
for existing policies and protocols, climate adap-
tation strategies called out in Oceanside’s sec-
tion of the MJHMP include the development of 
an audio warning system to notify residents of 
flooding and the identification of financial assis-
tance programs to fund flood mitigation projects.

FRAMEWORK 
FEMA is responsible for managing the 100-year 
floodplain, areas with a one percent or greater 
chance of flooding in any given year. A Flood 
Insurance Rate Map, an official FEMA-prepared 
map, delineates both Special Flood Hazard Areas 
(the 100-year floodplain) and flood-risk premium 
zones (as shown in Figure 6-3). FEMA adminis-
ters the National Flood Insurance Program, which 
includes floodplain management and flood hazard 
mapping and provides subsidized flood insurance 
to communities that comply with FEMA regula-
tions to limit development in floodplains. Under 
Executive Order 11988, FEMA requires local 
governments covered by the National Flood 
Insurance Program to pass and enforce a flood-
plain management ordinance that specifies min-
imum requirements for any construction within 
the 100-year floodplain. 
Current state guidance calls for preparing for 
at least 3.5 feet of sea level rise by 2050.17 To 
prepare for this extent of sea level rise, adapta-
tion strategies are incorporated into the goals 
and policies of the SRE. The City has begun to 
address how its shoreline can be rendered more 
resilient to sea level rise in the future. The City

RESPONSIBLE AGENCIES 
There are several agencies responsible for flood 
management and emergency response in the San 
Diego region. The SRE emphasizes interagency 
coordination and planning efforts between the 
City of Oceanside and the following agencies to 
best mitigate and adapt to flooding and sea level 
rise. 
The California Department of Water Resources 
engages in flood management and flood emer-
gency response programs. It developed the Flood 
Emergency Response Information Exchange 
to improve flood emergency preparedness, 
response, and recovery. 
San Diego Regional Water Quality Control 
Board. The San Diego Regional Water Quality 
Control Board enforces waterway protection 
and pollution control regulations in Oceanside. 
The San Diego Water Board regulates discharg-
es from Phase I municipal separate storm sewer 
systems (MS4s / City-owned storm drains) in 
the San Diego Region under the Regional MS4 
Permit. The permit has provisions that requires 
municipalities to implement low impact devel-
opment hydrology designs for projects within its 
land use planning authority. 
The San Diego County Flood Control District 
(FCD) deals with watershed issues in the 
Unincorporated County of San Diego, focusing 
primarily on flood control issues. The FCD is to 
provide for the control of the flood and storm 
waters of the district, and of the flood and storm 
waters that flow into the district. 
The Oceanside Fire Department (OFD) is the 
primary agency responsible for responding to, 
recovering from, and mitigating against any 
hazard that affects the City of Oceanside. OFD’s 
mission is to meet and exceed community needs 
and expectations through the preservation and 
protection of life, property, and the environment.

6.3	Fire Hazards
WILDFIRE/WILDFIRE URBAN INTERFACE
Wildfire has become an increasing occurrence in 
California. A wildfire is any uncontrolled fire on 
undeveloped land that requires fire suppression. 
According to the County’s MJHMP, wildfires are 
common in San Diego County, with large historic 
wildfires recorded in 2003 and 2007. In October 
2003, the Cedar Fire caused 17 deaths and 
destroyed 332,766 acres of land, 3,239 structures, 
and cost approximately 450 million dollars. San 
Diego County’s worst wildfire complex occurred 
in October 2007, known as the Witch Creek and 
Guejito Fires. At the height of the firestorm there 
were seven fires burning within the county. The 
fires destroyed 369,000 acres (13 percent of the 
County), 2,670 structures, 239 vehicles, and two 
commercial properties. There were 10 civilian 
deaths, 23 civilian injuries, and 10 firefighter inju-
ries with the cost of the fire exceeding 1.5 billion 
dollars. In total, wildfires have prompted nine 
Proclaimed States of Emergency in the County of 
San Diego between 1950-2014.  
Recent wildfires in San Diego County have also 
resulted in significant structure loss. The May 
2014 San Diego County Wildfires resulted in 
26,000 acres burned and 65 structures destroyed. 
In June 2016, the Border Fire resulted in 7,609 
acres burned and two deaths. In December 2017, 
the Lilac Fire left 4,100 acres burned and 157 
structures destroyed. In September 2020, the 
Valley Fire resulted in 16,390 acres burned and 
66 structures destroyed. In May 2022, a brush 
fire burned nearly 660 acres of vegetation at 
Camp Pendleton, which is located just north of 
Oceanside. In addition to direct impacts, major 
wildfires that occur outside Oceanside still have 
profound effects on economies, health, and eco-
system function throughout the region.  
State law requires the California Department 
of Forestry and Fire Protection (CAL FIRE), to 
identify areas, or zones, of very high fire hazard 
severity potential under the Fire and Resources 
Assessment Program (FRAP). These Fire Hazard 
Severity Zones (FHSZ) are mapped and identified 
based on expected burn probabilities, potential 
fuels over a 30- to 50-year time period, and their 
correlated expected fire behavior, in order to 
better predict possible vegetation fire exposure 
to buildings and developments. According to 
FRAP mapping shown in Figure 6-4, the north-
west portion of Oceanside along the San Luis Rey 
River, including the airport and just north of it, 
is designated by CAL FIRE as a Very High Fire 
Hazard Severity Zone (VHFHSZ). In addition, a

Urban Fire
An urban fire is a fire that can rapidly spread to 
adjoining structures and damage or destroy large 
commercial buildings, apartment complexes, and 
other residential or commercial facilities. Urban 
fires can also spread beyond the built environ-
ment and impact adjacent natural areas. As 
described above, much of the fire risk the City 
faces stems from the proximity of urban areas 
– primarily residential neighborhoods – to areas 
with high fire risk due to steep slopes, vegetation 
that can act as fuel for fires, and seasonal winds 
which can spread fire. The primary factors affect-
ing the risk of structural fire are the age and con-
dition of the building or structure, its proximity to 
other structures, and the methods and materials 
used in its construction. 
Urban fires usually result from sources within 
the structures themselves. Smoking in bed, 
faulty wiring, errant matches, and appliance mal-
functions are often causes of structural fires. 
Additionally, cinders from wood-burning fire-
places that remain alive and travel considerable 
distances have also been blamed for starting fires 
near residential locations. Therefore, urban fires 
can generally be mitigated through proper build-
ing code requirements, fire flow minimums, and 
zoning or subdivision ordinance requirements.
SOCIAL VULNERABILITY 
Oceanside is located in a semi-arid climate where 
wildfires and high temperatures are common, 
and such hazards are felt most acutely by front-
line communities. Neighborhoods that are more 
linguistically isolated are more likely to be at 
significant risk of wildfires. Linguistically isolat-
ed households (residents who do not speak or 
read English as a first language or at all) may not 
hear or understand important information when 
there is a fire or other emergency. According to 
American Community Survey estimates from 
2018, areas along the norther border of the city 
in close proximity to the WUI interface—including 
the South Morro Hills, North Valley, and East Side 
Capistrano neighborhoods—are more linguistical-
ly isolated than other areas of the city. Another 
population vulnerable to fire hazards are the 
City’s unhoused residents, many of whom live in 
isolated encampments in the San Luis Rey River.
Uncontrolled wildland fires do also have the risk of 
spreading beyond the very high and high vulner-
ability areas into urban areas, which could impact 
socially vulnerable populations. Additionally, the 
entire city will be impacted by wildfires occurring 
throughout the metropolitan region via impacts 
on air/wildfire smoke, water, and soil quality; 
damage to energy infrastructure and roads; and 
strain on local firefighting resources as the fire 
department is called to respond to fires across 
the region and State. Unhoused populations, out-
door workers, residents who live in poorly insu-
lated or ventilated homes, and people who are 
already burdened by elevated local (indoor/out-
door) pollution are increasingly at risk due to the 
consequences of climate change that have exac-
erbated the now-annual “smoke season.” Goals 
and policies developed by the City will work to 
mitigate fire hazards while prioritizing adaptation 
for socially vulnerable groups through compli-
ance with state laws, fire safe development regu-
lations, and local policies and programs.

INSTITUTIONAL AND REGULATORY 
FRAMEWORK 
Fire Safe Development Codes 
The California Fire Code (Title 24, Part 9 of the 
California Code) establishes regulations to pro-
tect life and property from the hazards of fires 
in new and existing buildings and structures. The 
provisions of the Fire Code apply to the construc-
tion, alteration, movement, enlargement, replace-
ment, repair, equipment, use and occupancy, 
location, maintenance, removal, and demolition 
of buildings or structures throughout California. 
Public Resources Code 4291 includes regulations 
and defensible space requirements for areas 
located in SRAs. The City adopted and amended 
the 2022 California Fire Code and regulates fire 
safety in Chapter 11 of the Oceanside Code of 
Ordinances (COD). In addition, Chapter 17 of the 
Oceanside COD establishes that any tree, plant, 
vine, or foliage that constitutes a danger to public 
safety by creating a fire hazard shall be consid-
ered a nuisance and must be abated.
Peakload Water Supply Requirement
The California Fire Code (Fire Code) stipulates 
fire-flow requirements for buildings.19 Fire-flow 
is the rate of a water supply, measured at 20 
pounds per square inch (psi) (138 kPa) residual 
pressure, that is available for firefighting. Fire-
flow minimums vary depending on building use, 
building size, and if a sprinkler system is provided. 
Water mains serving one- and two-family dwell-
ings, townhouses, and group homes must provide 
a fire-flow of 1,000 gallons per minute (gpm) for 
buildings without an automatic sprinkler system, 
or 500 gpm for buildings with fire protection 
devices such as automatic sprinkler systems. 
The required fire-flow standard for commercial, 
industrial, manufacturing, and large apartment 
buildings varies from 1,500 to 8,000 gpm based 
on the type of construction, type of use, and any 
built-in fire protection devices. School buildings 
are within Appendix BB of the Fire Code and 
range between 1,500 to 8,000 gpm depending 
on type and square footage.

According to the City of Oceanside’s 2020 Urban 
Water Management Plan (UWMP), approximate-

from the San Diego County Water Authority 
(SDCWA) in calendar year 2020. SDCWA sup-

the State Water Project and Colorado River 
Aqueduct as well as desalinated seawater from 
the Claude “Bud” Lewis Carlsbad Desalination 
Plant. Approximately 10 percent of the City’s 
water comes from the Mission Basin, a ground-
water sub-basin that stores water classified as 
subsurface flows from the San Luis Rey River. 
The City also reclaims wastewater at the San 
Luis Rey Water Reclamation Facility (SLRWRF); 
this non-potable recycled water comprises 
approximately one percent of total water use. 
In addition, the City of Oceanside’s Pure Water 
Oceanside Project is the first advanced water 
treatment facility to become operational in the 
San Diego region and will purify recycled water 
to create a new, local source of drinking water. 
The City plans to produce 4.5 million gallons per 
day, enough to provide about 30 percent of their 
water supply. Under a second phase to the plan, 
the facility will expand to produce up to 6 million 
gallons per day, with non-potable recycled water 
anticipated to comprise approximately 24 per-

As detailed in the UWMP, the City’s water supply 
reliability analysis shows that with implementa-
tion of additional planned supplies and conserva-
tion measures, supplies will meet demand under 
all hydrologic scenarios, including the normal 
year, single dry year, and five consecutive dry 
years from 2025-2045. Concurrently with the 
2020 UWMP, the City developed the 2021 Water 
Conservation Master Plan Update (2021 WCMP 
Update). As part of this effort, the City project-
ed water use, passive conservation, and active 
conservation into the future. Moving forward, 
the City assumes implementation of aggressive 
water conservation, smart meters, and further 
implementation of recycled water conversions. 
Such conservation measures will allow the City 
to maintain an adequate water supply for fire 
suppression needs.

Minimum Road Widths and 
Clearances Around Structures 
Clear emergency vehicle access to buildings is 
very important in the event of a fire or seismic 
event. Such access is regulated by the adopted 
and amended Fire Code. Under the current Fire 
Code, all portions of a building shall be within 150 
feet of a serviceable fire access road. Fire appa-
ratus access roads shall have an unobstructed 
improved curb-face to curb-face minimum width 
of 28 feet. 
Creating defensible space, or the clearance 
around structures, is another mechanism for 
improving a building’s chance of surviving a 
wildfire by limiting combustible materials and 
vegetation up to a 100 feet radius around the 
structure. The Fire Code requires buildings and 
structures within areas designated as a Very High 
Fire Hazard Severity Zone to maintain defensible 
space. In the adopted and amended Fire Code, 
combustible vegetation within the array and to a 
distance of 30 feet from the array and associated 
equipment shall be reduced to a height of no more 
than six inches. Further, any vegetation growing 
upon private or public property within the City 
of Oceanside which will with reasonable proba-
bility constitute a fire hazard, shall be abated to 
the satisfaction of the fire chief or his authorized 
representative. To align with the City’s objective 
of preserving and enhancing green infrastructure 
networks, it is important that the provision of 
defensible space does not result in the expan-
sion of impervious surface area. Property owners 
should integrate defensible space strategies with 
green infrastructure as much as possible.  
Accordingly, the City also plans to adopt local 
building ordinances which improve building stan-
dards in urban/wildland interface (UWI) areas 
including 
defensible 
space, 
noncombustible 
fencing, boxed eaves, extruded metal window 
frames, Class-A non-combustible roofs and exte-
rior wall coverings, and protected attic venting. 
In addition, the City will coordinate with federal, 
state and local wildlife agency representatives to 
develop regulations regarding brush management 
within sensitive areas and incorporate the regula-
tions policies in the Vital & Sustainable Resources 
Element of the General Plan. 
RESPONSIBLE AGENCIES 
There are several agencies responsible for fire 
prevention and emergency response in the San 
Diego region. The SRE emphasizes interagency 
coordination and planning efforts between the 
City of Oceanside and the following agencies to 
best mitigate and adapt to urban fire and wildfire. 
The U.S. Department of Defense’s Defense 
Community Infrastructure Pilot Program (DCIP) 
is designed to address deficiencies in community 
infrastructure, supportive of a military installa-
tion, in order to enhance military value, installa-
tion resilience, and military family quality of life.
The California Department of Forestry and Fire 
Protection (CAL FIRE) manages fire prevention 
and response for the State of California. CAL 
FIRE oversees enforcement of California’s forest 
protection regulations, implements fuel manage-
ment projects, participates in forest conserva-
tion and management, and provides training and 
educational programs. CAL FIRE also engages in 
general emergency response activities. 
The Oceanside Fire Department (OFD) is the 
primary emergency response service provider for 
the City of Oceanside, and provides comprehen-
sive strategies and training in fire prevention, fire 
suppression, emergency medical services, all risk 
mitigation, emergency preparedness, 911 ser-
vices, and community-based fire services. OFD 
consists of eight firehouses, 115 full-time fire 
personnel, and 34 full and part-time emergency 
medical technicians.
FIRE STATION 1
In September 2021, the City of Oceanside was 
awarded over $3,500,000 in grant funding from 
DCIP to go toward building a new Fire Station 
1. The current Fire Station 1, located at 714 Pier 
View Way, was built in 1929 and does not meet 
current building code requirements nor can it fit 
more staff and equipment. The new fire station 
will allow for three additional apparatus bays, 
a fire engine, a fire truck ladder company, an 
ambulance and a battalion chief. The new Fire 
Station 1 will be located at 602 Civic Center 
Drive, which will improve response times to fire 
and other emergencies at Camp Pendleton.

TOPOGRAPHY AND SOILS
Oceanside is located in northwestern San Diego 
County within the coastal sub-province of 
the Peninsular Ranges Geomorphic Province. 
Geological formations underlying the Planning 
Area are largely composed of sedimentary rocks, 
identified as coarse-grained, with alluvial forma-
tions along and fanning out from the San Luis Rey 
River, Loma Alta Creek, and Buena Vista Creek 
beds. Alluvial deposits are defined as materi-
al deposited by rivers consisting of silt, sand, 
clay, and gravel, as well as much organic matter. 
Alluvial deposits are usually most extensive in the

Landslide deposits are located in areas adjacent 
to the alluvial planes. These are areas where 
unsorted gravel, sand, and clay loosely deposited

The topography throughout the Planning Area can 
be characterized by three physiographic regions. 
The first is the low-lying coastal area along the 
Pacific Ocean. The next region is comprised of 
the City’s three principal waterways, and the third 
includes the foothills and higher elevations located 
along the eastern boundary of the City. Planning 
Area elevations range from zero along the coast to 
940 feet above sea level, inland.21
Soil types in the Planning Area primarily include 
sandy loam, loamy sand, clay, and loam. Sandy

TYPES OF SEISMIC HAZARDS
Surface rupture occurs when the ground surface 
is broken due to fault movement during an earth-
quake, and usually occurs along an active or poten-
tially active fault trace. AAA very strong earth-
quake struck San Diego on May 27, 1862, damaging 
buildings in Old Town and opening up cracks in 
the earth near the San Diego River mouth. This 
destructive earthquake was centered on either the 
Rose Canyon or Coronado Bank fault. Descriptions 
of damage suggest that it had a magnitude of about 
6.0 (M6).23
Ground shaking generally refers to all aspects 
of motion of the earth’s surface resulting from 
an earthquake and is often the primary cause of 
damage from seismic events. The extent of ground 
shaking depends on the magnitude and intensity 
of the earthquake, distance from the rupture (e.g., 
epicenter), and local geologic conditions. Intensity 
is a subjective measure of the perceptible effects 
of seismic energy at a given point, commonly mea-
sured by the Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale 
(MMI). This scale reports the intensity of shaking 
on a 10-tier scale from “not felt except by a few 
under especially favorable circumstances” (I, or 
light) to “rails bent greatly/underground pipelines

Magnitude is an objective measure of an earth-

Liquefaction occurs when loose, saturated, and 
granular soil, usually found near the ground sur-

lose strength and its ability to support structures. 
Additionally, liquefied soil exerts higher pressure

Creek, and Buena Vista Creek.
Land subsidence is a gradual settling or sudden

GEOLOGIC AND SEISMIC HAZARDS 
There are no known active or potentially active 
faults within the City. The nearest faults are the 
Elsinore Fault to the east and the Rose Canyon 
Fault to the south, which traverses the Pacific 
Ocean in a north-northeasterly direction. The 
Rose Canyon Fault is considered to be the great-
est potential threat to San Diego as a region, due 
to its proximity to areas of high population.27 
There is no potential for ground rupture due to 
the lack of any active faults. However, like most of 
Southern California, the community is considered 
seismically active and susceptible to earthquakes 
resulting from fault movement. The Working 
Group on California Earthquake Probabilities 
has estimated that Southern California has a 75 
percent chance of experiencing an earthquake of 
magnitude 7.0 or higher over the next 30 years, 
with the Rose Canyon Fault and the Elsinore and 
San Jacinto Faults being the most likely to cause 
such an event.

The degree of damage caused by an earthquake 
depends on an area’s geologic composition, 
the number of people and the designated land 
uses, the prevailing construction standards, the 
efficiency of the emergency-response system and 
even the time of day and day of the week. Most 
of Oceanside’s geologic and seismic threat comes 
from impacts to city utility lifelines, quake-relat-
ed fire, landslide, or flood, and impacts to struc-
tures or buildings, which can include partial or 
total collapse. The potential for structural haz-
ards largely depends on the structure’s design 
and construction type. While modern building 
codes require seismic study, safety measures, 
and earthquake-resistant design to help protect 
communities against structural hazards, many 
buildings in Oceanside may have been built 
before these codes were in place. To address this, 
the City is working to apply the State building 
code’s seismic retrofitting standards to existing 
buildings to ensure their safety.
While it is not possible to prevent earthquakes, 
the loss and damage resulting from their impacts 
can be minimized through proper design and 
construction of structures and land-use devel-
opment. Geological investigations and greater 
oversight of engineering practices and construc-
tion techniques will continue to remain essential 
elements of the development process, and the 
safety of existing buildings must be upgraded 
whenever possible.

SOCIAL VULNERABILITY 
Liquefaction and landslide risk is ubiquitous in 
low-lying coastal areas of the City adjacent to 
waterways. This risk is also present on slopes 
and mesas, including those that line the Mission 
Avenue and Oceanside Boulevard corridors. 
There are landslide-susceptible portions of the 
Loma Alta Neighborhood Planning Area where 
the majority of residents are low-income. There 
are also high greater percentages of renters 
facing two or more housing issues (e.g., lack of 
complete plumbing facilities, lack of complete 
kitchen facilities, more than one occupant per 
room, and housing costs greater than 30 percent 
of household income) residing along the San Luis 
Rey River in the East Side/Capistrano, Airport, 
and Guajome NPAs. These areas are all designat-
ed as liquefaction zones. Due to the large-scale 
nature of seismic events, however, the entirety 
of Oceanside is generally at risk of geologic haz-
ards. Goals and policies developed by the City 
will work to mitigate these geologic and seismic 
hazards while prioritizing adaptation for socially 
vulnerable groups through compliance with state 
laws, geotechnical regulations, and local policies 
and programs. 
INSTITUTIONAL AND REGULATORY 
FRAMEWORK 
Following the devastation of the San Fernando 
earthquake in 1971 (magnitude 6.6), California 
created the Alquist-Priolo Act to reduce damage 
and losses from surface fault rupture. This act 
established regulatory zones surrounding the sur-
face traces of active faults so that a structure for 
human occupancy cannot be placed or built on 
active faults with potential for surface rupture 
and must be sited at a minimum distance from the 
fault.28 
28 Alquist-Priolo Earthquake Fault Zones, 2019, California 
Department of Conservation (https://www.conservation. 
ca.gov/cgs/alquist-priolo) 
Established in 1990, Seismic Hazards Mapping Act 
(SHMA) directs the Department of Conservation, 
California Geological Survey to identify and map 
areas prone to earthquake-induced liquefaction, 
landslides, and amplified ground shaking. The 
purpose of the SHMA is to reduce the threat 
to public safety and to minimize the loss of life 
and property by identifying and mitigating these 
seismic hazards. The SHMA requires the State 
Geologist to establish regulatory zones and to 
issue appropriate seismic hazard zone maps to all 
affected cities, counties, and state agencies for 
their use in planning and controlling construction 
and development.29
While an earthquake fault zone generally prohib-
its location of structures for human occupancy 
within 50 feet of a trace of an active fault line, 
the State may grant exceptions for development 
within an earthquake fault zone if a geologic 
investigation approved by the State Geologist 
shows that the structure is not situated upon a 
trace of an active fault line.31 There are no active 
earthquake fault zones located in Oceanside (i.e., 
triggering seismic activity during the Holocene 
period). The nearest active fault zone is the 
Rose Canyon Fault Zone, which lies roughly five 
miles off the City’s coastline. This fault zone is 
an extension of the Newport-Inglewood Fault 
Zone. A 2017 study concluded that, together, 
the Newport–Inglewood Fault and Rose Canyon 
Fault could produce an earthquake of 7.3 to 7.4 
magnitude. The nearest onshore fault zone is 
the Elsinore Fault Zone, located about 20 miles 
east of the City. It is estimated that this fault 
zone is capable of producing a quake of 6.5 to 7.5 
magnitude.

California Building Code (adopted and amended 
as Chapter 6 of the Oceanside Municipal Code) 
establishes minimum standards for all develop-
ment projects intended to protect life safety. For 
example, Article XI establishes rules, regulations, 
technical guidelines, and specifications for pur-
poses of seismic strengthening of unreinforced 
masonry buildings.

6.5	Climate Change, 
Adaptation, and 
Resiliency 
The impacts of climate change are readily appar-
ent in San Diego County, which has seen exten-
sive wildfires in recent years, including in areas 
close to Oceanside. Drought and flooding are 
becoming more frequent in the county, as they are 
throughout California. Combating climate change 
and its effects is a top priority for state govern-
ment, and certain mitigation actions can only be 
undertaken at the state or national levels. There 
are various state laws and Executive Orders that 
aim to address climate change. Passed in 2006, 
AB 32 directs the California Air Resources Board 
(CARB) to develop and implement a scoping plan 
and regulations to meet GHG State targets. In 
addition, the Governor’s 2018 Executive Order 
B-55-18 established a statewide goal to achieve 
carbon neutrality as soon as possible, and no 
later than 2045, and to achieve and maintain net 
negative emissions thereafter. These goals within 
the Governor’s order were formalized in the leg-
islature’s AB 1279, which go beyond both the 
AB 32 and subsequent SB 32 statewide green-
house gas reduction goals. The recently updated 
2022 Scoping Plan lays out more strict targets to 
achieve carbon neutrality by mid-century. 
The California Climate Adaptation Strategy (the 
Strategy), mandated by 2015 AB 1482, links 
together the state’s existing and planned climate 
adaptation efforts and outlines the state’s six cli-
mate resilience priorities, as well as measurable 
metrics for each priority. The priorities identified 
in the strategy are as follows:
1.	 Strengthen Protections for Climate 
Vulnerable Communities
2.	 Bolster Public Health and Safety to Protect 
Against Increasing Climate Risks
3.	 Build a Climate Resilient Economy
4.	 Accelerate Nature-Based Climate Solutions 
and Strengthen Climate Resilience of Natural 
Systems
5.	 Make Decisions Based on the Best Available 
Climate Science
6.	 Partner and Collaborate to Leverage 
Resources
While the priorities are identified as state-level 
objectives, each can be furthered through plan-
ning and implementation at the local level, and all 
six priorities are addressed to some extent in this 
General Plan.
Adopting this element is intended to reflect a clear 
commitment from the City towards taking the nec-
essary steps to advance climate adaptation and 
resiliency strategies at the local level. While the 
General Plan outlines broad goals and strategies, 
the City has developed more detailed greenhouse 
gas reduction and climate adaptation strategies 
in its Climate Action Plan (CAP), which has been 
updated concurrently with the General Plan.

Urban Heat and Drought 
In the San Diego Region, heat waves have already 
become more frequent and intense. Temperatures 
are projected to continue to increase substan-
tially, increasing by more than 5°F to as much as 
10°F by the end of the century. Alongside the 
rise in global temperatures, precipitation pat-
terns in California are also changing. Less precip-
itation is falling as snow, and more is falling as 
rain. Reduced winter snowpack will negatively 
impact local water availability, particularly during 
drought periods. 
Drought frequency may increase as rainstorms 
become less likely during the summer months. 
Effects from drought are typically felt most 
acutely in winter months, with drought periods 
also becoming more likely in the future. In 2022, 
the driest winter months in 100 years marked 
the third year of drought for the state. January, 
February, and March of 2022 had the least rain 
and snow on record for any of these months in 
California, contributing to the state’s second 
extreme drought in 10 years. In contrast, the 
winter of 2023 was marked by a series of atmo-
spheric rivers that resulted in extreme flooding, 
mudslides, and overflow of the state’s water stor-
age infrastructure.30 The drastic change in weath-
er patterns, and associated risks and damages, are 
consisted with predictions that climate change 
will bring more “weather whiplash” to California 
in the coming decades.

led to an increased likelihood in compound extreme 
events, such as concurrent heat waves, droughts,

may see intense wildfire while others may not. As 
wildfire emerges from a variety of climate condi-
tions including vegetative cover, precipitation, and 
temperature, wildfire severity will continue to fluc-
tuate over time. However, climate change will favor 
many of the climatic conditions that make wildfire 
more likely, meaning that average wildfire intensity 
will gradually increase. Wildfire is associated with 
a host of secondary impacts such as poor air and 
water quality, landslides, erosion, and impacts to 
energy, and transit systems.

seasonal distribution of precipitation, with rainfall 
becoming more concentrated in the winter months 
and falling in fewer, higher-intensity events. These 
changes may result in a number of secondary 
impacts, such as riverine flooding, reduction in 
winter snowpack, drought, groundwater depletion, 
increased wildfire risk, changes in streamflow, and 
strain to health, energy, and infrastructure sys-
tems. Flooding effects will be felt most strongly in 
coastal and low-lying areas, as well as areas with 
inadequate stormwater infrastructure.

PUBLIC HEALTH IMPACTS 
Climate change also affects public health: global 
temperature increases and exacerbated weath-
er severity can lead to respiratory issues from 
wildfire smoke, an extended allergy season, and 
heat-related illness. Drought also has public 
health implications for food systems. Worsening 
climate change impacts, including prolonged 
drought, unpredictable weather patterns, fires, 
and flooding, are already straining and disrupting 
agricultural resources and food supply chains, 
which result in local food insecurity. Oceanside’s 
agricultural sector will need to continue to adjust 
to the effects of climate change, with new crops, 
new farming methods, and new approaches to 
distribution and marketing. 
Increases in urban temperature may be felt most 
acutely by those living in urban heat islands – 
pockets of the urban environment where tem-
peratures can dramatically exceed those in 
neighboring non-urban areas. The proliferation of 
impervious paved surfaces in built environments 
and use of air conditioning can lead to these 
urban heat islands, especially in places that lack 
tree canopy and water bodies. This can further 
increase summertime cooling costs. Reducing the 
heat island effect in the City’s built environment 
will be key to maintaining the comfort and health 
of community members. Urban heat island effects 
can be moderated by the cooling effects of trees 
and irrigation in urban landscapes. Oceanside’s 
existing tree canopy is shown in Figure 2-3 and 
illustrates areas that do not have sufficient tree 
coverage. Oceanside neighborhoods with the 
least tree canopy coverage include Tri-City, San 
Luis Rey, and Townsite. More information on 
tree canopy and urban forestry is included in the 
Remarkable Community Element.
The increasing emission of GHGs and resulting 
effects on the climate also presents an equity 
issue. Frontline communities, those that have 
been and will continue to be hit first and worst 
by the impacts of environmental injustice and the 
climate crisis, are often the least able to adapt, 
resist, or recover from climate impacts.34 For 
example, increasingly extreme weather events 
will have cost implications, such as energy costs 
needed to heat or cool indoor spaces. These 
additional costs will be felt more acutely by pop-
ulations already impacted by severe housing, 
transportation, and/or healthcare cost burdens. 
Furthermore, neighborhood level, resulting from 
historical disinvestment, can create an even 
greater burden. For example, neighborhoods with 
fewer trees and green spaces or inadequate fund-
ing to maintain these resources are often more 
susceptible to extreme heat events and excessive 
rainfall. Additionally, people who do not own a 
car and rely on other forms of transportation may 
face mobility constraints during extreme weath-
er events, especially where public transportation 
systems and active transportation facilities are 
not designed for these conditions.

CLIMATE ADAPTATION AND 
COMMUNITY RESILIENCE 
Climate change adaptation can be defined as the 
ongoing process of adjusting natural and human 
systems in anticipation of or in response to the 
effects of climate change, with the goal of reduc-
ing the harm of and/or increasing the benefits 
associated with these efforts. A key aspect of 
adapting to the impacts of climate change is urban 
resilience, defined as the capacity of individu-
als, communities, institutions, businesses, and 
systems within a built environment to survive, 
adapt, recover and grow, no matter what types of 
chronic stresses (e.g., high unemployment, poor 
or overtaxed infrastructure, water shortages) and 
acute shocks (e.g., wildfires, earthquakes, floods, 
and disease outbreaks) they experience. 
There are many elements to achieving success-
ful climate adaptation and community resilience. 
Prioritizing is a multi-step process that involves 
identifying those impacts to which Oceanside 
is most vulnerable, where within the City these 
impacts will be felt, how severe these impacts 
will be, and which City services and communi-
ties are most likely to be affected. Investments 
in community adaptation and resilience will 
create opportunities for employment, local food 
systems, traditional and green infrastructure 
upgrades and maintenance, community-building, 
and other benefits.
While the City’s CAP is largely focused on 
reducing greenhouse gas emissions, some of the 
emissions reduction measures in the CAPalso 
contribute to climate adaptation and resilience. 
Measure CS-1 calls for an enhanced Urban 
Forestry Program with the goal of planting 200 
street trees annually each year and adopting a 
Green Streets Policy that requires all new devel-
opment to incorporate shade trees. Measure 
CS-2 focuses on growing urban agriculture and 
community gardens in Oceanside. The City will 
continue to promote urban agriculture, commu-
nity gardening, and backyard composting edu-
cation through the Green Oceanside outreach 
program and consistent with State regulatory 
requirements such as SB 1383. 
In addition, the City has taken steps to improve 
its resilience to frequent droughts due to climate 
change. The City’s Water Conservation Master 
Plan was created to help the City meet its future 
water needs and is the guiding document for 
the City’s water conservation program. The City 
also maintains mandatory water use efficiency 
measures for its customers to ensure adequate 
water supplies even in drought conditions. The 
City’s Pure Water Oceanside program reduces 
the City’s reliance on imported water sources 
through the purification of recycled water to 
create a new, local source of high-quality drink-
ing water.  Pure Water Oceanside is expected to 
eventually provide more than 30 percent of the 
City's total potable water supply. 
To encourage efficient and equitable use of the 
City’s limited resources, the theme of urban resil-
ience is woven into the goals and policies of the 
SRE and other elements of the General Plan.

6.6	Hazardous Materials
A material is considered hazardous if it appears 
on a list of hazardous materials prepared by a fed-
eral, state, or local agency, or if it has characteris-
tics defined as hazardous by such an agency. The 
California Code of Regulation defines a hazardous 
material as a substance that, because of physical 
or chemical properties, quantity, concentration, 
or other characteristics, may either (1) cause an 
increase in mortality or an increase in serious, 
irreversible, or incapacitating, illness or (2) pose a 
substantial present or potential hazard to human 
health or environment when improperly treated, 
stored, transported or disposed of, or otherwise 
managed. Hazardous materials have been and are 
commonly used in commercial, agricultural, and 
industrial applications and, to a limited extent, in 
residential areas.
Hazardous wastes are defined in the same 
manner, which are materials that no longer have 
practical use, such as substances that have been 
discarded, discharged, spilled, contaminated, 
or are being stored prior to proper disposal. 
Hazardous materials and hazardous wastes are 
classified according to four properties: toxic 
(causes human health effects), ignitable (has the 
ability to burn), corrosive (causes severe burns 
or damage to materials), and reactive (capable of 
causing explosions or generating toxic gases).
Exposure to hazardous materials can result in 
lung damage, cancer, cardiovascular disease, low 
birth weight infants, and other negative health 
outcomes that reduce life expectancy. Hazardous 
sites and materials threaten environmental quali-
ty and can result in soil and groundwater contam-
ination. This is especially true of sites that used 
hazardous materials before adoption of current 
environmental regulations. Exposure to haz-
ardous materials can also result in lung damage, 
cancer, cardiovascular disease, low birth weight 
infants, and other negative health outcomes that 
reduce life expectancy. 
HAZARDOUS SITES/CLEANUP SITES 
A review of the online regulatory databases 
(EnviroStor and GeoTracker) reveals that there 
are approximately 250 documented hazardous 
materials sites currently identified within the City. 
As shown in Figure 6-7, a vast majority of these 
sites are concentrated on Interstate 5, Mission 
Ave, and Oceanside Blvd. About 82 percent of 
sites have been “closed” to indicate that they have 
completed remediation and/or have demonstrated 
that existing site uses do not present a significant 
risk to human health or the environment. About 
six percent of all sites are actively being remedi-
ated and two sites are operational facilities that 
involve manufacturing and garbage collection 
services which are currently certified to handle 
hazardous materials. Both Oceanside Blvd and 
Mission Ave are envisioned to accommodate new 
housing and employment growth as outlined in 
the City’s Smart and Sustainable Corridors Plan. 
As such, SRE goals and policies must aim to miti-
gate risks associated with existing hazardous sites 
to the greatest extent feasible before develop-
ment takes place.  
Risks associated with hazardous sites, closed or 
not, may be worsened by seismic activity, flood-
ing, groundwater intrusion, or new development. 
Excavation and grading work that occur during 
construction have the possibility to expose 
the public to contaminated materials either 
through physical contact or hazardous vapors. 
Mismanagement or mishandling of contaminated 
groundwater and soil may spread contamination 
through surface water runoff or airborne dust, 
increasing the public’s exposure to these hazards.

Underground storage tanks (USTs) and leaking

by law as "any one or combination of tanks, 
including pipes connected thereto, that is used 
for the storage of hazardous substances and that 
is substantially or totally beneath the surface of 
the ground." Leaking underground fuel storage

Figure 6-7, there are currently four open LUSTs in

ed to, pesticide and fertilizer facilities, rail yards,

SOCIAL VULNERABILITY 
Many of the most vulnerable census tracts in 
Oceanside are also areas with the highest den-
sity of hazardous materials sites. According to 
CalEnviroScreen, some tracts bear a dispropor-
tionate environmental burden compared to other 
census tracts in the Planning Area. In general, 
tracts with higher pollution burdens are located 
in the Townsite and Loma Alta neighborhoods.36 
As confirmed in Figure 6-7, these neighborhoods 
are also home to the majority of the hazardous 
materials and sites with known contamination in 
Oceanside. Further, census tracts in the Townsite 
and Loma Alta neighborhoods are also ranked in 
the top quartile for poverty rates, or higher than 
75 percent of the census tracts in California.37
Known hazardous materials and contamination 
sites in Oceanside are also concentrated along 
major transit corridors, including I-5 and Highway 
78. Exhaust from cars and trucks is the major 
source of air pollution in much of California. 
Exhaust fumes contain toxic chemicals that can 
damage DNA, cause cancer, make breathing dif-
ficult, and cause low birth weight and premature 
births. Children who live or go to schools near 
busy roads have higher rates of asthma and other 
lung diseases than children in areas further from 
roads. Major roads can also generate noise in 
nearby neighborhoods. Goals and policies devel-
oped by the City will work to mitigate the effects 
of pollution and hazardous materials while prior-
itizing adaptation for socially vulnerable groups 
through compliance with state laws, pollution 
regulations, and local policies and programs.

6.7	Airport Hazards
OCEANSIDE MUNICIPAL AIRPORT
The Oceanside Municipal Airport, also known 
as the Bob Maxwell Memorial Field, is a public 
airport located two miles northeast of the City’s 
downtown. State Route 76, a major east/west 
corridor, is located immediately south of the air-
port. The airport is surrounded by the San Luis 
Rey River and residential development to the 
north, commercial development to the east, res-
idential and commercial/recreation development 
to the west, and industrial uses to the south. The 
43-acre facility is classified as a public-use gen-
eral aviation airport featuring one runway and 
fuel services. The airport also has a small helipad 
located midfield on the southern aircraft park-
ing apron near the administration building. The 
airport is owned by the City of Oceanside and 
operated, maintained, and developed by Airport 
Property Ventures, LLC (APV).39
The 2010 Oceanside Municipal Airport Land Use 
Compatibility Plan (ALUCP) is the fundamen-
tal tool used by the San Diego County Regional 
Airport Authority (SDCRAA) in promoting airport 
land use compatibility. The ALUCP establish-
es policies which the SDCRAA uses for orderly 
growth of the airport and surrounding area, and 
to protect human health, including policies to 
address potential compatibility impacts related 
to noise, safety, airspace protection, and flight 
activity zones. Accordingly, the General Plan poli-
cies below are fully compliant and consistent with 
all aspects of the ALUCP in terms of location and 
land uses.

Oceanside is also in proximity to additional air-

Camp Pendleton 
The northern boundary of the city is adjacent 
to Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton Airfield 
(Camp Pendleton). Camp Pendleton is a major 
west coast base of the United States Marine 
Corps and is one of the largest Marine Corps 
bases in the country. The 125,000-acre site 
operates the Marine Corps Air Station (MCAS) 
Camp Pendleton airfield, also known as Munn 
Field. The primary mission of the airfield is to 
provide facilities and services to support flight 
operations to prepare Marines for combat. 
MCAS Camp Pendleton encompasses 410 acres, 
199 square miles of airspace, more than 100 
structures, a single 6,005-foot runway, taxiways, 
parking aprons, roads and parking areas, and 
several utility systems. Facilities, including the 
airfield, within Camp Pendleton are neither con-
trolled, influenced, nor programmed by the City 
of Oceanside. See the Efficient and Compatible 
Land Use Element (ECLU) for goals and policies 
regarding Camp Pendleton and surrounding land 
uses.
McLellan-Palomar Airport
The McClellan-Palomar Airport is a general 
aviation airport owned and operated by the 
County of San Diego and located in the City of 
Carlsbad, about seven miles south of Oceanside. 
The airport provides general aviation, corporate, 
and commercial services acting as an economic 
engine for North San Diego County and the City 
of Carlsbad. The 2011 McClellan-Palomar Airport 
Land Use Compatibility Plan by the SDCRAA 
regulates growth of the airport and surrounding 
area to safeguard the general welfare of residents 
within the vicinity of the airport and the public in 
general.

6.8	Evacuation and 
Emergency 
Response
In addition to developing and implementing pro-
active measures to reduce Oceanside’s vulner-
ability to natural and human-induced hazards, 
the City must also be prepared to respond when 
disaster strikes. To this end, this section of the 
SRE outlines goals and policies that support the 
City’s Emergency Preparedness and Response 
programs, agencies, and operations. This sec-
tion also provides information on Oceanside’s 
emergency alert systems, an analysis of evacu-
ation routes, and descriptions of current capital 
improvement projects that contribute to emer-
gency preparedness and response. Informed by 
natural disasters that have occurred over the 
City’s 135-year history as an incorporated munic-
ipality, the goals and policies of the SRE seek to 
address the needs of all community members 
during times of emergency through response pro-
tocols and preparedness strategies that account 
for Oceanside’s increasingly diverse population, 
local economy, and built environment.
EXISTING PROGRAMS, AGENCIES, 
AND OPERATIONS 
All jurisdictions within the San Diego Operational 
Area operate under the Standardized Emergency 
Management System (SEMS) and the National 
Incident Management System (NIMS). SEMS and 
NIMS are based on the Incident Command System 
(ICS), which is a management system designed to 
provide a structure for response to any emer-
gency, large or small. The City of Oceanside uses 
NIMS and SEMS. These emergency management 
systems provide not only local on-scene manage-
ment of an incident, but also for the coordination 
of response activities between neighboring juris-
dictions and the County of San Diego.
The Oceanside Fire Department (OFD) is the pri-
mary emergency response service provider for 
the City and provides comprehensive strategies 
and training in fire prevention, fire suppression, 
emergency medical services, emergency pre-
paredness, 911 services, marine safety, lifeguard-
ing services, and community-based fire services. 
OFD operates eight fire stations throughout the 
city (shown in Figure 6-9) and employs approxi-
mately 134 full time public safety professionals. 
The OFD aims to provide emergency service 
within five minutes of notification 90 percent of 
the time—as a result, 12 paramedic units cover 
Oceanside’s 42.5 square miles.
The OFD also administers the Community 
Emergency Response Team (CERT) training pro-
gram. The purpose of CERT is to create a team 
of trained volunteer emergency responders who 
can assist the City during an emergency. During 
emergencies the City can activate CERT volun-
teers for additional operational support.
In 2016, the OFD edited and modified the City 
of Oceanside Emergency Operations Plan (EOP), 
a document providing a roadmap for emergency 
response personnel in performance of their duties 
before, during, and after initial emergency recov-
ery. The EOP focuses on continuity of government, 
emergency services, mobilization of resources, 
mutual aid, and public information. The EOP pro-
vides a comprehensive overview of the roles and 
responsibilities of City departments in the event 
of an emergency, as well as plans for coordination 
between city, county, state, and federal agencies.

In October 2023, San Diego County published its

also provides an assessment of risk from a variety

Terrorism 
The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) defines 
terrorism as “the unlawful use of force or vio-
lence committed by a group or individual against 
persons or property to intimidate or coerce a 
government, the civilian population, or any seg-
ment thereof, in furtherance of political or social 
objectives.” It could also include use of weapons 
of mass destruction, cyber terrorism (communica-
tions and information systems), or agroterrorism 
(disruption to food supplies). With military bases, 
biomedical firms, and high-tech research facilities 
spread throughout San Diego County, the San 
Diego County Operational Area could become a 
target for future terrorist attacks. The City’s 2016 
EOP outlines the tactical operation of emergency 
response based on the type of incident, includ-
ing facilitating an effective local response to a 
terrorist attack. Further, the City of Oceanside’s 
section of the County’s MJHMP includes action 
items to harden information technology process-
es to prevent cyber-attacks. 
Cyber-attacks are becoming an increasingly prev-
alent risk for cities and are a difficult threat to 
prepare for due to the ability of malicious actors 
to operate from anywhere in the world, the link-
ages between cyberspace and physical systems, 
and the difficulty of reducing vulnerabilities and 
consequences in complex cyber networks. For 
government entities, developing and implement-
ing a comprehensive cybersecurity plan is an 
important step towards protecting and maintain-
ing city operations.

Public Health Crises 
Climate scientists and public health experts warn 
that climate change will increase the risk of infec-
tious disease transmission and potential pandem-
ics.41 The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the 
need for coordination across a wide range of sec-
tors, including city departments, public organiza-
tions, and the community, in effectively plan for 
and respond to future public health emergencies.
In the event of a public health emergency, the 
coordination and acquisition of medical/health 
mutual aid resources involves federal, state, and 
local agencies as well as the private sector (hos-
pitals, medical supply vendors, ambulance com-
panies, etc.). The Oceanside Fire Department will 
work with the San Diego County Department 
Health & Human Services Agency to coordinate 
medical and health responses.  
At the state level, the California Governor’s 
Office of Emergency Services (Cal OES) responds 
to and aids in the recovery from emergencies 
within the State of California under the authority 
of the California Services Act, California Disaster 
Assistance Act, and the Stafford Act. Cal OES is 
responsible for managing disaster recovery and 
aiding local governments, special districts, cer-
tain nonprofit organizations, individuals, busi-
nesses, and agricultural communities impacted 
by disasters.
The California Department of Public Health con-
tains the Emergency Preparedness Office, which 
maintains and manages the Medical and Health 
Coordination Center, distributes funds to local 
health departments for disaster planning, and 
operates the California Health Alert Network. 
The Department also manages the Climate 
Change & Health Equity Program, whose goal 
is to systematically integrate work from climate 
change planning and public health planning with 
policies and principles that promote equity.

Emergency Operations Center (EOC)
The City of Oceanside General Plan Update is 
an opportunity to enhance community resilience 
for all Oceanside communities with policies in 
support of local hazard mitigation innovations. 
For example, the City of Oceanside’s Hazard 
Mitigation section of San Diego County’s Multi-
Jurisdictional Hazard Mitigation Plan (MJHMP) 
includes several goals, objectives, and actions 
related to climate adaptation. The City will imple-
ment Action 3.6.4 which will move Fire Station 
1 from its current unreinforced masonry building 
into a modern hazard-resistant self-contained 
structure and include a separate standalone 
Emergency Operations Center (EOC). The City 
will also construct Fire Station 8 in the Rancho 
Del Oro commercial area. Action 4.A.1 specifies 
that the City will plan, practice, exercise, and 
operate the EOC following the National Incident 
Management System (NIMS) and the Standardized 
Emergency Management System (SEMS).
Other actions to strengthen community resil-
ience and safety outlined in the City’s section of 
San Diego County’s MJHMP include retrofitting 
City-owned infrastructure and facilities to be 
hazard resistant; accessible emergency alerts and 
information systems; and aggressive vegetation 
management programs. The General Plan Update 
will incorporate these MJHMP recommenda-
tions into its goals and policies to ensure their 
implementation.

Essential Public Facilities 
Resilience hubs are defined as “communi-
ty-serving facilities that support residents 
year-round and support resource distribution 
and onsite services before, during, or after a 
natural hazard event.” General Plan policies 
will further support resilience hubs to protect 
frontline communities during climate crises, 
earthquakes or other shocks, and chronic 
stressors. Resilience hubs address the unique 
cultural and situational needs of the commu-
nities in which they are located. They also pro-
vide critical disaster preparedness, response, 
and recovery functionalities. Minimum char-
acteristics include all-electric buildings with 
microgrids (onsite renewable and backup elec-
tricity with islanding capability), refrigeration, 
gathering and shelter capacity, emergency 
communications, and cooling and air filtration.
Public facilities operated by the City such as 
libraries, senior centers, cultural centers, 
parks, and recreational centers may function as 
essential service facilities and play an import-
ant role in emergency response as resilience 
hubs, respite centers, and local assistance 
centers. These essential public facilities also 
play an important role as neighborhood hubs 
where Oceanside residents can go to build 
and strengthen community networks. In the 
event of a large-scale emergency or disaster, 
the City of Oceanside may designate tempo-
rary shelter locations, most-likely at pre-des-
ignated schools, until permanent shelters can 
be established by San Diego County Office of 
Emergency Services. Information would be 
provided to the public on the locations of the 
temporary shelters if the need arises.  
Currently, the San Diego County Health & Human 
Services Agency publishes information on its 
Cool Zone Program. The program which operates 
from June 1 to October 31 is an established net-
work of free, air-conditioned settings across San 
Diego County that allow respite for older adults, 
persons with disabilities, or anyone looking to 
escape the extreme heat during the summer. Cool 
Zones are a way for residents to lower individu-
al utility usage and help conserve energy for the 
whole community. The San Diego County Board 
of Supervisors launched the Cool Zone program 
out of concern for seniors, persons with disabili-
ties, and those with health concerns that could be 
complicated by the effects of heat. Table 6-2 lists 
public air-conditioned facilities in Oceanside. Such 
facilities include two senior centers designated as 
Cool Zones by San Diego County and branches of 
the City of Oceanside public library. A data driven 
approach is recommended during the placement 
of future respite facilities to equitably distribute 
supportive resources to Oceanside communities.

Emergency Power
Due to increased wildfire risk, San Diego Gas and 
Electric (SDGE), Oceanside’s public utility elec-
tricity transmission and distribution as well as 
gas provider, has instituted Public Safety Power 
Shutoffs (PSPS) when forecasts indicate the 
potential for extreme weather and wildfire risk. 
Although SDGE endeavors to provide multiple 
days’ notice and warning to those who may be 
affected, these events are still disruptive to many 
residents, especially in rural areas of the unincor-
porated county. Power outages may be especially 
hazardous to residents who use prescribed med-
ications and treatments that rely on electricity 
and refrigeration and may exacerbate food inse-
curity due to lack of refrigeration.42  
To help mitigate the effects of power outages, 
Action 3.B.1 of the City’s section of San Diego 
County’s MJHMP is to install a backup power 
generator for the existing EOC. The General Plan 
Update will continue to incorporate recommen-
dations that build and support local capacity to 
improve overall resilience in the event of a power 
outage into its goals and policies.
ALERTS AND EVACUATION
Evacuation procedures are most effective when 
residents are aware of the emergencies that are 
most likely to affect them and have ample time 
and support to prepare their own emergency 
plans. The City of Oceanside, in conjunction with 
San Diego County, has a variety of systems and 
procedures in place to protect residents and vis-
itors to plan for, avoid, and respond to a hazard 
event. Various emergency warning systems uti-
lized by the City of Oceanside include mobile 
emergency vehicle sirens and loudspeakers, PA 
systems, local TV and radio stations, social media, 
emergency preparedness apps for smartphones, 
a door-to-door notification by Community 
Emergency Response Teams (CERT), law enforce-
ment and fire department volunteers, and reserve 
police officers. Television and radio are also used 
to keep the residents of Oceanside informed.

EQUITABLE RESPONSE, 
NOTIFICATIONS, AND PREPARATION 
Local hazard mitigation and emergency response 
policies should be tailored to the needs of the 
communities they serve. Some populations are 
at greater risk from hazard events because of 
decreased resources or because they have a 
disability. Linguistically isolated households (res-
idents who do not speak or read English as a first 
language or at all) may not hear or understand 
important information when there is an emergen-
cy like a fire, earthquake, or extreme heat waves. 
Persons with disabilities may require communica-
tions in alternative, accessible formats to ensure 
effective communication- particularly individ-
uals who are deaf or hard-of-hearing, blind or 
low-vision, or those with intellectual disabilities. 
Additionally, lack of familiarity with local govern-
ment and planning processes may present barri-
ers to community engagement and accessing key 
government-provided resources.

EMERGENCY EVACUATION 
Evacuation occurs only when a serious threat 
to public safety exists. The Oceanside Fire and 
Police departments are responsible for the autho-
rization, direction, routing, and relocation of 
people from their homes, schools, and places of 
business. During emergency evacuations, these 
departments will coordinate transportation sup-
port for evacuated populations as well as pop-
ulations with disabilities and others with access 
or functional needs. The City of Oceanside will 
attempt to ensure that evacuation vehicles have 
the capacity to move individuals with function-
al needs. Potential transportation-dependent 
persons include persons with functional needs, 
elderly people, individuals living in group homes, 
and those without access to a vehicle. The City 
will attempt to transport the affected persons 
with access and functional needs with their care-
taker, guide, signal, and service dogs, and neces-
sary equipment and medication.45 According to 
the City’s EOP, law enforcement is responsible 
for ensuring that transportation resources are 
being prepared for deployment, if necessary, to 
the affected areas.

include Interstate 5, State Route 78 and State 
Route 76. In a disaster, they would be assessed

VULNERABLE POPULATIONS 
Vulnerable populations are often less likely to 
evacuate prior to a disaster due to lack of access 
to personal transportation, health conditions that 
make mobility difficult, jobs that require them to 
remain, or misperceptions about their level of 
risk.46 For example, individuals who cannot afford 
access to private transportation and services such 
as hotels may be disadvantaged during emergen-
cy evacuation events. Approximately four per-
cent of Oceanside’s households lack access to a 
household vehicle and may be at greater risk of 
harm because they lack the capacity to evacu-
ate.47 In addition, households that lack internet 
access may have a limited ability to receive emer-
gency alerts and disaster preparedness strate-
gies. Oceanside’s downtown area and the Lake 
neighborhood tend to have households with less 
internet access in the city. Homeowners who are 
underinsured may also be at risk-- increasingly, 
homes in high-fire-risk areas and high-flood-risk 
areas have fewer options for companies that 
will insure them, and higher premiums, making it 
harder to rebuild in cases of disaster. Goals and 
policies developed by the City will work to imple-
ment programs that protect all vulnerable popu-
lations during an emergency event.

Public safety, perception of safety, and response 
to crime are complex issues. For example, public 
safety and crime reduction can be a health 
issue, with ample evidence showing that access 
to healthcare, public services, and community 
violence intervention programs can help reduce 
crime.48 Youth who may be at risk of violence 
are often those who are most disconnected from 
school, employment, or social connection. Many 
institutions that are supposed to keep people safe 
are underlain by systemic racism and have result-
ed in inequitable or harmful outcomes, especially 
for Black, Indigenous, Latinx, and Asian com-
munities. Therefore, in order to improve public 
safety, the City of Oceanside needs to address 
the root causes of social and economic inequali-
ties, as well as the underlying social determinants 
of health.
The General Plan focuses on broad overarching 
issues related to the intersection of safety ser-
vices and topics such as land use and transpor-
tation; staffing and programming decisions are 
beyond the scope of the General Plan. This sec-
tion covers police safety services offered by the 
City. Other services, including fire and EMS, are 
discussed in Section 6.2 Fire Hazards.

COMMUNITY POLICING STRATEGIES
It has been recognized that traditional law 
enforcement responses to crime and disorder, 
by themselves, are ineffective. Community ori-
ented policing is an emerging law enforcement 
philosophy that was conceived as the result of 
a realization that many causal factors that con-
tribute to criminal behavior include social and 
environmental issues that may not fit into tra-
ditionally accepted law enforcement methods. 
These broader problems are best identified and 
addressed through this community and police 
partnership.
The U.S. Department of Justice’s Office of 
Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS 
Office) defines community policing as “a philos-
ophy that promotes organizational strategies 
that support the systematic use of partnerships 
and problem-solving techniques to proactively 
address the immediate conditions that give rise 
to public safety issues such as crime, social dis-
order, and fear of crime”.50 In order to achieve 
a true community oriented policing partnership 
with citizens, police must integrate with the com-
munity to positively impact social problems and 
improve the quality of life.
Rather than simply responding to crimes once 
they have been committed, community policing 
concentrates on preventing crime and eliminat-
ing the atmosphere of fear it creates. Examples 
of initiatives that align with community policing 
philosophies include de-escalation training, crisis 
intervention teams, and transparency programs 
to readily share relevant information on police 
operations. The City of Oceanside will continue 
to invest in crime prevention initiatives, including 
community policing programs, to improve com-
munity safety.

RESPONSIBLE AGENCIES 
There are several other agencies responsible for 
public health and public safety in the San Diego 
region. The SRE emphasizes interagency coor-
dination and planning efforts between the City 
of Oceanside and the following agencies to best 
prioritize public health and safety in the face of 
environmental inequities, crime, and other com-
munity stressors. 
The U.S. Department of Justice’s Office of 
Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS 
Office) responsible for advancing the practice of 
community policing by the nation's state, local, 
territorial, and tribal law enforcement agencies 
through information and grant resources. The 
COPS Office awards grants to hire community 
policing professionals, develop and test innova-
tive policing strategies, and provide training and 
technical assistance to community members, 
local government leaders, and all levels of law 
enforcement. 
The California Department of Public Health con-
tains the Emergency Preparedness Office, which 
maintains and manages the Medical and Health 
Coordination Center, distributes funds to local 
health departments for disaster planning, and 
operates the California Health Alert Network. 
The Department also manages the Climate 
Change & Health Equity Program, whose goal 
is to systematically integrate work from climate 
change planning and public health planning with 
policies and principles that promote equity. The 
Department works with local, State, and national 
partners to assure that climate change mitigation 
and adaptation activities have beneficial effects 
on health while not exacerbating preexisting 
health disparities.
The San Diego County Sheriff’s Office is the chief 
law enforcement agency in San Diego County. 
The department is comprised of more than 4,000 
employees, both sworn deputies and profession-
al staff. The department provides general law 
enforcement, detention, court security services, 
and performs civil processes, as well as region-
al investigative support and tactical emergency 
response for San Diego County.
